Imagine a friend asks you: "Hey, what's your phone number?".

Now imagine they ask again the next day. And the next. And the day after that. Fifty different times, fifty different friends.

At some point you stop digging through your contacts to find it. You just know it. It lives in your head, ready to go.

Congratulations, you just invented caching.

Caching is one of the simplest ideas in all of computing, and one of the most powerful: if you've already worked out an answer, remember it instead of working it out again.

That's the whole idea. This post is about how it works, where it lives, and the one thing about it that makes even experienced developers grumpy.

There's an old joke in programming: "There are only two hard things in computer science: naming things and cache invalidation.".

Here's why. A cache remembers an answer. But the real world keeps changing. Imagine you cache a product's price at $20. Then the shop updates it to $25. Your cache doesn't know. For a while, some customers still see $20.

That's stale data: a remembered answer that stopped being true. And deciding when to forget is genuinely tricky, because you're balancing two things that pull in opposite directions:.

When someone updates a product, your code also removes the cached version. Next request, the cache misses, and the fresh data gets loaded. This keeps things much more accurate, but you have to remember to do it everywhere data changes. Forget one spot, and you've got a sneaky bug.

A good rule of thumb: match the freshness to the stakes. A "like" count being a few seconds behind? Nobody cares. An account balance or a stock level? Be much more careful, or don't cache it at all.

Caching things that are personal. If you cache a page that includes one user's data and then serve it to everyone, Alice sees Bob's account. It's a real, embarrassing, sometimes serious bug. Be careful about what goes into a shared cache.

Cache avalanche ("everything expired at once"). Say you load a thousand items into the cache at the same moment, all with a 10-minute TTL. Ten minutes later, they all vanish together, and your database suddenly gets hit by a thousand requests at once. Picture a whole crowd leaning on a door that was holding them back. The classic fix: add a little random wiggle to your expiry times (say 10 minutes, plus or minus a bit) so they don't all disappear in the same second.

Cache stampede, also called "thundering herd" ("one popular thing just vanished"). This is the avalanche's close cousin, but about a single item. Imagine your homepage data is cached and thousands of people are asking for it every second. The moment it expires, every one of those requests sees a miss at the same time, and every one of them rushes to the database to rebuild the same answer. Thousands of identical, expensive queries, all at once, for something only one of them needed to do. This is how a fast site suddenly falls over.

Cache penetration ("asking for things that don't exist"). Someone (or some bot) keeps requesting items that aren't real, like user ID 99999999. The cache never has them, so every request misses and goes to the database, which finds nothing. Since there's nothing to cache, it happens again and again. A simple fix: cache the "not found" answer too, for a short time. Fancier fixes exist, but that one handles most cases.

In a recent development, never expiring anything. "I'll just cache it forever" is how you end up debugging at midnight, wondering why an old value won't go away.

Caching without measuring. If you don't know your hit rate (what percentage of requests are answered from cache), you don't know if your cache is helping. A cache with a 5% hit rate is mostly just extra complexity.

Industry observers note that 1. What am I caching, and is it worth it? Pick the things that are read often, change rarely, and are expensive to produce. Look for the slow spots first, then cache those. If you can't point to something slow, you don't need a cache yet.

Recent findings suggest that 2. What should the key look like? The key is the label on the sticky note. It has to be unique and predictable, or you'll pull out the wrong note. A good habit is a clear pattern like user:42:profile or product:991:v2.

Recent findings suggest that the most important part: include everything that changes the answer in the key. If the page looks different per language, put the language in the key (home:en, home:sw). If you forget, a Swahili reader gets the English page, and you spend an evening confused. Adding a version (v2) also lets you "invalidate" everything at once by simply changing the version number.

It has been revealed that 3. How long should it live? Pick a TTL based on how bad it would be to show old data. Seconds for fast-moving things, minutes or hours for slow-moving ones. When in doubt, start short and lengthen it once you trust it. And remember the wiggle: add a little randomness so things don't all expire together.

In a recent development, 5. How major should it be? Here's the happy surprise: a cache usually doesn't need to hold everything. In most apps, a small slice of the data gets most of the requests (people tend to ask for the same popular things). So a modest cache can give you a high hit rate. Start small, watch the hit rate, and grow only if it helps.

Sources indicate that 6. What if the cache disappears? Design for it. Your app should still work if the cache is empty or down, just slower, by falling back to the database. And since an empty cache means a flood of misses, make sure your database (and the stampede protections above) can survive the "cold start" moment.

7. How will I know it's working? Track your hit rate, how much memory you're using, how many items are being evicted, and how fast reads are. If the hit rate is low, the cache isn't earning its keep. If evictions are constant, it's probably too small. Numbers beat guessing every time.
