That’s kind of a new norm now because the industry can’t keep up. Technology is messy. As you describe it and go through these examples, the air traffic one seems like a particularly fragile one. Is the system fragile? I think technology is just fragile, whether you’re in an Apple ecosystem, Android, or you use Microsoft or Google, whatever you’re using. Every day people use this, and they sort of just expect it to work, and they should. But behind the scenes, when you actually look at how complicated these systems are, how they operate, and the interaction with hardware and software, disks get full, memory gets overloaded. Are any of these episodes an “aha” moment for you, of us sort of crossing a threshold? When you look at the latest Patch Tuesday from Microsoft, it was 1,000-plus vulnerabilities that were fixed. You develop something, it kind of works, it does great things, but when you put it in the real world, it may not actually work as intended in the lab, which is why the security industry has now moved into the safety industry and has become a great opportunity for folks like us. Anyone can slow down. If the model is not right, if it’s not aligned, if it’s not getting the output you want, if the quality control is not there, you know what you do? You don’t release it. You go back and do another run until you get it right. So what should any business leader do right now to improve their cyber footing? Well, I think maybe first is go find your security and IT people and give them a hug, because they are at 110% of capacity now. I’ve never seen this before, but it’s happening across industry. Company CEOs are making a decision: Take something offline or be hacked. They’re saying, “Hey, we would rather take a massive service offline than be hacked.” They’re willing to make that decision if it’s so bad. When PHP came out, I had plenty of companies say, “Hey, we may just take everything down because it’s so bad.”.
