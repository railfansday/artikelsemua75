Recent findings suggest that with Thanksgiving approaching, many of us are busy planning our menus. Turkey or ham? Mashed or roast potatoes? Pumpkin or pecan pie?

While we may spend countless hours and enormous effort preparing the perfect meal, how much time do we invest in cultivating the emotion at the heart of the holiday?

Too often, gratitude is served up as a side dish—a brief expression of thanks before we dive into our feast. But what if we made gratitude the main course? (Related: Course Launch Community Event...)

Sources indicate that gratitude offers enormous benefits for our physical and mental health. Given its importance, we dedicate an entire chapter to it in our book, Happy Together: Using the Science of Positive Psychology to Build Love That Lasts, and have written about it here, here, and here. (Related: How to train a latest language model from scratch using...)

According to positive psychology research, it is one of the five most crucial character strengths for individual well-being. Leading emotion scientist Barbara Fredrickson similarly argues that it’s vital to our relationships, noting that couples who express gratitude well have a greater chance of staying together.

Recent research suggests that gratitude’s positive reach extends far beyond the two people directly involved.

According to recent reports, in a paper published in Affective Science, researchers conducted two studies involving 680 participants. In the first study, participants watched a video of someone helping another person solve a problem. Afterward, the person who received help responded with either gratitude or simple positivity. Viewers then shared their personal impressions of that person. In the second study, participants had to decide whether to cooperate with the grateful person to earn money, even though doing so left them vulnerable to exploitation.

Industry observers note that the researchers found that simply witnessing expressions of gratitude—rather than general positivity—fostered more favorable impressions of that person. In fact, it created a “halo effect” wherein participants felt greater trust, closeness, and willingness to interact with the grateful person, even when cooperating on a risky financial task. When people witness gratitude in action, it signals warmth and trustworthiness, creating opportunities to bring groups and communities together.

To build that same sense of trust, closeness, and community around your Thanksgiving table, try shifting how you share thanks this year.

This Thanksgiving, rather than just filling our plates, let’s also remember to focus on nourishing our relationships. Gratitude, it turns out, is a dish best shared—and its benefits may extend far beyond the people at the table.
