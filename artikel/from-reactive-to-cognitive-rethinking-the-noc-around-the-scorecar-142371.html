Traditionally, network operations centers (NOCs) were reactive, supporting customers only after problems arose. Over time, they became proactive, with “Mean Time to Respond” taking center stage, and now they are becoming predictive, or cognitive. First came bot-driven automation, where human intent was translated through engines and grounded in relevant CLIs, observability or configurability. Next came agentic workers, built for network queries, incidents, trouble tickets, inventory and more. (Related: Tech Report: How we sped up transformer inference 100x ...)

Industry observers note that despite decades of investment in automation, monitoring, observability and IT service management (ITSM), many enterprises still operate with fragmented workflows, multiple escalation layers, reactive decision-making and heavy dependence on human intervention. So the question is how we improve Net Promoter Score (NPS) across the board, whether for internal developers, end customers or assurance teams. In short, how do we improve the holistic experience? With Model Context Protocol (MCP) integrations, agents became smarter through open LLMs and third-party resources. We introduced retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which sat at the center of these grounding mechanisms and worked well. But we became so absorbed in these advances that the conversation turned into one about tools.

Sources indicate that to confuse matters further, application performance monitoring (APM) and observability frameworks crowded the discussion. We went from human-driven desks to micro robot-driven workflows, which is no small feat. But before we congratulate ourselves, the real questions remain. How does deploying this infrastructure improve an organization’s balanced scorecard? What do we do with the engineers who supported these systems for so long? Do we need to re-skill them? How do we manage governance, accountability and risk? How do we make the infrastructure more intelligent? How do we leverage existing APM tools and infrastructure as code (IaC)? Who is the executive sponsor? What’s our fail-fast strategy? Eventually, all of these questions boil down to the balanced scorecard.

Is this the way to create better NPS, faster turnaround time (TAT), lower MTTR and less management overhead? The tools are the ingredients and recipes, but the metrics we are solving for the business are the food on the table.

Industry observers note that for a while, core semantics mattered for episodic and procedural memory, but then the need arose for semantics bundled with adjacencies. The intent was to ground queries as well as possible, in the shortest time, with near-zero hallucinations and no extra tools. Can we reduce mean time to respond by 30%? Can we improve first call resolution by 50%? Can we measure retention rate? If we do this right every day, there could come a time when there are no tickets, and we will have redefined CX.

According to recent reports, this is where knowledge graphs built on ontology frameworks such as RDF/OWL could be one option. As we define this better, we may see the emergence of VKN (vector and knowledge graphs, similar to Graph RAG). The ontology could cover networks or anything else, but it would go beyond vector databases, helping queries get processed and grounded without many iterations. Yet while we have vectors and now knowledge graphs, how do we deal with people? Remember, our key questions at the beginning were about the balanced scorecard, and NPS and people are strong indicators on it.

According to recent reports, I believe we will still need engineers for Day 0 (assessment) and Day 1 (program management and governance). This could also mean shifting the traditional desk model from Levels 1/2/3 to just two sets of engineers: a “front end” and an “escalation end.” The front end performs Day 0 and Day 1. The escalation end provides tool support and integrations, creates and maintains vectors and graphs, uses agent orchestration tools, and keeps the configurability and observability infrastructure clean and lean. Everyone elevates their tasks and skills.

In a recent development, the way I envisage it, operational designs will have humans at the front, architecting and governing the scorecard metrics; AI agents, MCP, RAG, orchestrators, ontologies and databases in the middle, enabling those metrics; and human experts at the back end, where cognition is the only thing that matters. Other options may exist or emerge, but this approach will likely serve enterprise utilization and customer experience better, and therefore retention.
