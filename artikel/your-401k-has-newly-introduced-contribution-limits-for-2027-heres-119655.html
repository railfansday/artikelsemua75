According to recent reports, at this point, 401(k)s have become synonymous with retirement accounts because so many Americans have one. It's by far the most used retirement account in the U.S., and it's why millions can have a financially comfortable retirement.

Industry observers note that A 401(k) is a true win-win. It helps you save and invest for retirement proactively, and you get a tax break for doing so. There are many moving parts to a 401(k), but one key rule to keep track of is the contribution limit. Let's dive into what to expect next year. Beginning next year, you can contribute $25,500 to a 401(k), up from this year's $24,500 limit. If you're 50 or older, you can add an $8,500 catch-up contribution (up from $8,000), bringing your limit to $34,000.

According to recent reports, workers aged 60 to 63 can make a special catch-up contribution of up to $11,750, meaning anyone in that four-year age range can contribute $37,250 to their 401(k)s in 2027. Once you turn 64 or older, you go back to the standard catch-up amount. (Related: Hugging Face Reads, Feb. 2021 - Long-range Transformers...)

The contribution limit applies only to your personal contributions. If your job matches a certain amount of your contributions, that match doesn't count against your limit. For example, if you're younger than 50 and your job matches up to $15,000 annually, you can also contribute $15,000, even though $30,000 is above the $25,500 limit for 2027.

Industry observers note that not many people can afford to max out their 401(k)s year in and year out, but you should try to contribute at least the amount your employer will match. It's essentially "free" money because it's a guaranteed instant 100% return on your investment. Don't leave it on the table.

IRA contribution limits often increase, but they're staying put next year. The contribution limit for IRAs -- both traditional and Roth combined -- is $7,500. People 50 or older can add a $1,100 catch-up contribution, bringing the limit to $8,600.
