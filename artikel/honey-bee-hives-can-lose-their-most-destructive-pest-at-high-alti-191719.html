Honey bee hives can lose their most destructive pest at high altitudes

Low oxygen levels at high altitudes prevent varroa mites from reproducing, offering a potential way to protect honeybees.

Varroa mites kill honey bee colonies around the world. Beekeepers fight them with chemical treatments year after year.

On the Tibetan Plateau, however, hives have gone untreated since 2006. Beekeepers there report that the mites simply disappear.

At two sites above about 11,500 feet (3,500 meters), low oxygen pressure kept the mites’ young from maturing.

A lack of oxygen had never before been shown to stop the mite from breeding. The finding could lead to fresh ways of controlling the pest without chemicals. (Related: How to train a fresh language model from scratch using ...)

It has been revealed that the work was led by Huoqing Zheng of Zhejiang University. Researchers at the Xizang Academy of Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Sciences in Lhasa took part. So did scientists at the University of Salford in England.

Infested hives were trucked to different heights in China. The high-altitude effect was then copied in hives close to sea level. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Sources indicate that that second test was small, with five hives over 11 days. The team also hasn’t yet worked out how low oxygen stops the mites.

“For the first time, beekeepers in Tibet noticed the parasitic varroa mite mysteriously disappear from their colonies,” the authors wrote in a statement.

“We found that the low oxygen pressure on the Tibetan Plateau prevents the mites from reproducing.”.

In a recent development, honey bees are the most vital managed pollinator of farm crops. For 60 years, Varroa destructor has been a leading cause of their deaths.

Industry observers note that A female mite enters a cell holding a bee larva about a day before the cell is sealed. Worker bees close it with a wax cap. Shut inside, the mite feeds on the larva. Her first egg is laid about 60 hours later.

According to recent reports, the first egg always becomes a male. The next three or four become females. They mate with their brother inside the cell.

In a recent development, time in a worker bee’s cell is limited. Only the male and up to three daughters grow up before the bee emerges. Only the adult daughters can survive outside, where they live and feed on adult bees.

In a recent development, mite populations can grow rapidly once a colony becomes infested. Viruses are also transmitted by the mites as they feed. In cooler regions this combination can kill a colony within three years.

In China, hives are treated with chemicals up to five times a year. Despite these treatments, high colony losses continue to be reported. The mite has been identified as the main cause of these losses.

Beekeeping on the plateau began only recently. In 2004 a total of 30 colonies were brought up to the region. Twenty years later the number of colonies had grown to more than 12,000.

In May 2024 a total of 60 colonies were taken from a single apiary in southern China. The colonies were divided into four groups of 15 for the experiment.

In a recent development, one group was moved to another site in Xishuangbanna at roughly 1,800 feet (554 meters). The remaining three groups were transported by truck to sites located at higher elevations.

Kunming lies at roughly 6,450 feet (1,966 meters) and Nyingchi lies at roughly 9,600 feet (2,928 meters).

The highest site was Lhasa at roughly 11,900 feet (3,637 meters), more than twice the elevation of Denver.

Sources indicate that A decline in mite infestation was observed only in the Lhasa group. Before the move, mites were present in 9.3% of developing bees in the colonies headed for Lhasa.

Industry observers note that this proportion had decreased to 1.6% after four months and no mites were detected after one year. No treatment had been applied to these hives. Instead the offspring of the mites failed to reach adulthood.

An increase in infestation was recorded at the lower sites over the same period.

In Kunming, the proportion of infested bees increased from 2.8% to 21.8% within two months. Those colonies were also attacked by hornets.

According to recent reports, the elevation of Nyingchi proved insufficient to provide protection. Infestation there increased from 3.4% to 10.6% over four months and all colonies had died within a year.

By the four-month mark, 25 of the 30 colonies at the two lowest sites had died. Mites were considered the most likely cause of these losses.

In contrast, 14 of the 15 Lhasa colonies remained alive and free of mites after one year. Thirteen of these colonies survived for two years without any treatment.

To see what was going wrong, the team opened sealed cells with forceps. Each cell was checked under a flashlight, and young mites were counted in at least 218 infested cells per group.

In a recent development, mother mites in Lhasa never laid more than two eggs. A second high site, tested in 2023, highlighted the same thing. Those eggs seldom developed into the next stage.

Across 178 cells with older pupae at both sites, no daughter mite was on track to become a mated adult.

Industry observers note that it all happened fast. Just three days after the trucks arrived in Lhasa, mothers were laying far fewer eggs. By day six, each was laying one or two eggs that never developed.

In a recent development, the team also looked for other explanations. Bees keep the brood nest at a steady warmth whatever the weather.

Nests in both Lhasa and Xishuangbanna stayed at about 93°F to 94°F (34°C), and humidity was similar too.

In a recent development, worker bees took about 21 days from egg to adult in both places, so altitude had not slowed them. The lowland colonies had been trucked as well.

Yet in Lhasa, mite breeding stayed low for months after the hives stopped moving.

Industry observers note that that left oxygen as a possible explanation. At Lhasa’s high altitude, oxygen pressure is only about two-thirds of that at sea level.

A second experiment was designed to test the role of oxygen directly. Low-oxygen hives were set up in Shaoxing in eastern China at about 290 feet (88 meters) above sea level.

Sources indicate that A nitrogen-rich gas mixture was supplied from a cylinder to each of five small hives. The hives were wrapped in cling film to reduce leakage of the gas.

According to recent reports, the flow was adjusted by hand whenever the oxygen readings moved outside the target range. The entrances were left open so that the bees could continue to forage.

It has been revealed that each hive was first kept on ordinary air and then switched to air with roughly Lhasa’s oxygen level. Under ordinary air the mother mites produced an average of 3.9 offspring.

After 11 days under low oxygen this figure fell to 1.7. Almost none of these offspring developed beyond the egg stage.

Eleven days after their cells were sealed, the worker bees examined in both conditions had reached the newly emerged adult stage.

This fits with honey bees being known to forage as high as about 13,450 feet (4,100 meters). A similar decline in mite reproduction had been seen after six days in a 2023 trial with six hives.

Recent findings suggest that adult mites appear to tolerate low oxygen better than their offspring. In Lhasa the mother mites remained in the hives for several months although they could not reproduce.

In a recent development, an adult mite breathes through two networks of air tubes. Each network opens to the outside through a single pore. Adult females have also been reported to move air through these tubes by changing their body volume.

Newly hatched mites have no air tubes at all. Instead they absorb oxygen through the skin until the first molt, when the tubes are formed.

Recent findings suggest that most young mites in Lhasa failed to develop beyond this first stage. The authors suggest that this feature may contribute to the vulnerability of the young. This explanation has not yet been tested.

A similar pattern has been reported in fruit flies and in a common spider mite. In both species egg production is more sensitive to low oxygen than adult survival.

According to recent reports, other mechanisms may also be involved, such as the mother’s energy reserves or how cells use and sense oxygen. These possibilities are listed by the authors as questions for future work.

Sources indicate that moving hives to such high elevations is not practical for most beekeepers. The team therefore tested a simple and low-cost alternative.

Hive entrances were narrowed at night so that the breathing of the bees lowered the oxygen inside. In a pilot test this method partly reduced mite reproduction. “This physiological limit of varroa mites opens newly introduced avenues of controlling this globally destructive pest,” the authors wrote.

All of the results so far come from hives in China. Only one kind of bee was studied, the Italian honey bee, the type most commonly kept there.

The effect has not yet been tested in other regions or with other kinds of bees.

It is also unclear whether lowland hives can stay low in oxygen long enough to stop mites safely. The bees themselves would need to remain unharmed.

Future research could test whether narrowing hive entrances keeps mite numbers down over a full season.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Biology.
