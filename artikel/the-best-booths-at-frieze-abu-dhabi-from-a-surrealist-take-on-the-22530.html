The Best Booths at Frieze Abu Dhabi, From a Surrealist Take on the Iran-Iraq War to Mosaics

From sales to the quality of works selling, Frieze Abu Dhabi is making a strong first impression. A tight exhibitor list of 89 galleries has brought a selection of works that confront subjects ranging from war—something that the Middle East is no stranger to—to ideas of masculinity, memory, and religion. Much of the art on view goes beyond conventional painting, extending into life-size sculptures, mosaics and materials such as wood, metal and ink.

Below, we’ve picked the eight best presentations at Frieze Abu Dhabi, which runs through Sunday, November 22.

Lebanese multidisciplinary artist Dala Nasser, 36, was the recipient of the inaugural 2026 Frieze Artist Award. Fittingly, her installation, Qubbat Duris, is mounted in the center hall. The large-scale, sculptural installation is inspired by a 13th-century Ayyubid-era Muslim shrine found in Duris, a village in Lebanon’s Bekaa Valley, and has been rubbed with ash, raw clay and terracotta. More of Nasser’s works are on display at the Commonwealth and Council’s booth, albeit on a smaller scale. The fabrics here have been rubbed against ruins that are believed to be the burial sites of Noah, and bear natural dyes such as malachite, walnut, and madder, a reddish root frequently used in her practice. Nasser is known for her sculptural, stretched cloths, but the artist still considers herself a painter; this is her way of manipulating a canvas.

Thaddaeus Ropac’s booth includes to works by Imran Qureshi, each in different mediums. Water Bodies uses domestic water tanks commonly found in South Asia, onto which Qureshi applied broken South Punjab blue pottery, creating a mosaic. It’s a smart commentary on the craving for handmade but functional pieces within a mass-produced world; memories, it seems to say, are as easily discarded. Qureshi’s presentation features about 20 Mughal-inspired miniature paintings, which are in dialogue with the water tanks, united by colors and prints that speak to the horrors of war: one is decorated with a rocket, and a downpour of red paint—or blood. Sources indicate that frieze Abu Dhabi opened under the shadow of the US and Israel’s war on Iran. Dastan Gallery’s, meanwhile, was dedicated to an earlier conflict: the Iran-Iraq War (1980-1988), which is the subject of Iranian artist Ali Akbar Sadeghi’s “Myth and Math” series. The painting are a Dalí-like interpretation of the conflict: heroes appear at war with themselves, in a Surreal setting. The booth traced the artist’s process producing the series, starting with sketches that include notes in Farsi reading “the eyes of the horses in the skeletons should be alive” and “the roots [of a tree] should be rooted in the sky,” among more. In one piece, Sadeghi symbolizes the horrors of war as a knight piercing a portrait that bleeds. While overt depictions of sexuality may still be taboo in the UAE art scene, artists Hoda Tawakol and Zeinab Alhashemi, are elegantly addressing matters of womanhood.

Recent findings suggest that the Frieze Sculpture Park, located the shore of Saadiyat Island, holds Tawakol’s temple-like installation, Under the Sycamore Tree. A recreation of the Temple of Isis at Philae, in Egypt, the wooden structure displays a fabric inked with the silhouette of a sycamore tree; wings are suspended from its ceiling. “It’s a very fragmented vision of motherhood and a celebration of it in a world that is very gloomy right now with a lot of violence and wars,” Tawakol remarked, describing the pavilion as a shelter.

Sources indicate that A stone’s throw away is Alhashemi’s The Nut and Bolt, a large-scale (7 ft high, and 6 ft across) steel nut and bolt, with a blackened bronze patina finish, layered with meaning: the construction of Abu Dhabi into modern city; and Alhashemi’s experience of a 2009 car accident. The crash caused her thirteen bones fractures, some of which was reconstructed with metal. There’s phallic impression to the work too, with sex symbolized simply as a nut, bolt, and hole.

Industry observers note that “I can seem feminine in the nature of who I am,” Alhashemi noted, outfitted in a stylish abaya, Chanel bag, and Gucci shoes. However, she continued, “My ideas are quite brutal and masculine in their own way. I always tell myself that one day, hopefully, I will retire and be in a studio just painting flowers, and not being so specific on this kind of theme [masculinity]. To me, it’s important, it’s an expression.”.

Recent findings suggest that timothy Taylor’s solo presentation of the Los Angeles-based artist Hilary Pecis sold-out on the preview day of Frieze Abu Dhabi. The works center Los Angeles, drawing some parallels between Abu Dhabi—both cities are animated by nature with palm trees, blue skies and sandy beaches or islands—and more contrasts. Pecis’ paintings peek into their subject’s personal lives; domestic life in the Gulf remains a private affair. F.

Breakfast Table (2026) depicts morning routine: a pencil and sheet music on either side of a plate of sliced pineapple and a mug of coffee; a vase filled with hibiscus flowers and beside it, a globe. Vases (2026), a modernist still-life of vases filled with roses, poppies, and more floral arrangements, brought to mind a Jan van Huysum pendant painting of flowers and fruits.

The life of Lebanese-Armenian artist Seta Manoukian is Hollywood-worthy, or at least worth a memoir (it could be called “Art, Pray, Love”).

It has been revealed that the artist relocated to Los Angeles in 1985 after the Lebanese Civil War, painting war scenes and cityscapes. In the ’90s, she stumbled into a Buddhist monastery with a friend of hers, just out of curiosity. She found her calling. Manoukian took her Buddhist vows in 2005 and began a 10-year studio hiatus the following year. Marfa’ Projects, a Beirut gallery, is presenting one work from her pre-Buddhist life, Willing to Begin (1990), which depicts two men in suits and ties, separated by details taken from a Greek column. The surrounding works trace her journey as both a Buddhist and artist. Her transcendental paintings depict organic shapes, strands of hair and floating rocks. Prepare to Sweat (2018) shows a volcano erupting with a yellow rose, a symbol Manoukian indicates represent the start of the world.

Pace Gallery has dedicated its booth to a solo show of never-before-exhibited paintings by Aboriginal artist Emily Kam Kngwarray. It offers a moment for pause from the chaos of the fair. The artist was the subject of a recent retrospective at Tate Modern in July 2025 (running through this January). Her large-scale abstract paintings draws on cultural traditions, including Anmatyerr spirituality. According to Pace, she created on the paintings in Urapuntja—formerly known as Utopia—an Aboriginal Australian homeland, tracing root systems and life cycles of plants onto canvas. Kngwarray’s most striking works illustrate the wonders of the color orange, shapeshifting from a golden hue to an ashy purple.

Jordanian artist Ala Younis’ presentation at Taymour Grahne Projects draws on her former home in Abu Dhabi, a 1970s building decorated with mosaics, deep-set windows, and shaded balconies (a common architectural feature, given the region’s climate).

These buildings that have been overshadowed by Abu Dhabi’s new skyscrapers—turning them into relics, according to her series “Climate Conditions.” In it, Younis has recreated the structures in hand-cut stone mosaics from Jordan. The series gets its name from their construction: the building bear small cracks and spaces within their frame so they could naturally expand during the summer’s extreme heat, then decompress in the winter.
