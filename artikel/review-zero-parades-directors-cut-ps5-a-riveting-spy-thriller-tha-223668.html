Review: Zero Parades: Director's Cut (PS5) - A Riveting Spy Thriller That Still Lacks Disco Elysium's Bite

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

I’m on sub-level three of a long-abandoned rocket silo, reading heavily redacted files by flashlight. Most of the screen is black, save for a little pang of light from my torch, and my heart beats rapidly as I comb through reams of text on the right of the screen.

Industry observers note that it’s not flashy nor is it a massive spectacle, and it’s taken me hours to get to this point. However, it may be one of the most engrossing gaming moments I’ve had on PS5 this year. (Related: Latest Update: F1 star Max Verstappen played 40 hours o...)

This game is Zero Parades: Director’s Cut, the spiritual successor to ZA/UM’s enormously praised Disco Elysium, which has finally made its way to PS5 after launching on PC earlier in 2026. (Related: Director of Machine Learning Insights...)

It’s a text-and-dice-based RPG, where Operant (read: spy) Cascade has returned to the town of Portofiro five years after an assignment went horribly wrong. This is her chance at redemption.

However, upon arriving in Portofiro, she finds her double comatose in their safe house, and with no clue as to what her assignment actually is, Cascade must take to the streets.

What you’ll find there is a massive, sprawling collection of mysteries that weave and connect in myriad ways. A girl’s plea to help find her missing dad may link to a poetry magazine that could be a front. A cosmonaut figure in the bustling bazaar was my first thread that eventually led me to read through documents underground in the dark 20 hours later.

According to recent reports, admittedly, there are times that pacing is to its detriment. The opening hours are especially slow, as you get a lay of the land. However, like any good mystery thriller, I'm of the opinion you have to put in the time before you can reap those mysterious rewards. It's not going to be for everyone — which should be obvious with the whole text-and-dice-based RPG descriptor — however, if it is your thing, then your patience will pay off.

According to recent reports, zero Parades is a familiar game for anyone that played Disco Elysium, but it does have its own unique identity. There’s a lot of crossover, from the style of narration to the stacked-against-you odds of its dice-driven actions. However, where Disco is a more internal reflection of our initially nameless detective, Zero Parades sprinkles some of that in with a deal more narrative mystery, and I love that about it.

According to recent reports, cascade is basically a f*ck up, haunted by the elusive errors of her past, so while you may have the skill somewhere inside of you to be a good spy, it’s but a fleeting thing. It’s entirely down to how you level up Cascade, across the faculties of action, relation, and intelligence to define the kind of spy you are.

Kicking off my investigation, I pile a ton of points into intelligence skill, particularly Technoflex, the study of all things tech. Sometimes this skill is subtly useful, like being able to fix the fax machine in the photo printing store where my safe house is located. Other times, it proves its worth in massive ways, like being able to bypass the security lock of a secret prison, because I am aware of a system flaw in that precise model.

Recent findings suggest that however, there is real flexibility to Zero Parades’ exploration and progression. For example, that secret prison has multiple ways in, and you may be able to talk yourself in via the front door if you have the right skills levelled up.

By the time you figure out your assignment, the narrative has laid the foundation for many different approaches, and each can take you on its own weird road through the streets of Portofiro, and your success down those roads is dependent on how you build up your character.

And let me be clear: you can simply fail in Zero Parades – it’s sort of the crux of the whole experience. Whether it’s a botched escape from a mysterious man, or completely messing up an attempt to construct a homemade gun, Zero Parades is brilliant at making it feel like the odds are stacked against you.

That is emphasised all the more with the stress system. Across fatigue, anxiety, and delirium (which relate to your action, relation, and intelligence upgrades), certain actions will build up pressure in any three of these gauges. As it ascends, Cascade will be hit with nerfs of varying severity, and if the scale maxes out, it will refresh and deduct a skill point for its related skill group.

In a recent development, there are ways to juggle this, of course. Smoking cigarettes, eating sweets, or certain drinks can lower specific gauges whilst increasing others, and sleeping is possible to reduce all gauges but is time-locked. You are then able to exert yourself to increase the chances of hitting the required dice numbers, but this comes at the cost of increasing the scale of its related gauge.

According to recent reports, the whole experience becomes a balancing act while trying to figure out which gauge may still require space to carry out certain actions. Maybe you decide not to engage in some activity for fear of needing it in something more important. Or maybe you spend it all wanting to cover everything, and then suffer the consequences when you really need it in a pivotal story moment.

According to recent reports, this whole system means I am vehemently aware of Cascade’s physical and mental state at pretty much all times across the story. It also means that no part of the game’s content is trivial, as it all ties into one and other, not just narratively but mechanically. What I do now directly impacts what I can do later.

Recent findings suggest that by the time the credits rolled, my ending felt especially my own. My own failures, successes, and regrets. And it massively encourages me to go for another try, to hopefully unlock new avenues, level up in different areas, and maybe even finish successfully (I messed up badly).

According to recent reports, as you can tell, I am a major fan of this game, but it doesn’t get everything right. For one, the writing is great, as you would hope for, but it lacks the same razor edge that Disco Elysium has in spades. Voice acting is generally brilliant, but at times it feels like a game doing its best Disco impression, with a yearning to be as edgy as possible.

I did come to like Cascade’s voice performance a lot more as I spent more time with it, but it undeniably lacks the same punch as Lenvel Brown’s dulcet tones as The Narrator in Disco Elysium.

Sources indicate that then we come to the PS5’s performance. I played this on both PS5 and PS5 Pro, and the performance is surprisingly bad. For 90% of the game, as you talk to various characters, it’s obviously not an issue – you’re mainly just looking at still screens as text moves down the right side. However, when you’re running around the town of Portofiro, the frame rate consistently tanks.

According to recent reports, it’s a shame because the art style is quite gorgeous, as it somehow mixes a painterly art style with a simultaneously dull aesthetic. Like in the story, Portofiro is a town of stalled potential, left to rot over its beauty. DualSense haptics are also surprisingly great, albeit subtle. It really makes you feel like you're flicking through redacted documents with nuanced ripples at your fingertips.

Recent findings suggest that zero Parades: Director’s Cut is a dense and highly detailed text-based RPG, that moves at a painstakingly slow pace. However, it’s part of the magic of this story. It lacks the same authenticity as Disco Elysium, but manages to hold its own with a riveting mystery that very naturally unfolds.

It’s riveting when a small detail links back to the previously abandoned thread, and you feel like a spy yourself as you try to piece it all together. It lets you relish in the world and revel in the chaos, and as the credits rolled, I just wanted to jump back in and try it all over again.
