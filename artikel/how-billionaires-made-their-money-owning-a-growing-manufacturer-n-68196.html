How Billionaires Made Their Money – Owning a Growing Manufacturer, Not a Salary

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Most billionaires got rich by founding or holding a big stake in a company that kept growing, not by earning a salary. You can use the same method on a small scale. Own stock through low-cost index funds and hold it for decades, and keep as much ownership as you can in any business you start.

Even a $1 million salary, saved in full, would take 1,000 years to add up to $1 billion. (Related: Breaking: A child support fight exposed a Trump adviser...)

A stake in a tech giant is different, because its value rises as long as the company grows and you hold on. Jeff Bezos founded Amazon in 1994 and still owns 8% of it, the largest part of his fortune. Elon Musk’s fortune is mostly the 38% of SpaceX he owns, counting options to buy shares, with less in his nearly 11% of Tesla. (Related: Meta’s ‘secret’ Black Friday sale just started, and it ...)

Forbes counts 71% of the 2026 Forbes 400, its list of the richest Americans, as self-made, meaning they built their fortunes rather than inheriting them. The rest started with an inheritance, like Alice Walton, whose fortune comes from her stake in Walmart, the company her father cofounded.

The part you can copy is ownership. If you start a business, keep as much of it as you can, because each share you sell early gives its future gains to the buyer.

Recent findings suggest that don’t copy how much of their wealth they keep in one company, though, since few single stocks pay off like that. Finance professor Hendrik Bessembinder found that four of every seven U.S. stocks since 1926 returned less over their lifetimes than one-month Treasury bills, which are short-term U.S. government debt. The best-performing 4% of companies produced the stock market’s entire net gain over that span.

In a recent development, A broad, low-cost index fund, such as one tracking the whole U.S. stock market, owns thousands of companies, so it holds the few big winners without you having to pick them. At an assumed 7% average annual return, $10,000 left in one for 30 years grows to about $76,000, because each year’s gain earns returns too. Building wealth on an ordinary income works the same way, by spending less than you earn and investing the gap for decades.
