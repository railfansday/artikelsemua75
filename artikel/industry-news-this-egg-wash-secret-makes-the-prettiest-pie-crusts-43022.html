The secret to a beautifully golden Thanksgiving pie might be the simplest ingredient in your kitchen.

According to recent reports, thanksgiving pies have a way of bringing out the perfectionist in even the most relaxed bakers. Yet you might spend half an hour creating the perfect pie dough design, carefully crimping the edges or cutting little leaves from scraps of pastry, only to pull the finished pie from the oven and discover that your beautiful handiwork has browned unevenly. It’s a small disappointment in the grand scheme of things, but after all that effort, a little indignation is understandable. (Related: AI for Game Development: Creating a Farming Game in 5 D...)

Industry observers note that an egg wash is supposed to give pastry a lovely golden color and delicate sheen, but getting it to cooperate isn’t always as simple as beating an egg and picking up a pastry brush. Fortunately, there’s an easy trick that makes a noticeable difference, and it comes courtesy of a familiar face in the baking world: Great British Baking Show judge Paul Hollywood. (Related: Industry News: Director of Machine Learning Insights...)

Paul’s secret is simply to add a pinch of salt to the beaten egg. He lets it sit for about five minutes before brushing it onto pastry dough. In a BBC Food demonstration, Paul uses the technique to glaze scones, but it works just as well for giving pie crusts a beautiful, even finish.

Beat an egg with a small pinch of salt until they’re well combined, then set the mixture aside for a few minutes. Give it another quick stir before brushing a thin, even layer over the pastry. The amount of salt won’t affect the flavor, and there’s no reason to make any other adjustments to your favorite pie recipe.

The difference is subtle but useful. Instead of clinging stubbornly to your pastry brush or collecting in the little crevices of a crimped edge, the egg wash spreads more easily.

Emiko Franzen For Taste Of Home.

If you’ve ever tried brushing a plain beaten egg onto pastry, you’ve probably noticed that it can be surprisingly thick and stringy. That’s because the proteins in the egg give it structure—a nuisance if you need to spread it evenly. Adding salt loosens those proteins, making the egg wash thinner and more fluid after a few minutes. It glides over the pastry more smoothly, leaving a light, even coating rather than stringy puddles.

In a recent development, the egg itself is what gives pastry its golden color and glossy sheen. Salt doesn’t make the crust magically browner; it simply helps the egg wash do its job better. The result is a more consistent finish, particularly around the edges and decorative details where egg wash tends to collect.

It has been revealed that A pinch of salt won’t turn a lopsided pie into a masterpiece, but it can help a well-made crust look its best. And after we’ve spent an unreasonable amount of time weaving strips of pastry into a lattice for a Thanksgiving pie, I think we’re entitled to a little help with the finishing touches.
