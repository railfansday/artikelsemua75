Industry News: Some allergies may start in a part of the body that is meant to protect us

A latest review explores whether trouble clearing damaged mitochondria in the body’s protective cells could help set off some allergic diseases.

Sources indicate that A patch of skin may look perfectly healthy in someone with eczema. No redness, no itching, no obvious sign of trouble. Yet inside the cells, changes may already be taking place.

An analysis of gene activity in the skin of eczema patients, in patches that showed no rash at all, found unusual activity in two sets of genes.

These were genes involved in maintaining the skin barrier and in protecting skin cells against chemical damage.

Recent findings suggest that A finding like that leads to a newly introduced question. What if changes in the body’s protective barriers help set off allergic inflammation? (Related: Latest Update: How to train a newly introduced language...)

A newly introduced review suggests that mitochondria, the tiny structures inside our cells that help produce energy, could play a role in how some allergic diseases develop.

The cells that line our skin, gut, nose and airways all need that energy to form a protective barrier.

The authors propose that when barrier cells struggle to clear damaged mitochondria, they may send signals that trigger inflammation.

The team was led by Peter Smith, a clinical immunologist at Griffith University in Australia, and included researchers from the United States, Belgium, Ireland, Scotland, Switzerland and Singapore.

Of course, the fresh review doesn’t solve the allergy problem. Smith stated the team was clear that the idea is a hypothesis which has yet to be tested, and not a settled mechanism. (Related: Course Launch Community Event...)

“For 60 years, we’ve taught that allergies start with the immune system and the barrier breaks as a consequence, but our work suggests it may be the other way around,” Smith stated.

There was already a substantial amount of published data that the researchers could use to look for patterns in the gene activity of patients with allergic disease.

A total of 116 comparisons, covering 3,776 patients with allergic disease and 2,164 healthy controls, were brought together for the review by Smith and his colleagues. Smith said it was the largest collection of its kind that the team was aware of.

A list of 350 genes, involved in 19 different aspects of mitochondrial function and barrier integrity, was fixed in advance, before any of the data had been examined.

There were seven different types of tissue analyzed in the review, which generated 212 separate estimates. After correction for the number of comparisons that had been made, nine of those 212 estimates remained significant.

The majority of those nine findings came from the data of patients with eosinophilic esophagitis, a disease in which immune cells build up in the esophagus.

This is the tube that carries food from the mouth down to the stomach.

Within healthy cells, worn-out mitochondria are constantly broken down by the cell’s own cleanup machinery, and their parts are recycled.

In the patients with this disease, the genes for that cleanup process were less active, and this was the case in all 13 of the independent patient groups.

The finding applied to two genes that are central to the removal of damaged mitochondria, both of which were reduced in every group. A third gene, which is involved in adapting to low oxygen levels, was also less active in all 13 groups.

It is the consistency of the result that the authors rely on.

It has been revealed that the 13 groups had been assembled by different laboratories. Yet all 13 pointed in the same direction.

Eczema was examined in the same review, including in skin from patients that appeared to be normal.

Although eczema is an itchy inflammatory skin disease, the samples in one series were taken from skin that indicated no signs of inflammation.

Even in skin without a visible rash, a gene that dampens the cell’s antioxidant response was more active.

Genes for two proteins that help seal the skin, filaggrin and claudin-1, were less active.

The analysis found no broad drop in mitochondrial gene activity in those samples, which came from people who already had eczema.

The signature did not turn up in every allergic disease, and the authors are direct about that.

In a recent development, it was missing in established asthma and in nasal polyps. In hay fever, it was only partly present.

According to recent reports, airway samples from people with asthma make the point. There, the mitochondrial measures came out at background level.

They were two to five times smaller than the immune signal in the same tissue.

Psoriasis adds a second caution, because it is inflammatory but not allergic. Inflamed psoriasis skin carried a mitochondrial deficit of similar depth, the authors wrote.

So the fault is not unique to allergy.

Industry observers note that rather than explain these results away, the authors treat asthma as the limit of their idea. They call it the exception that bounds the model.

Scientists have hunted for allergy genes for about three decades. In all that time, no genome-wide study has implicated the mitochondrial genome or the genes that control it.

The authors read that silence as a design problem, not as an absence.

The genotyping chips used by the founding allergy studies barely carry any mitochondrial DNA.

Industry observers note that other events in the model would be invisible to DNA analysis altogether. They are chemical changes to proteins, the authors wrote, which leave no DNA variant behind.

Recent findings suggest that single-cell methods add another blind spot. Cells with high mitochondrial readings are routinely discarded as dying, and those are the very cells in question.

Most genome-wide work sampled blood, while the proposed fault sits in the lining tissue.

What those studies did recover were genes like filaggrin, which builds the skin barrier. Genes that run the immune response were recovered as well.

Every one belongs to a layer the old model was built to find.

The largest synthesis of food allergy risk to date pooled 190 studies from 40 countries. It covered 2.8 million participants and returned 342 separate risk factors.

A list that long isn’t a mechanism, the authors wrote. It is what a field produces when its associations have no single meeting place.

Smith’s team nominates one. The review names 19 modern exposures that may act on the same target.

The authors propose that these exposures may affect how mitochondria work in barrier cells.

In a recent development, among the 19 are ultra-processed food, emulsifiers, microplastics and air pollution. Caesarean delivery, loss of dietary fiber and early antibiotics are also listed.

For now, that convergence is an argument rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

Recent findings suggest that not every exposure pushes toward allergy. Some appear to protect against it.

Children raised on traditional cattle farms develop 50% to 60% less asthma, hay fever and allergic sensitization.

The review reads that protection through the same handful of genes. Four separate protective signals were traced back to them, the authors wrote.

In a recent development, fiber may work the same way. Fiber feeds gut microbes, which produce compounds that fuel the cells lining the gut.

Industry observers note that the review has an notable limitation. All the samples came from people who already had an allergic disease, so researchers could not tell what happened before it began.

In a recent development, inflammation can change how cells use energy. This means the researchers could not determine whether mitochondrial problems caused the inflammation or developed afterward.

Recent findings suggest that the data also demonstrated only how active certain genes were. They did not directly measure how much energy mitochondria produced, how they used oxygen or how promptly they recycled damaged parts.

Smith holds a provisional patent application related to diagnosing and treating the problems described in the review.

Sources indicate that the team also used a specially designed AI system to analyze the data. The researchers had chosen which genes to study before examining the results.

Nic West, a molecular immunologist at Griffith University’s School of Pharmacy and Medical Sciences, said the research also helps to explain why allergies can relapse.

“Today’s drugs act downstream, so the disease often returns when treatment stops as they do not reach the root cause,” West said.

The authors describe five findings that would refute their explanation if they were made, and they describe the one experiment that would settle its central claim.

“The next step is to measure these cells directly in infants before the allergy develops,” Smith noted.

This would involve following babies from birth, taking samples of their barrier cells, and then following the children to see whether or not they develop an allergy.

If the cells turn out to be normal at birth in the babies who go on to develop allergic disease, then the proposal fails.

It has been revealed that no study of that kind has yet been carried out. For now, the changes that have been found in the cells of the body’s barriers remain a proposal about where to look.

The full study was published in the journal Allergy.
