Ken Ringdahl is the Chief Technology Officer at Emburse.

Sources indicate that even as enterprises say farewell to “tokenmaxxing” and hello to “tokenminning” to rein in ballooning AI costs, some employees are still using AI for everything they can. That “everything” includes a healthy share of non-work-related tasks.​. (Related: Latest Update: How to train a newly introduced language...)

My company’s June study on expense fraud found that 61% of U.S. and U.K. business professionals use employer-funded AI tools for personal purposes. Another survey from research institute Epoch AI found that 76% of responding employees with employer-provided AI subscriptions “reported using AI at least as much for work as for personal tasks.”. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Recent findings suggest that while enterprises limit agentic coding tools to cut down on AI spending, workers are using those same licenses to fix their dating profiles, initiative weddings and write cover letters. If that’s not a sign to get serious about implementing AI spend control and usage policies, I don’t know what is.

Recent findings suggest that according to our survey, 26% of responding U.S. and U.K. professionals admitted that nearly a third of their logged AI use was for personal reasons. These ranged from routine tasks to major life events, such as:​.

• Handling life admin, such as meal planning or gift research (23%).

• Asking for life and relationship advice (17%).

• Planning a wedding or other special event (13%).

• Improving a dating profile (8%)​.

It has been revealed that asking AI to draft a cover letter or a meal plan doesn’t burn tokens at nearly the same rate as legitimate enterprise use cases such as writing code or multistep reasoning, for example. Still, these costs add up, particularly when personal queries account for a meaningful portion of overall AI use. Enterprises are scrambling to control AI costs while unwittingly financing employees’ job searches.

Ask if they’d consider their activity to be misuse, and many employees would point out that they’ve been encouraged or even pressured to adopt these tools. In fact, some think crossing the line between home and work benefits their employers.

The number one reason (stated by 35% of U.S. employees we surveyed) workers are motivated to use their employers’ AI tools is to be more effective in their personal lives so they can ultimately be more effective on the job. Another 25% mentioned that employer-sponsored programs either mandating or promoting AI drive them to use these tools outside of work to “learn faster.”.

Further underscoring the need for education and clearer guidelines, we found that 23% of respondents noted they simply weren’t aware of policies against personal AI usage.

It has been revealed that businesses depend on their employees to become AI-proficient. They need to make the most of their investments. How can they keep workers safe and compliant once they’ve handed over the keys to the toolshed?​.

Stronger infosec controls have to be part of the solution. HERE Enterprise’s August 2026 “AI Toggle Tax Survey” found that 72% of enterprise workers admitted to navigating around their organization’s AI policies. Popular workarounds included using personal AI accounts on company-issued devices or using corporate AI tools on their personal devices.

Recent findings suggest that secure web gateways (SWG) can help prevent this by blocking unauthorized LLM access. Data loss prevention (DLP) tools can reduce the risk of sensitive data leaks. Browser-level controls can redact or watermark an AI-assisted export before it leaves the workspace and prevent capture when PII is displayed on screen.​.

Education is another part. Because many AI tools are presented as chat interfaces, employees can forget they’re sharing information with an outside provider. Employees may not realize that inputting something as “harmless” as a spreadsheet of wedding guest contact information risks exposing personal data.

It has been revealed that crafting an AI usage policy can unite both of these efforts, and every credible one starts with a list of approved tools. InfoSec, finance and IT should work with procurement to vet AI vendors and publish a record of which platforms are cleared for what tasks and with what data. Anything off-list should be off-limits by default. This group should scrub the list on a quarterly basis, since latest enterprise tools pop up every day. ​.

It has been revealed that your AI usage policy should also make it clear what data can and can’t leave the perimeter. Describe your infosec controls in plain language so employees think twice about taking their work AI tools home with them.​.

If companies knew more about how employees were using their AI, they’d cut a lot of waste and reinvest in revenue-driving activities. Finance already tracks spend by vendor and department everywhere else in the business. Why shouldn’t AI platforms get the same treatment?

In a recent development, the problem is that treating employees like suspects as you investigate their AI use hardly fosters an innovation culture. A better approach is to ask teams to make the business case for the tools they want. At my company, teams requesting a new AI platform are required to state the expected return as part of our review process. Once approved, we evaluate its impact at regular intervals. That encourages a sense of ownership.​.

When teams are responsible for proving ROI, they’re more likely to use AI tools judiciously. You don’t want to police every prompt. You do want to make AI spend, access and accountability as disciplined as every other business investment.​.
