Creative Arts Agency CEO Bryan Lourd urged Congress on Saturday to pass the NO FAKES Act, a bill that would regulate the wave of unauthorized AI deepfakes proliferating the internet, writing in a New York Times Opinion piece that “every one of us should have the right to say no to an unwelcome use of our likeness.”.

The NO FAKES Act was introduced by Sen. Chris Coons (D-ME) in May, following a previous attempt at the bill last year. While it advanced out of the Senate Judiciary Committee in June and has a bipartisan coalition of co-sponsors, the full Senate has not taken it up. “I come to this issue not as a policymaker but as a practitioner. I run a global talent agency. Part of our job is to help our clients license their likenesses and performances, sometimes to AI companies,” he added. “We regularly negotiate those deals, and I can tell you that the bill pending in Congress delivers what the industry needs and what will protect all Americans.”.

Industry observers note that lourd laid out the stakes for Hollywood by citing Tom Hanks, who warned his followers in 2023 about a dental office using an AI deepfake of a younger version of him in an advertisement. Hanks likely wouldn’t have agreed to the dental ad, Lourd wrote, but he should’ve been given a choice before the dental office ran with an unauthorized deepfake. “What’s missing is a bright-line rule affirming that right in the AI age, and it is one Congress can supply before the year is out,” Lourd wrote.

CAA has advocated against the unauthorized proliferation of celebrity deepfakes online, working with the Cate Blanchett-backed Human Consent Foundation to help people control permissions for their likenesses if they’re scraped by AI models. The agency also joined the foundation and other entertainment backers in an amicus brief in the Latest York Times’ lawsuit against OpenAI, noting the active licensing market for likenesses that’s developed in the AI era.

Recent findings suggest that deepfakes have been a constant presence since large-language models emerged in 2022, with many being used to produce unauthorized and sometimes explicit depictions of actors, singers and other celebrities. Other industry backers of the NO FAKES Act include SAG-AFTRA, the Motion Picture Association, the Recording Industry Association of America, the Recording Academy and the National Association of Broadcasters, OpenAI, the AFL-CIO, YouTube and IBM.
