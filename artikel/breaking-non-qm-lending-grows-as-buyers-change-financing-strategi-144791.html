Homebuyers are increasingly willing to change their mortgage financing strategies to get into the housing market, helping drive growth in nonqualified mortgage (non-QM) lending according to latest research from Cotality.

Data presented during a Cotality webinar last week found that 69% of buyers initially cut lifestyle spending to make homeownership more affordable. But once these adjustments become insufficient, 65% turn to changing their financing options, according to the organization’s analysis of homebuyer behavior.

The findings point to growing demand for alternative mortgage products.

It has been revealed that “The economy and the way people earn income has changed,” remarked Selma Hepp, chief economist at Cotality. “Non-QM is becoming a permanent component of the mortgage market because the borrower base it serves is permanent.”. Recent findings suggest that hepp pointed to the growing number of business owners, independent contractors, commission-based workers, real estate investors and households with multiple income sources. She cited approximately 11.9 million independent contractors, representing 7.4% of employment, and 9 million multiple-job holders, representing 5.5% of employed people. In a recent development, many of these borrowers may have the financial resources to repay a mortgage but struggle to demonstrate their income through conventional underwriting. But Hepp cautioned that alternative underwriting should not be presented as a simple affordability solution. “What it can do is align underwriting more closely with what the borrower’s actual financial capacity is,” she mentioned.

Nonagency lending has expanded in recent years, driven primarily by non-QM loans, noted Patrick Kaiser, Cotality’s principal product manager in property intelligence.

Kaiser said non-QM lending was on pace to exceed 2025 volume by the end of 2026 and could grow again in 2027 if current trends continue. Loans for consumer borrowers remain the largest share of the market, but debt-service-coverage ratio (DSCR) loans for real estate investors are gaining ground.

Industry observers note that investor lending brings its own risks. Rising insurance premiums, property taxes and other operating costs can weaken rental-property cash flow, Hepp remarked. A softer labor market could also put pressure on borrowers with self-employment or variable income.

It has been revealed that but Holly Niemeier, a senior loan officer at NEXA Lending who works primarily with investors, told HousingWire that investor demand has kept her busy despite elevated borrowing costs. Her clients range from first-time investors to those with hundreds of properties, including some looking to acquire as many as 20 at a time.

Industry observers note that “People are having a hard time getting loans, and the investors now, the prices have dropped some, so I’m busier than I’ve ever been this month,” she noted. “The rates aren’t really affecting the investors. They’re still buying.”.

Niemeier said investors use different strategies depending on their available capital. Some buy properties with cash, renovate them and refinance, while others purchase turnkey rentals with existing tenants. Those who run low on capital may use hard money loans to acquire and renovate properties before refinancing into DSCR loans.

Virtually all of Niemeier’s investor business involves DSCR financing, either for individual properties or through portfolio loans. She also sees investors favoring lower-cost properties in urban markets such as St. Louis and Detroit, where smaller down payments can make it easier to expand a portfolio.

It has been revealed that “The investors don’t seem to flinch,” she mentioned. “They don’t change anything.”.
