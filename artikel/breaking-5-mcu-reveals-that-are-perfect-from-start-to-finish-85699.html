According to recent reports, in just a few years, the MCU has gone from dabbling (sometimes awkwardly) in TV to churning out small-screen adventures on an almost industrial level. Since the launch of Disney+, Marvel has drastically expanded its TV universe with live-action and animated shows that introduce newly introduced characters and bring back beloved heroes in unexpected ways. While some have been more successful than others, and not everything has landed, Marvel's TV shows remain a place for reinvention and creative risks their movie counterparts rarely attempt. Recent findings suggest that that willingness to take chances has led to some of Marvel's most inventive storytelling, as well as a few forgettable indicates that missed the mark entirely. With Avengers Doomsday just around the corner, there's still time to revisit or discover for the first time some MCU TV shows that are perfect from start to finish.

A meta Hollywood satire that's more buddy comedy than superhero spectacle, Wonder Man features one of the MCU's most unexpectedly sincere friendships, and is a refreshingly unconventional change of pace. Down-on-his-luck actor Simon Williams (Yahya Abdul-Mateen II) has real superpowers, though he'd rather be a star than a hero. A chance to live out his dream arrives when he meets veteran actor and former fake Mandarin, Trevor Slattery (Ben Kingsley), whose King Lear is still the toast of Croydon. Trevor reveals that Simon's favorite movie, Wonder Man, is getting a substantial-budget reboot, and suggests Simon could be perfect for the starring role. Yet their encounter wasn't an accident; to stay out of trouble for his past transgressions, the Department of Damage Control (DODC) is forcing Trevor to spy on Simon. Sources indicate that created by Destin Daniel Cretton and showrunner Andrew Guest, Wonder Man keeps the stakes personal, focusing on both leads' hopes of finding success in their craft. For Simon, that's a difficult proposition, as those with superpowers are banned from working in Hollywood due to an unfortunate incident that led to actor Josh Gad's super-powered demise. As the duo navigate Hollywood's absurdity and poke fun at franchise filmmaking, Simon and Trevor's unlikely friendship becomes the true star of the series. Wonder Man was initially renewed for a second season, but that was reversed a few months later, leaving one of Marvel's best TV indicates abruptly canceled. We might not have gotten an ending, but Wonder Man is still perfect from start to finish.

We'd barely had time to get the Agatha All Along theme from WandaVision out of our heads before Kathryn Hahn's scene-stealing sorceress conjured her own wickedly enjoyable spin-off. After a mysterious yet familiar teenager (Joe Locke) breaks Wanda's spell, Agatha is freed from her crime-drama prison, but all her powers are gone. To get them back, she'll assemble a upcoming coven of magical misfits, forge unlikely alliances, and journey down the treacherous Witches' Road that promises to restore what was lost, if she can survive.

Recent findings suggest that doctor Strange fully opened the magical and mystical doors in the MCU, and the brilliant one-off Werewolf By Night embraced the horror. Series creator Jac Schaeffer finds the perfect balance between the two, and explores the losses and decisions that shaped Agatha into the heartlessly cruel (yet fabulous) witch she is today. Stopping short of making her a full-on antihero, Agatha All Along lives in the same moral grey area as its main character, and has an outrageously good time doing it. Aided (and frequently undermined) on her quest by former friend-turned-rival Rio Vidal (Aubrey Plaza), Agatha All Along is a darkly enchanting adventure into Marvel's supernatural. It’s the kind of magic we need to see more of in the MCU.

The closing chapter in the story that started with WandaVision is as thoughtfully profound and riotously entertaining as you'd expect from this groundbreaking TV trilogy. Five years on from Westview, Vision (Paul Bettany) is in hiding, working in a bar in Scotland, a role he seems oddly over and under-qualified for simultaneously. He may seek a quiet life, but inside, his digital mind is crowded with older Stark A.I. programs like J.A.R.V.I.S. (James D'Arcy), F.R.I.D.A.Y. (Orla Brady), and the eloquently spoken, ever-scheming Ultron (James Spader) living in the attic. Vision's introspection spirals out of control when a mysterious figure from his past resurfaces, forcing him to confront painful memories and to ask himself, "What does it truly mean to be alive?".

Created by Terry Matalas, the father-son dynamic between Vision and Ultron is the central processing unit of the series, with Spader on exceptional form as the snarky in-the-flesh Ultron. Almost every line he utters is quotable, yet there's a genuine softness and begrudging affection for his would-be son. Vision's journey becomes more than mere survival; it's a story about self-discovery, acceptance, and family. Pulling threads together from WandaVision and Agatha All Along, VisionQuest is a tremendous achievement: a smart, soulful, deeply satisfying series that ends with a post-credits scene that shockingly sets up what's next, Avengers Doomsday.

Recent findings suggest that avengers Endgame may have closed out an era of the MCU, but it still left many unanswered questions. At the top of most fans' lists was: Where did the 2012 Loki (Tom Hiddleston) go when he nabbed the Tesseract during the time heist? As it turns out, he landed in the Gobi Desert in Mongolia, but the God of Mischief is swiftly detained by the Time Variance Authority (TVA) and whisked to their mind-bending headquarters. There he meets Mobius (Owen Wilson), a jet-ski-loving agent who enlists his help to find Sylvie (Sophia Di Martino), a dangerously unpredictable Loki variant with her own agenda.

In a recent development, loki's redemptive arc seemed complete by Thor: Ragnarok and his brief, heroic appearance in Infinity War. But by pulling him straight from 2012's Battle of Fresh York, series creator Michael Waldron rewinds the clock and dives into a more self-serving, unrepentant version of Marvel's beloved bad boy. Loki's two-season journey finds him burdened with a new glorious purpose that reshapes the future of the multiverse itself. Along the way, he encounters a wild cacophony of variants (including fan favorite Classic Loki played by Richard E. Grant), forcing him to examine the multitudes within himself. It's a reality-warping and stylish series that rarely loses momentum or its sense of mischief, with every episode as complex and layered as the titular character.

Industry observers note that the first Marvel TV show to launch on Disney+ promised it was going to be something different, and series creator Jac Schaeffer fully delivered with WandaVision. The events of Infinity War and Endgame left Wanda (Elizabeth Olsen) grief-stricken and adrift. In that compromised state, she accidentally creates a powerful hex that traps an entire small town in her fantasy. Wanda and Vision skip through decades of classic TV, with each episode taking on a different style that perfectly mirrors Wanda’s fluctuating emotional state. Meanwhile, Vision grows steadily suspicious of Westview, and outside forces close in. From 1960s studio sitcoms to mockumentary and single-camera formats, WandaVision tells an intimate story of loss, longing, and moving on.

Sources indicate that while that may sound like a lot for a nine-episode series to pack in, WandaVision never fails to have fun with its concept. The show delights in comic-book nods, like Halloween-accurate costumes and Evan Peters' unforgettable guest role as a man claiming to be Wanda's twin brother, Pietro. Also starring a scenery-chewing Kathryn Hahn, alongside returning MCU actors Randall Park, and Kat Dennings, the show juggles its humor and emotional moments effortlessly. In the end, WandaVision is an inventively heartfelt journey that beautifully reframes grief as "love persevering," making it one of the MCU's most complete and rewarding stories to date.
