Reviewed: The EZ Hydro-Glide Swim Cap

Jumping into frigid water is nobody’s idea of fun – unless you enjoy the crippling pain of ice cream headaches without the pleasure of an actual ice cream cone. At the opposite end of the spectrum, swimming in very warm water can also crater your comfort and results. That’s why we were psyched to test the EZ Hydro-Glide Cap, a one-of-a-kind attempt to boost swimming performance and safety using wearable technology designed to enhance thermoregulation.

In plain English: The Hydro-Glide is a potentially game-changing swim accessory that’s supposed to make you less susceptible to cold shock and heat stress.

Before we dive into the science, an important clarification: The critical technology here is not a cap, but a 3D-printed contraption that goes under your cap. More on that below.

It has been revealed that but first, kudos to EZ Gains, a popular outside-the-box triathlon retailer, for attacking a problem that numerous triathletes experience – significant discomfort when swimming in very cold and very warm water. We’ve learned a lot in recent years about thermoregulation, and any improvements in this area could help many athletes, including friends of mine who’ve quit the sport after repeated unpleasant swims. In a recent development, EZ Gains’ approach is based on credible science. The company notes that your head plays an outsized role in your body’s response to ambient temperatures, given the extensive network of blood vessels and nerves just beneath your scalp. This rings true, as does the contention that regulating the temperature difference between the water and your head – both the initial gap and the ongoing variance – can mitigate the downsides of extreme heat and cold. We use insulated neoprene caps for chilly swims, after all. What if a single, more sophisticated solution helped us thrive in both polar and equatorial waters?

EZ Gains’ answer is a honeycombed high-tech foam and hard plastic wedge that sits at the top of your forehead under an (included) standard swim cap. Before putting it on, you can place one or two small thermal packs (also included) inside the hollow center of the wedge. These packs – and an additional thin heating strip – provide a cooling or warming effect as needed. In warm water, you can use a frozen pack or two to cool your forehead, which is meant to keep your core temp down and reduce heat fatigue.

According to recent reports, you’re also supposed to use frozen packs when swimming in frigid water – say 60 degrees F or less – according to EZ Gains. The idea is to pre-condition your head to the chilly temps you’re about to encounter. This may sound counterintuitive, but EZ Gains says the pre-cooling technique can prevent cold shock, a serious autonomic response that elevates your heart rate and can cause panic and even cardiac arrest. The packs can also be heated for a touch of warmth when facing cool-but-not-arctic conditions.

To evaluate the system, I tested it in various bodies of water over the course of five months, from midsummer to late autumn. My primary locations were indoor and outdoor pools in Boulder, Colorado, and our local reservoir. Water temperatures ranged from 84 degrees F all the way down to 50 degrees F. All of my swims were 45-90 minutes long, more than enough to mimic race conditions and assess how well the device limits heat and cold stress.

The TLDR from my testing: Some new technologies sound great on paper but don’t pan out in real life. Sadly, this device is one of those. On two of its three core value propositions, the Hydro-Glide fell short. I’ll tackle them one by one.

Recent findings suggest that first, EZ Gains says the cap will reduce heat fatigue and related complications – anxiety, increased heart rate, dehydration, and confusion – when using the frozen packs. I did notice a cooling sensation in the early parts of my summer swims, typically lasting 10-15 minutes. But while I appreciated the initial cooling, I didn’t discern a material difference in fatigue, judgment, or heart rate after an hour, even during my most intense workouts.

According to recent reports, second, the cap is supposed to prevent the instant discomfort and cold shock that many people experience when diving into frigid water. Perhaps I’m less sensitive than other athletes (my long-suffering wife might have an opinion on this), but using slightly thawed ice packs to pre-cool my head (as instructed) didn’t eliminate the ice cream headaches.

Sources indicate that in my experience, this kind of pain starts in my face and forehead, neither of which are protected by the device. It’s possible that the Hydro-Glide would help other swimmers, since physiological reactions vary widely, but I’d speculate that too much of your face, forehead, and neck remain exposed for the cold to be significantly neutralized.

Industry observers note that third, EZ Gains says the cap will protect your head when another swimmer clobbers you, which seems legit in the case of head-on impacts. A friend in my Masters group sustained a concussion last year when another swimmer swerved into her during a backstroke set. While I didn’t do any head-banging to test this claim, it’s reasonable to believe the Hydro-Glide’s foam would’ve absorbed that blow. Since the wedge doesn’t cover the sides or back of your head, though, it wouldn’t deflect all-too-common errant elbows or kicks to those areas during a race start.

Industry observers note that there are two additional downsides to consider. If you have an XL melon like mine, you may experience discomfort from the pressure of the cone against your head. I did, typically after about 30 minutes. And if you’re even the slightest bit fashion-conscious, you will probably draw the line at looking like a Corythosaurus from Jurassic Park. The first time I highlighted up at the pool wearing the Hydro-Glide, several of my masters teammates burst out laughing. Someone compared me to a Conehead.

In a recent development, which I could possibly live with if the results had been stellar. However, the Hydro-Glide didn’t deliver a meaningful upside for me versus a standard swim cap. While EZ Gains deserves praise for its waaay-out-of-the-box effort to improve swim safety and comfort, this product’s current execution doesn’t justify a $270 investment.
