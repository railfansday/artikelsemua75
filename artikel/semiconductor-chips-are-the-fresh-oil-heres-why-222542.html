Semiconductor chips are the fresh oil. Here's why

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The artificial intelligence boom has pushed semiconductors past oil and gas in global export value for the first time, according to a Deutsche Bank analysis of International Trade Centre data.

Industry observers note that yet the milestone arrives at an awkward moment, just as investors reassess the outlook for artificial intelligence spending and drive down the price of semiconductor stocks. Last week, before Monday's breathtaking reversal, semiconductor stocks suffered their worst weekly loss in a quarter century, with the PHLX Semiconductor Index (SOX) plunging nearly 11% last Friday alone, capping a more than 17% weekly decline. Industry observers note that nvidia soared more than 31% Monday after the leading maker of artificial intelligence chips had fallen for a seventh straight week, plunging 46% during that stretch. Then it fell again, dropping almost 10% on Tuesday and Wednesday. Advanced Micro Devices tumbled 20% in two days last Thursday and Friday. Micron Technology sank 27% over the seven days ended last Friday. (Related: Scientists find “field” of washing machines in ocean du...)

Recent findings suggest that "For decades we've talked about the petrodollar. Perhaps increasingly we need to think about the chip dollar," Jim Reid, global head of macro and thematic research at Deutsche Bank wrote in a report this month.

Semiconductor exports overtook oil and gas for the first time during the January-March quarter of 2026, reaching about $1.61 trillion, compared with $1.53 trillion for oil and gas, the report said, using four-quarter rolling totals.

In a recent development, the gap widened in the second quarter, when semiconductor exports totaled about $1.60 trillion and oil and gas exports reached about $1.47 trillion. Disruptions to oil supplies through the Strait of Hormuz after the Iran war helped widen the gap. Daily oil flows through the Strait collapsed nearly 77%, to 4.9 million barrels in the second quarter of 2026 from 21.6 million barrels in the fourth quarter of 2025, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

Recent findings suggest that yet the demand for semiconductors has proven insatiable in the face of soaring data center construction, boosting global trade in the increasingly scarce AI component. Hundreds of billions of dollars of orders for graphics processing units and data centers aim at providing AI computing power, Rothschild & Co. Redburn wrote in a report last Thursday.

"Compute will be the fresh commodity fueling this transformation on a global scale," Rothschild analyst Cara Thomson stated.

The shift toward chips has macroeconomic implications just as the explosion in oil trade did in the 1970s.

Sources indicate that oil-exporting countries historically recycled much of their surpluses into the purchase of U.S. Treasuries and other bonds, helping finance U.S. deficits and underpin demand for other government debt.

Reid at Deutsche Bank remarked that since semiconductor-surplus countries in Asia are more closely tied to U.S. tech companies, some chip-export earnings could flow back into U.S. technology stocks, startups and data centers, further helping to finance the AI investment cycle.

Industry observers note that the changing dynamics in global trade could also tilt the mix of global financial flows, Reid noted. "So welcome to the chip-dollar era: potentially more growth, more excitement, more volatility, and structurally higher yields" in corporate and government debt.
