Fifteen years ago, I wrote an article about the Grand Theft Auto (GTA) franchise and its impact on underage boys who played it.1 For the uninitiated, GTA is a series of single player, action-adventure games designed for adults which has just released version six after a lull of 13 years. In 2011, when I wrote that article, I worked in a child and adolescent mental health service (CAMHS) where I saw countless referrals for boys aged nine to 13 whose behaviour was concerning, whose emotions were dysregulated and whose spare time was spent playing GTA.

They played for several hours a day, behind closed doors, with their parents’ consent. I heard about the boys’ inability to concentrate, their uncontrollability, their use of sexually explicit language, their violence towards siblings and peers and their lack of empathy. In GTA, violence is commonplace and the role of female characters is merely to provide visual and sexual gratification. I wondered about the link between the boys’ gaming and what adults around them were witnessing. (Related: Latest Update: How to train a newly introduced language...)

It has been revealed that I was particularly concerned by the sexual violence in games like GTA and its impact on sexually developing boys. In GTA IV,2 the protagonist picks up prostitutes, drives them to secluded locations and selects from three levels of service — masturbation, fellatio or full intercourse — while listening to a sexually explicit audio. Afterwards, they choose whether to pay the women or kill them. An early scene in GTA V3 shows sex with a woman on a kitchen counter. Players also visit strip clubs, where they watch, flirt with and fondle female dancers. GTA V also contained a coded minigame described as (even more) “interactive and sexually explicit” which led to its reclassification in the US from M (mature) to AO (adult only).

I’ve heard the argument touted vociferously that there is no link between sexually explicit and violent gaming and sexually explicit and violent language and behaviour. I’ve read plenty of research to the contrary, especially relating to young people whose brains and behaviours are shaped by these and all early experiences. As with most things, context is paramount. Many of the boys I worked with in CAMHS grew up in families where boundaries were fragile at best. Their parents had, after all, sanctioned games which allowed them to simulate sex and violence. Many homes featured domestic abuse and/or trauma and neglect. In order to escape their miserable realities, these children retreated into fantasy worlds of gaming, where they formed identifications with killers, cannibals and abusers of women. Holding a gun in their (virtual) hand while a (virtual) female companion provided sexual gratification, they could press pause on their own intolerable vulnerability and feel omnipotent, while the chaos around them ensued. I remember the chilling response from one ten-year-old when I asked what he liked about the games and he told me, “Swearing and killing whores”. I wonder how your son would answer that question. Maybe ask him. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

And so, to GTA VI, the long-awaited, highly anticipated addition to the $10 billion dollar franchise, which many boys bunked off school to play on the day of its release and many more added to their Christmas list. Of course, it’s more explicit. Of course, the graphics are more realistic. Of course there’s more extreme content. We’re told that high impact violence is “less restrained” than in previous versions and now includes dismemberment and decapitation. Like that’s a selling point. Sex is no longer “implied” or “off camera” but is unequivocally explicit. There’s a mission set in a pornographer’s mansion, a corseted woman hitting golf balls at a man’s testicles and the familiar strip clubs where players can rub the dancer’s breasts and buttocks while inhaling cocaine. Parents are feverishly and obliviously “adding to basket”.

According to recent reports, this is the blatant stuff, but there’s also subliminal messaging which the creators describe as “satirical political parody”. This includes an anti-transgender billboard and a “no paws clause” to keep the state “free of furries”. There are political insults and racial slurs, including reference to the N-word. These are supposedly digs at right wing rhetoric, but I doubt your kid will get the irony.

Recent findings suggest that GTA VI introduces the first female protagonist, demonstrating that women can be as “violent, tough and deeply messed up as the men.”5 And in another move to redress the gender imbalance, male strippers have been recruited to the clubs. An avid fan of GTA described GTA VI as: “absurdly foul-mouthed, filthy and over-the-top offensive in every imaginable way. It does not so much push the boundaries of taste as ignore the existence of taste. It contains every perverted scenario you could imagine.”5 GTA is classified globally as 18+, Mature or Adult Only. As one reviewer stated, “I have never played a game that has more thoroughly earned its 18 rating.”5.

Sources indicate that I’m no prude. I regularly and willingly talk about sex, fetish, drugs and alcohol with young people, if that’s what’s on their mind. I’m often the one advocating for leniency and compromise regarding arbitrary age limits. But I am not on board with parents sanctioning their teens and pre-teens simulating dismemberment and decapitation, sex with prostitutes, killing sex workers and sexually harassing female dancers, while picking up “satirical” discriminatory language about gender, sexual and ethnic diversity. So please, for their sake, and the sake of everyone they are yet to meet, don’t buy GTA VI for your children.
