The conversation you’re avoiding is already costing you

It has been revealed that it’s 2 a.m., and a capable leader is wide awake. In their mind, they’re replaying a difficult conversation that hasn’t happened yet. They’ve been waiting for the “perfect time”: a calmer week, a better mood, the right moment. The irony is that they can approve a major budget without blinking, yet they’ve spent the last few weeks avoiding a five-minute talk. And it’s not because they don’t care. It’s because they care a great deal about the person, the relationship, and the work. The organizational psychologist Liane Davey makes a useful distinction here. Conflict aversion is the natural discomfort most of us feel when a conversation could go badly; conflict avoidance is what happens when we let that discomfort drive our behavior. Avoiding a difficult conversation may buy some relief in the short term but it usually costs far more later on. But something else is going on when leaders avoid difficult conversations. By staying silent, they’re deciding for the other person that they can’t handle it, don’t want to know, or will react badly. What employees actually want Employees do want to know. In a March 2024 survey of 1,500 employees in Canada and the U.S. by the Achievers Workforce Institute, two-thirds said they want to have tough conversations at work. But one-third said they don’t feel safe having them with their manager, and fewer than one-third say they trust their managers. That gap is the real story. People aren’t asking to be protected from hard truths. They’re asking for two things: the truth, and a safe way to hear it. Let’s agree that not every conversation ends in agreement, and it shouldn’t be the goal. Leaders have to make decisions that disappoint people from time to time. It’s part of the job, part of the responsibility to deliver excellence, not the easiest or most popular solution. And the beauty is that people usually accept a no when they’ve been heard and understand how the decision was made. What they struggle with is a no that arrives out of nowhere. Nice isn’t the same as kind I work with leaders and teams to help them get more comfortable with difficult conversations. One of the first things we look at is the difference between being nice and being kind. Protecting everyone from the discomfort of the conversation can feel nice, but it isn’t kind. Kindness means we respect people enough to give them the information they need to make their own choices, even when it costs short-term friction. Imagine an employee who repeatedly misses deadlines and nobody says anything. That’s unkind to the employee who is never given the opportunity to adjust, learn, and protect their reputation. It’s also unkind to their teammates who are watching. Every missed deadline chips away at their trust in their leader, their team, and the organization. And, as their teammate’s poor performance continues, they start asking why they should work hard when good work earns them only the job of doing someone else’s work. Fast-forward eight months. Valuable team members are quitting out of frustration. And the low-performing employee is surprised by a “meets most expectations” performance review, so starts looking for another job. All this happens because no one told one person they were falling short or gave them a chance to adjust. Avoidance happens at the executive level as well. I’ve coached business partners who have come close to losing their company because trust between them slowly eroded. In that case, as in so many, conflict wasn’t the original problem. Conflict was the result of the distrust and frustration that grew because important conversations had been avoided. Build trust before you need it Building trust is the best first step. In the Achievers study, trust in the manager was the key predictor of whether employees felt safe having tough conversations. But safety on its own isn’t the whole answer. Amy Edmondson, the Harvard Business School professor who pioneered research on psychological safety, tells us that psychological safety alone can create a comfort zone. Safety doesn’t mean everyone agrees and it doesn’t protect anyone from disappointment. What safety does mean is that both people believe there’s respect and a purpose worth protecting, and that openness is expected. I find one of the best ways to build trust is for leaders to ask for permission to have difficult conversations long before they are needed. My two favorite moments are during employee onboarding and during team resets. In both situations, you might say, “At some point we will need to have a difficult conversation: a difference of opinion, a mistake, a missed expectation. That’s a usual part of work. I know most of us, me included, are tempted to avoid these moments. Can we agree now that we won’t hide from or avoid those moments, that we’ll reward honesty, respect differing opinions, and respond calmly, and that this goes in every direction, including toward me? I’d like to hear how you feel about that.” Notice that this is a request, not an announcement. It’s a conversation that gives the team a say in how they’ll talk to each other, so when the day comes, you’re not springing a surprise on anyone; you’re keeping an agreement you made together. And you might open with, “We agreed we’d be honest with each other, so I’d like to talk about the client deadline.” Continue earning trust The agreement is only a starting point. From there, everyone needs to keep earning and building trust every day. That means honoring every commitment, protecting confidence, admitting mistakes, and offering help. It also means showing respect, celebrating successes, and recognizing greatness whenever it happens. I can’t promise you’ll never have another anxious 2 a.m. But when trust is in place and expectations are clear, these conversations come with less dread. So what conversation have you been putting off? And what might become possible if you approached it not as conflict, but as an act of respect?
