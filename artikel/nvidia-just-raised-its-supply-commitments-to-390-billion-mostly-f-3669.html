Sources indicate that when Nvidia (NVDA -3.30%) reported on Nov. 17, its fiscal third-quarter revenue had more than doubled from the year before, to a record $115.8 billion. But for memory investors, the more telling number was how much the artificial intelligence (AI) chip leader has agreed to pay its suppliers.

Sources indicate that nvidia's supply commitments rose to $390 billion, up from $279 billion a quarter earlier. And the company stated the increase was "primarily related to the multi-year procurement of memory supply.".

Industry observers note that that's good news for Micron Technology (MU -2.10%), one of the three big makers of the memory chips Nvidia buys. But Micron stock is around $702 as I write, about 42% under the record $1,213.56 it closed at on June 25. (Related: Industry News: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Fa...)

I think the market's underrating what Nvidia's report notes about Micron's business.

Showing how fast its buying has picked up, Nvidia's supply commitments have more than tripled over two quarters. They were at $119 billion as of April 26, climbed to $279 billion by July 26, and hit $390 billion by Oct. 25, the end of its fiscal third quarter. In both of the last two quarters, the firm linked most of the rise to memory. What's more, the money's coming due sooner. Of the $390 billion, $203 billion falls in Nvidia's fiscal 2028, which ends in January 2028. Three months earlier, that year's figure was $87 billion.

In short, Nvidia is locking in memory long before it needs the chips, and much of this spending will probably land within about a year.

Nvidia doesn't say how it splits the spending between its memory suppliers. SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics sell Nvidia high-bandwidth memory (HBM), too. (HBM is the stacked memory that sits next to AI processors.).

Micron doesn't name its largest buyers, either. However, no single customer made up 10% or more of its revenue for its fiscal 2026, which ended Sept. 3, down from one customer at 17% the year before.

So Micron's direct sales to Nvidia, whatever they are, were under a tenth of its revenue last year.

Industry observers note that what Micron does say is encouraging. It's working with Nvidia on what it calls the industry's first custom HBM4E chip, designed for Nvidia's next generation of graphics processing units. (Related: Breaking: How to generate text: using different decodin...)

Sources indicate that and its cloud memory business unit, which sells HBM to all data center customers, keeps growing. It pulled in $16.3 billion of revenue in the three months ended Sept. 3, up 18% from the previous quarter and more than triple the $4.5 billion of a year before, with an 83% gross margin. On its fiscal fourth-quarter call, Micron said it had lifted its HBM prices significantly for calendar 2027.

Nvidia's commitments look like the buyer's side of a change Micron is already building its business around.

In a recent development, micron has now signed 26 multiyear strategic customer agreements, up from 16 a quarter earlier. All are take-or-pay deals, so customers commit to buying fixed volumes and can lose deposits if they fall short.

Customers have committed $32 billion under the deals, mainly as cash deposits, compared to the $22 billion Micron forecast a quarter before.

Recent findings suggest that micron also puts its remaining performance obligations (contracted sales it hasn't recognized yet) at around $150 billion, counting just committed volumes at minimum prices. "[E]ven at floor prices, we expect margins meaningfully above any prior cycle peak margins," chief financial officer Mark Murphy noted in Micron's prepared remarks for the fiscal fourth quarter.

But the stock trades like the boom might end soon. At around $702, Micron's price-to-earnings ratio is about 4 based on analysts' estimates for fiscal 2027 (which ends next September), and only about 9 on fiscal 2026's actual earnings of $74.33 per share. A price like that suggests investors expect profits to dive before long.

Sure, memory prices have crashed before, and Micron aims to spend more on fresh plant space, which might loosen supply in late 2028 and beyond. But a growing share of its sales now comes with committed volumes, deposits, and price floors, protections Micron didn't have in past memory busts.

In a recent development, of the three companies selling Nvidia its memory, Micron is the one I'd buy. It's working with Nvidia on custom HBM, and unlike Samsung, it's a pure memory and storage business. At around 4 times expected earnings, the stock looks cheap to me even if Nvidia's money is split three ways.
