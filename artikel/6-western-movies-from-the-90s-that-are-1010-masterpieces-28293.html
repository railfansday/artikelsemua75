There's nothing quite like a good Western. Although the golden age of the horse opera was undoubtedly the mid-20th century, the genre continued to persevere long afterward. In fact, there was a Western renaissance in the 1990s that took Hollywood — and the Academy Awards — by storm. From genre staples like Clint Eastwood and Kevin Costner to visionary filmmakers like Ang Lee and Richard Donner, the '90s sparked latest life into the Old West.

Whether you prefer comedic takes on the genre, action-packed shoot-'em-ups, or deconstructive takes that raise the Western from the ground up, there's something here for everyone. Outlaws, lawmen, soldiers, and cowboys galore make up some of the greatest thrills of the '90s centered entirely on the American West. Whether you adore these flicks already or have yet to visit them, these are some of the decade's greatest Westerns — so don't sleep on them. Starting this list off with perhaps the most controversial pick, Young Guns II deserves its place in the Western canon. A high-octane sequel to the original 1988 flick, the Emilio Estevez-led Billy the Kid action romp pits the Regulators against the lawman Pat Garrett (William Petersen), who aims to bring Billy and his friends in on several outstanding warrants. The result is an intense cat-and-mouse ride across the Latest Mexican desert, and it's a blast for those who haven't had the pleasure. In a recent development, young Guns II is arguably even greater than its predecessor. Billy, Doc (Kiefer Sutherland), Chavez (Lou Diamond Phillips), and the rest of the Regulators carry the weight of the picture with their trademark charm and "Brat Pack" pull. The fast-pulling sequel does everything it can to surpass the original, armed with a killer Jon Bon Jovi theme and plenty of fabulous performances that deserve high praise. There's a reason that folks are still calling for a third entry after all these years — and long after the fates of the Regulators have already been met.

The Wild West era and the American Civil War are so inexplicably linked that one cannot divorce the Western from the war itself. Director Ang Lee understood this profoundly when tackling Ride with the Devil, a Civil War-era trial by fire that chronicles a group of Missouri Bushwhackers as they battle the Jayhawkers invading their Southern home. Its meditations on the origins of Western violence as an outgrowth from the war itself are quite complex, and the picture pulls no punches as it does so. Tobey Maguire and Skeet Ulrich star as childhood friends Jake Roedel and Jack Bull Chiles seek revenge on those who threatened their home.

Like many Westerns, Ride with the Devil offers a nuanced take on the war that explores the evils of both sides. That said, the Irregulars are portrayed as almost akin to a typical Western outlaw gang. Jake's struggle with the Southern cause, and his growing friendship with former slave Daniel Holt (Jeffrey Wright), is what forges his path as an honorable man. It may not be the first film that comes to mind when you think of the Old West — indeed it's very much rooted in the Civil War itself — but it would be hard to consider the film anything but a Western in style and substance.

Recent findings suggest that based on the popular Western television program of the same name, Richard Donnor brought Bret Maverick (Mel Gibson) to the massive screen with the comedic Maverick. When the titular gambler finds himself in search of the funds to enter a high-stakes poker game, he ventures across the Old West alongside Annabelle Bransford (Jodie Foster) and Marshal Zane Cooper (James Garner), who certainly give him a run for his money. If you loved the show, you'll get a kick out of the big-screen version.

Industry observers note that maverick reminds us, like Young Guns II, that the genre can be fun as much as it is introspective. Gibson plays the unusual Western "hero" with genuine charm and an unassuming con artistry that certainly honors the original series. It's not your typical Western, but that's exactly why it's such a stellar entry. Of course, Maverick turns out to actually be a continuation rather than a straight-up reboot, but the way it reveals such a fact is exactly the sort of slight-of-hand that gives Bret Maverick his edge.

Sources indicate that the first Western to win Best Picture at the Oscars in decades, Dances with Wolves was Kevin Costner's directorial debut that proved the Western could be as epic as ever. With a screenplay by Michael Blake based on his novel of the same name, the film follows Lt. John Dunbar as he is stationed out on the American frontier following his heroic (but secretly suicidal) actions during the Civil War. Caught between the Pawnee and Sioux, Dunbar eventually finds himself on the other end of his own country's barrel.

Sources indicate that dances with Wolves is a breathtaking motion picture. It's an introspective glimpse into both the effects of the Civil War and the lives of the Native American peoples who lived on the frontier long before it was settled. More than that, it was a trailblazer in native representation in Hollywood, and though not a true story, is largely based on historical truths. Beyond that, it's also just a great time, as Costner proves himself to be one of the genre's biggest champions.

In a recent development, is there a more well-known or highly-celebrated '90s Western by audiences and critics alike than Tombstone? The spectacular Kurt Russell-led picture follows former lawman Wyatt Earp, his two brothers, and their friend Doc Holliday (Val Kilmer) as they arrive in the titular Arizona town just in time to square off with the ravenous Cowboys gang. As they do, things go from bad to worse as Tombstone falls into chaos — and the Earps bring hell on their adversaries.

Tombstone does absolutely everything right. It's thrilling, action-packed, and chock-full of some of the best on-screen dialogue in the business. Russell and Kilmer steal the show, but the entire stacked cast more than prove themselves over the course of these fast-moving 130 minutes. It's hard to rank the best performances in Tombstone given how stellar each actor truly is, but we all know that this movie wouldn't work nearly as well without Val Kilmer.

It has been revealed that just two years after Dances with Wolves reminded everyone that the Western still had real life left in it, Unforgiven came in and nabbed that Best Picture Oscar for itself. Directed by Clint Eastwood, the genre star tackles his final on-screen Western as William Munny, an aged former gunslinger who returns for one last job to provide for his family. It's a stellar revisionist picture that does everything right as Eastwood bids the genre that made him a final heartfelt farewell.

In a recent development, it doesn't get much better than Unforgiven. With brilliant performances by Morgan Freeman, Richard Harris, and Gene Hackman, the picture excels in the way it unpacks the genre's most famous tropes and reconstructs them into Eastwood's final dissertation on what makes the Western truly exceptional. There are few Westerns so highly celebrated as Unforgiven, and although Eastwood may best be known for his work on the Dollars Trilogy, there's a reason this won Best Picture.
