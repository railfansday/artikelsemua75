According to recent reports, full Moon occurs at 9:54 A.M. EST, bringing us our second Super Moon of the year. A Super Moon occurs when the Moon reaches Full phase close to perigee, its closest point to Earth, making our satellite appear larger and brighter than average. The November Full Moon is also called the Beaver Moon. (Related: Latest Update: Fine tuning CLIP with Remote Sensing (Sa...)

A few hours earlier, before dawn, the Full Moon passes through the Pleiades star cluster (M45) in Taurus, occulting several of its bright stars. This is an easy event to watch, visible to the naked eye — or you can get a closer view with binoculars or a telescope (just note that you may want a Moon filter if available, as our satellite is so big and bright). The timing of the event will depend heavily on your location; note that it begins right around sunrise along the East Coast, so observers there may only see the Moon approaching the famous group of stars. The 5th-magnitude star Celaeno (16 Tauri) disappears behind the Moon’s leading edge just moments before 6 A.M. CST, followed minutes later by 4th-magnitude Taygeta (19 Tau). Next to vanish is 4th-magnitude Maia (20 Tau) just before 6:20 A.M. CST, followed rapidly by Sterope (21 Tau) a few minutes later. The latter two may be difficult to catch from the Midwest without optical aid, as sunrise is fast approaching. If you are using binoculars or a telescope in this region, make sure to put them away several minutes ahead of sunrise from your location. Celaeno reappears around 6:40 A.M. CST, 15 minutes or so before sunrise.

For specific timings from your location, visit the International Occultation Timing Association at http://www.lunar-occultations.com/bobgraze/index.html.
