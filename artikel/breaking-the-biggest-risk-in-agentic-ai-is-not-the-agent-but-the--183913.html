Breaking: The Biggest Risk in Agentic AI Is Not the Agent But the Business It Doesn’t Understand

OpenAI’s recent disclosure exposes a critical weakness in enterprise AI governance: watching what agents do is not enough if they can also alter the controls designed to keep them accountable. To deploy agents safely at scale, businesses must first understand how their processes really work, writes Guillaume Bacuvier.

OpenAI’s recent disclosure of two incidents in which AI agents interfered with the monitoring systems designed to oversee them raises an uncomfortable question for businesses preparing to deploy autonomous AI. What happens when an agent can influence not only the work it performs, but also the mechanisms intended to keep its behaviour in check?

The immediate response is to strengthen monitoring, improve safeguards and prevent agents from interfering with their own oversight, but while these are necessary steps, they address only part of a larger challenge. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

As AI moves from generating information to executing tasks and making decisions, businesses must reconsider how they govern their operations. And that starts with something surprisingly basic: understanding how their organizations work. (Related: Restaurant owners left in tears after discovering their...)

Industry observers note that in my experience, many of the world’s largest and most sophisticated companies cannot accurately describe how they operate at the level of detail autonomous AI requires. Yes, they have process documentation, operating procedures and compliance frameworks, but ask whether they have a comprehensive, up-to-date understanding of how processes run, who makes decisions, which rules apply and how different activities depend on one another, and the answer is frequently no.

That has always complicated business transformation – with agentic AI, it becomes a significant governance risk.

Industry observers note that the OpenAI incidents expose the limitations of treating AI governance primarily as a monitoring problem. An organization can invest heavily in observing agent behaviour, recording decisions and detecting anomalies. But those measures become less reliable if agents can modify the systems providing oversight or influence the information on which monitoring depends.

This is why the distinction between monitoring and control matters. Monitoring tells you what happened, assuming the information captured is trustworthy. Control establishes what an agent is permitted to do, the boundaries within which it operates and when human intervention is required.

Both are essential, yet neither can function effectively without understanding the business process in which the agent operates.

It has been revealed that consider a financial services organisation introducing agents into customer onboarding. An agent might verify documents, retrieve information and initiate decisions at remarkable speed. But what happens when a customer falls outside standard eligibility criteria? When information conflicts across systems? Or when regulatory requirements differ between jurisdictions?

It has been revealed that the agent needs to understand more than its immediate task: it needs information about the wider process, relevant policies, dependencies and limits of its authority. Without that context, even a highly capable agent can make decisions that appear reasonable in isolation but are inappropriate for the business.

Industry observers note that and responsibility for the consequences remains with the organization deploying it. Technology teams may select and implement AI systems, but accountability for business outcomes generally rests with the executives and process owners responsible for those operations.

Those leaders need confidence that appropriate controls exist before authorising agents to operate at scale.

In a recent development, the fact is enterprise processes rarely operate exactly as documented. Employees develop workarounds, systems evolve, regulations change, and exceptions become embedded in everyday operations. A standardized process can develop numerous variations across departments, countries and business units.

Industry observers note that in a large multinational, these variations can number in the tens of thousands, involving different responsibilities, systems, rules and dependencies. The challenge is not simply documenting this complexity, rather it is maintaining an accurate understanding as the organisation changes.

Historically, detailed process knowledge was often considered desirable rather than essential, but AI fundamentally changes that calculation. Experienced employees recognize when documented procedures don’t reflect reality – they understand unwritten rules, know whom to consult and exercise judgement when unexpected situations arise.

An agent cannot be expected to possess that institutional knowledge unless the organization makes it available in a form the agent can understand and use. This is the importance of process context: a connected, machine-readable understanding of how work is performed, including the relationships between processes, people, systems, data, policies and controls.

It also means reconciling how work is designed to happen with how it actually happens. Process models establish intended structures and rules, while execution intelligence reveals deviations, bottlenecks and variations.

Recent findings suggest that together, these perspectives provide the foundation for a digital twin of the enterprise, helping organisations understand the implications of change before introducing autonomous systems into live workflows. This matters for governance, but also for business performance.

Too many companies approach AI as a series of opportunities to automate individual tasks. An agent might process invoices ten times faster, but if the downstream payment process remains unchanged, the overall productivity improvement could be negligible.

Improving one activity without understanding its dependencies can simply move a bottleneck elsewhere. Meaningful returns from AI will require companies to redesign how their operations work, not simply deploy increasingly capable agents.

This raises a question that should be central to every enterprise AI strategy: which processes are genuinely ready for autonomous execution? The temptation is to start with tasks that appear easiest to automate or promise immediate productivity gains. But technical feasibility is only one consideration.

Business leaders must understand the underlying process, its dependencies and operating rules. They must establish which decisions can safely be delegated, which require human approval and what happens when an agent encounters an exception.

Recent findings suggest that independent monitoring, reliable audit trails and mechanisms for intervention are equally crucial. An agent should never be able to disable or alter the controls governing its own behaviour – there must also be a credible business case considering the entire process rather than the efficiency of an isolated task.

Responsibility for these decisions cannot sit with technology teams alone. CIOs and CTOs establish secure architectures and safeguards, but business leaders must define the outcomes, responsibilities and controls, working alongside risk and compliance functions. Ultimately, AI governance is a business leadership responsibility, supported by technology.

Having witnessed several major technology revolutions over the past three decades, one pattern is familiar: we tend to overestimate their immediate impact and underestimate how profoundly they eventually change businesses – I suspect AI will follow a similar path.

Pressure to demonstrate progress has encouraged a proliferation of pilots, chatbots and automation projects. Some deliver useful results, but many organisations are discovering that deploying AI technology is considerably easier than transforming the business around it. Real-world operations involve unexpected events, human judgement, regulatory obligations and countless dependencies that cannot simply be wished away through better algorithms.

Industry observers note that for the foreseeable future, successful enterprises will enable people and AI agents to work together effectively rather than assume autonomous systems can run the organization independently. Achieving this will require process redesign, fresh operating models, stronger governance and a willingness to confront organisational complexity rather than automate around it.

The rewards will be substantial, but the winners will not necessarily be those deploying the most agents or moving fastest in the short term. They will be those building the operational understanding and governance needed to deploy AI confidently across their core processes.

Recent findings suggest that openAI’s disclosure is a timely reminder that autonomous systems require more than sophisticated monitoring – they need clear boundaries, independent oversight and a deep understanding of their operating environment. Before businesses can trust AI agents to make decisions on their behalf, they must first understand – and take control of – how their own organizations work.
