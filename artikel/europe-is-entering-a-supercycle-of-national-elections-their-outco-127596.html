Europe is entering a ‘supercycle’ of national elections. Their outcomes could shake the EU’s foundations

As nationalist, Eurosceptic parties surge in four of the bloc’s biggest member states, the coming year’s polls could test its resilience like never before.

In late 1998, Tony Blair was prime minister of Britain, Lionel Jospin was prime minister of France, and Gerhard Schröder had just been elected chancellor of Germany. The EU had 15 member states and pro-European, centre-left parties were in power – either alone or in coalitions – in all but two of them.

In a recent development, brisk economic growth was lifting living standards. Eastward enlargement was under way. In Washington, Bill Clinton was pursuing the same brand of “third way” centrism as a middle path between free-market capitalism and traditional socialism. Not quite three decades later, the continent’s politics once again seem to be tilting in a single direction – except this time, the momentum belongs to the nationalist far right, and the outcome could be a significant unpicking of the postwar European consensus that built the EU.

Between the end of this month and the end of next year, an extraordinary electoral sequence will sweep the bloc. Voters in four of the EU’s five biggest member states, by population and economy, head to the polls, with parliamentary elections also due in Estonia, Finland, Greece and Slovakia, and regional ballots in Germany. Spain kicks off the cycle with a snap general election on 29 November, with prime minister Pedro Sánchez’s socialist coalition facing probable defeat to rightwing parties in the shape of the conservative People’s party (PP) and far-right Vox, which already govern together in some Spanish regions.

Next come presidential and parliamentary elections in France in the spring. The clear frontrunner in the presidential race is Marine Le Pen of far-right the National Rally (RN), well ahead of the far-left firebrand Jean-Luc Mélenchon and a hopelessly divided mainstream opposition.

In a recent development, when Poland votes later next year, nationalist parties that between them command nearly half the national vote could well unseat the liberal coalition of prime minister, Donald Tusk, despite recent splits. And in Italy, Giorgia Meloni, whose Brothers of Italy party has far-right roots, hopes to secure a second term in elections due before the end of 2027 – while facing pressure on her right flank from the upstart National Future party.

According to recent reports, paradoxically, this nationalist surge arrives amid strong public support for the EU. Shocked by the chaos of Brexit and seeking reassurance in an increasingly alarming world, a record 74% of Europeans say their country benefits from membership, and very few Eurosceptic parties now advocate leaving outright.

It has been revealed that the Eurosceptics and nationalists do, however, still view the bloc as a top-down project that undermines national sovereignty, and aim to transform it into an altogether looser association of sovereign nation-states. In the words of the Italian political scientist Natalie Tocci, that means this coming electoral supercycle could “dramatically change the face of Europe” – triggering not simply “a slow erosion of the European project but a hollowing out, if not an outright implosion”.

Industry observers note that all elections differ and national specifics loom large. But analysts agree the underlying forces at work here are connected: sluggish growth, ageing populations, strained public finances and a spiralling cost of living have chipped away at support for Europe’s traditional catch-all centre-left and centre-right parties. The wave of youth-led protests sweeping Europe is only the most visible sign of deep voter discontent.

Meanwhile, nationalist parties have become increasingly adept at conjuring and exploiting an ever-evolving list of supposed threats and crises, from migration and vaccines to the green transition, support for Ukraine, globalisation, international elites and supranational institutions – narratives bolstered by Russian disinformation and overt backing for “patriotic” parties from Donald Trump’s White House. As a result, politics has fragmented, and polarised: nearly one in four voters now backs far-right parties – a share that has grown almost fivefold since the mid-1990s.

The rise has not been linear: over the past year or so, while the far-right Alternative für Deutschland scored a historic regional election victory in eastern Germany, in the Netherlands Geert Wilders’s Freedom party lost nearly a third of its seats, Viktor Orbán’s Fidesz was soundly beaten in Hungary and the far-right Sweden Democrats shed support for the first time in 16 years.

But the underlying upward trend appears clear – fuelled above all, political scientists argue, by the normalisation and mainstreaming of far-right ideas, most notably on immigration. Centre-right parties are gradually dismantling “firewalls” that once barred cooperation with the radical right. At the European level, the centre-right European People’s party has increasingly turned to farther-right groups to roll back parts of the European green deal and tighten migration rules.

In a recent development, for the EU, the biggest impact of this election supercycle could be in the European Council of national leaders, which now features just three centre-left heads of government out of 27. If Eurosceptic leaders in France, Spain and Poland join those of Czechia, Italy, Romania, Slovakia and Slovenia by the end of 2027, they would not have the numbers to rewrite EU law – but they would constitute a formidable blocking minority.

EU budget negotiations, future enlargement, aid to Ukraine and deeper integration in essential fields such as defence, energy, finance and technology could all face paralysis, analysts warn.

Safety in numbers, notes Tocci, could change current leaders’ behaviour in the council. Meloni’s relative restraint has been dictated, in part, by her relative isolation among mainstream peers such as Tusk, Sánchez and France’s Emmanuel Macron (alongside Rome’s reliance on €200bn in EU recovery funds).

If she were instead sitting down with the likes of Le Pen, Poland’s far-right Law and Justice (PiS) leader, Jarosław Kaczyński, and a Vox-backed Spanish government, with National Future’s hardline Roberto Vannacci breathing down her neck at home and those EU funds winding down, she might have less incentive to play pro-EU ball.

Sources indicate that analysts agree, though, that the decisive battleground is France. Le Pen’s election platform sets her on a collision course with Brussels: she aims to cut €11bn from France’s EU budget contribution, hold a constitutional referendum to proclaim the supremacy of French over European law, and breach various single-market rules.

In a recent development, and France “carries very significant influence over the EU”, noted Mujtaba Rahman of the Eurasia Group consultancy. Compared with Orbán, he notes, a president Le Pen would have “far more leverage to carry out the far-right playbook of subverting the EU’s law-based, supranational architecture”.

It has been revealed that until now, Brussels has contained disruptors such as Orbán largely because there were rarely more than two or three round the table. A cohesive Eurosceptic bloc would leave the EU unable to enforce rule-of-law standards, further integrate economic and security policies or, indeed, agree on anything much at all. The first victim would very probably be Ukraine, with Le Pen leading a blocking caucus against further aid.

Three decades on from 1998, Europe’s coming elections, concluded Stefan Lehne of the Carnegie Europe thinktank, could well turn into “key tests of the EU’s resilience”. The threat to the bloc might not be immediate or existential, he said, but the medium-term risks were significant.

Sources indicate that “A stronger role for the radical right may be unlikely to dismantle the EU as it exists today,” Lehne said, but could “prevent it from becoming what it needs to be to deal with the challenges of the future”. And gradually but inevitably, prolonged failure to act collectively as and where needed would lead a “loss of relevance”.
