Swordfish hunt about 2,000 feet down, but every so often one plunges as deep as a mile, and scientists suspect it is reading the ocean

Satellite tags on 34 North Atlantic swordfish caught rare dives past 3,280 feet. They were short and came near eddies and fronts, hinting the fish check the ocean.

Sources indicate that swordfish spend their days hunting in the dark, about 2,000 feet (600 meters) below the surface. Every so often, one keeps going.

It has been revealed that satellite tags on North Atlantic swordfish have caught these rare plunges past 3,280 feet (1,000 meters). Nobody knows why the fish make them.

A team from the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI) has now looked at years of tagging data in search of an answer. The dives were short, fairly steady, and most common where surface temperatures varied and the sea surface stood higher. The authors think the fish may be sampling the water column, possibly to help them find their way. The study was published in Marine Biology.

In a recent development, swordfish live in open oceans around the world, from about 45 degrees north to 45 degrees south. They follow a daily rhythm. By day they stay deep, usually between about 1,300 and 2,300 feet (400 and 700 meters), and at night they rise toward the surface. Recent findings suggest that that routine follows their food. Huge numbers of small animals live in the ocean’s twilight zone and move up and down each day, and swordfish track them. Like other large predators of the open sea, tuna and swordfish get much of their food from that dim layer.

Sources indicate that swordfish can handle water from about 36 to 88 degrees Fahrenheit (2 to 31 degrees Celsius). They keep their eyes and parts of their body warmer than the surrounding water, which helps them stay deep for long periods.

It has been revealed that the researchers combined data from 34 pop-up satellite tags placed on North Atlantic swordfish between 2003 and 2024. Twenty-three were tagged off the Atlantic coast of Florida, six near the Grand Banks off Canada, three in the southern Sargasso Sea and two in the Gulf of America, also known as the Gulf of Mexico. Twenty-four were probably adults and 10 were probably juveniles. (Related: AssetOpsBench: Bridging the Gap Between AI Agent Benchm...)

Industry observers note that the tags stayed on for one to six months and logged depth, temperature and light. Together, they covered 3,666 days of swordfish life.

The data came from a partnership with NOAA’s Southeast Fisheries Science Center and the Massachusetts Division of Marine Fisheries.

“It wouldn’t have been possible to analyze this from just a single tagging operation, or from a single lab,” said Simon Thorrold, a fish ecologist at WHOI and an author of the study.

In a recent development, the team flagged 39 days on which a fish went unusually deep for its own normal pattern. On 25 of those days, recorded by 10 tags, the data were detailed enough to confirm a distinct extreme dive.

Recent findings suggest that these dives averaged about 3,250 feet (991 meters) at their deepest point, and the deepest reached roughly 5,460 feet (1,664 meters), more than a mile down. On ordinary days, the fish’s deepest point averaged about 2,090 feet (637 meters).

According to recent reports, A single extreme dive lasted about 45 to 55 minutes on average. When a fish made one such dive in a day, it usually dropped two to three times faster than it climbed back. In one fish’s full-resolution record, it spent between a few seconds and about six minutes at the bottom.

Recent findings suggest that the water at the bottom of these dives averaged about 47 degrees Fahrenheit (8.4 degrees Celsius). On other days, the coldest water the fish met averaged about 54 degrees Fahrenheit (12.1 degrees Celsius).

Most dives started and ended in the twilight zone, during daylight. In 12 of 16 such dives, the fish came back to within about 33 feet (10 meters) of the depth where it began.

The authors weighed seven possible reasons for deep dives, including feeding, escaping predators, saving energy and navigation.

According to recent reports, feeding seemed unlikely. A hunting fish would be expected to linger at depth, but these fish barely paused at the bottom before heading back up. The dives also did not cluster at dawn and dusk, when prey is easiest to reach.

It has been revealed that escape did not fit well either. The descents were steady, about 3.3 feet (1 meter) per second, and the fish behaved normally afterward. Only one dive began and ended near the surface at night, when the authors think predators may be more likely to be around. The authors could not rule out that the two juveniles, which made several dives in a single day, were avoiding predators.

It has been revealed that extreme dives were much more likely where sea surface temperature varied a lot and where the sea surface stood higher. In the team’s model, each extra degree Celsius of temperature variation made a dive about 23 times more likely. These conditions mark swirling eddies, fronts and major currents such as the Gulf Stream.

According to recent reports, the authors suggest that crossing into a new water mass may prompt a swordfish to check what lies below. Short, repeatable dives of the same shape would fit that kind of sampling.

“They could be coming into an ocean weather system, like an eddy or a frontal system, where we have a change in water masses,” mentioned Ciara Willis, who led the study as a doctoral student in the MIT-WHOI Joint Program and now works at the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute.

Deeper water could also give a clearer reading of Earth’s magnetic field, which many fish can sense, away from the busy, changing layers near the surface. Some dives happened near seamounts, ridges and magnetic anomalies, but those links were weak, though rough seafloor indicated a smaller but significant effect. Other ocean travelers are under similar suspicion, as oceanic manta rays also dive deep, apparently not to feed.

It has been revealed that the authors stress that their conclusions are inferences. Satellite tag positions carry an average error of about 60 to 90 miles (100 to 150 kilometers), and possibly more for deep divers like swordfish. That makes it hard to match a dive to a specific seafloor feature.

It is still unclear how common these dives are among swordfish, and whether they differ between populations or ages.

Swordfish support valuable fisheries, so knowing what steers them could help predict where they go as the ocean changes.

The information was obtained from the study published in Marine Biology and from the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution.
