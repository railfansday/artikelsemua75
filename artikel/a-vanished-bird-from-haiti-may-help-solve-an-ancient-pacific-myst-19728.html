A vanished bird from Haiti may help solve an ancient Pacific mystery

DNA reveals how snipes and woodcocks reached distant islands, stopped migrating, changed physically, and became vulnerable to extinction.

The sandpiper family includes some of the most extreme long-distance migrants among birds.

Two branches of that family, the snipes and the woodcocks, have behaved quite differently. These birds reached islands again and again, and migration was then abandoned. (Related: Hawaiian Interisland Awards — Hop Between Islands...)

Recent findings suggest that the results suggest that island settlement was accompanied by changes in body size and flying ability. Island species later proved vulnerable when people arrived with rats and cats.

Industry observers note that the work was led by Min Zhao, then a postdoctoral researcher at the Florida Museum of Natural History. Colleagues at Virginia Tech and the Field Museum in Chicago also took part.

According to recent reports, according to Zhao, the whole project grew out of one extinct bird from Hispaniola. That Caribbean island is shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic.

The dates reported in the study were estimated from family trees built with DNA. The uncertainty attached to them can run to millions of years.

Florida Museum researchers dug the bird’s bones out of a cave in Haiti in 1984.

In 2015, those bones were described by two scientists as a latest species of woodcock. Woodcocks are long-billed birds that probe soft soil for food.

Recent findings suggest that one of the two scientists was David Steadman, who is now an emeritus curator at the museum. He is also listed as a co-author of the new study.

Recent findings suggest that zhao’s team extracted DNA from two of the fossil specimens. The researchers then read the mitochondrial genome, a small set of genes passed from mother to offspring.

Both specimens were placed with the snipes, which form the sister group of the woodcocks. Their closest living relative turned out to be the giant snipe of lowland South America.

Sources indicate that the analysis suggested a South American origin for the Haitian bird’s ancestors. The two lineages are thought to have split between 5 and 12 million years ago.

Why, then, did the bones resemble those of a woodcock? The authors think the bird’s build may have been altered by island living, which could explain the mix-up.

It has been revealed that the outer wing bone of the Haitian bird was unusually short when compared with its upper wing bone. The authors wrote that it could fly but probably not well.

The team sequenced 45 samples in total. Two were the Haitian fossils and 19 were toe pads taken from old museum specimens. The remaining 24 were fresh tissue samples.

All but one of the 29 living species of snipes and woodcocks were covered by these samples. Specimens were supplied by ten museums and research collections.

A family tree was built from the data, and both groups were traced back to Eurasia.

The ancestors of today’s American birds crossed from the Eastern Hemisphere to the Western Hemisphere four separate times.

Industry observers note that one of those crossings took place roughly 20 million years ago. It was then that the ancestor of the American woodcock reached North America.

The researchers could not tell whether the bird arrived across the Atlantic or by way of Alaska.

According to recent reports, the Austral snipes are found on the outlying islands of New Zealand. Scientists have long placed these small brown birds in a group of their own.

Industry observers note that some snipes from Eurasia spend each winter in Australia and on nearby islands. The DNA showed that those visitors are not close relatives of the Austral snipes.

Instead, the Austral snipes share a branch with several large South American snipes and the extinct Haitian bird.

Two DNA datasets placed the split between the Austral snipes and their relatives at about 14 million and nearly 18 million years ago, respectively.

It has been revealed that the southern continents had broken apart long before either estimate. The snipes’ ancestor most likely crossed the Pacific.

In a recent development, how the crossing happened remains an open question. The authors suggested that birds straying far outside their normal range may have played a role. Such straying was perhaps caused by extreme weather.

Sources indicate that the authors also raised a route through Antarctica. Patches of treeless tundra are thought to have lasted there until at least 12.8 million years ago.

Several living snipe species still breed on tundra in the Arctic.

The analysis indicated that the ancestors of both snipes and woodcocks were migrants. Their island descendants are not.

Ten of the 29 living species are found only on islands, and none of those ten migrates.

It has been revealed that the habit was lost several times. Woodcocks gave it up when they settled the Malay Archipelago in Southeast Asia. Snipes did the same when they settled South America and Madagascar.

Body size changed as well, although not in a single direction.

It has been revealed that the Amami woodcock of southern Japan is about 45% heavier than the Eurasian woodcock, its closest mainland relative. Madagascar’s snipe is about 91% heavier than the common snipe of Eurasia.

Sources indicate that the opposite change is seen in the Austral snipes. Their average weight is 3.4 ounces (96 grams), which is about the weight of a deck of cards.

Recent findings suggest that south American snipes on the same branch average 8.6 ounces (243.5 grams). That is more than twice as much.

In a recent development, the smaller size may be explained by scarce food, the authors suggested. Competition from kiwis and other birds that probe the soil for worms was also considered possible.

It has been revealed that ten species of snipes and woodcocks are known to have died out in the recent geologic past. Nine of them lived on islands.

Sources indicate that the Austral snipes suffered the heaviest losses. Three of nine known species survive. All three are ranked as Vulnerable or Near Threatened by the IUCN Red List.

The South Island snipe died out in 1964, and introduced black rats are regarded as the likely cause.

Recent findings suggest that no resident snipe or woodcock is left in the Caribbean. At least three species have been lost there.

Sources indicate that the youngest dated bones of the Haitian bird are about 600 years old. Rats and feral cats brought by people are suspected of causing its extinction.

Snipes nest on the ground, where they can be reached by those predators. Island species had given up migration, and in some cases strong flight as well.

The authors wrote that these changes made such species especially vulnerable to human impacts.

Overhunting was named as one of those impacts.

Jessica Oswald, a co-author, also worked on the study at the Florida Museum.

In a recent development, “People will eat anything,” said Oswald. She said a crew of hungry explorers or whalers could wipe out an island’s birds in a single weekend.

Island woodcocks in Japan and Southeast Asia appear to have fared better. They live in mountain forests, which may be harder for people to reach.

Latest Zealand may have been reached first by the Austral snipes.

It has been revealed that from there they could have spread to Norfolk Island, Latest Caledonia and Fiji. The opposite path cannot be ruled out, because no genetic data exist for those islands’ extinct snipes.

In a recent development, more snipe species may once have lived in the Pacific and are now extinct. In that case, today’s birds would be the last survivors of a larger group. Direct fossil evidence of that link across the Pacific is still missing.

The Haitian bird’s place on the tree rests on mitochondrial genes from two specimens. A possible close relative from Puerto Rico is also extinct and has not been tested genetically.

The authors suggest that DNA from fossils yet to be found on Pacific islands could help trace how snipes spread across the region.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
