Gen X’s $2.3 trillion caregiving curse: I’m the sister from John Mulaney’s SNL skit and I did the math

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, on Saturday Night Live this weekend, John Mulaney sold America its oldest elder-care product. “Sister,” his pitchman explains, is a live-in, 24/7 caregiver who is “completely free, and also your sister.” Mikey Day’s Dr. Grant, author of Not It! A Son’s Guide to Elder Care, explains how it works: years of internalized sexism have convinced her that caring for Mom and Dad is her job and somehow not yours. The best part, another brother adds, is that the parents drew up the will before she moved in, so you still get half the house she’s deep cleaning. Within a day, the sketch had 2 million views on YouTube, and women all over the country were sending it to their brothers.

The joke lands because it isn’t one, particularly the part about finances. It’s impossible to create or maintain wealth while caregiving. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

It has been revealed that earlier this month, AARP released a report, built on the Urban Institute’s long-range income model, estimating that the typical working caregiver will lose $120,500 over her lifetime in wages, workplace retirement income and Social Security. Across a single generation of caregivers, those born in the early 1980s, the losses come to $2.3 trillion. And that study counts only caregivers who kept their jobs. The sister in the sketch, the one who moved in, isn’t in that number. Her losses are larger because the damage doesn’t end when the caregiving does. Social Security calculates benefits from a worker’s 35 highest-earning years, and every year without a paycheck goes into that average as a zero. A woman who leaves work for five years to care for a parent doesn’t just lose five years of income. She locks in a smaller check for the rest of her life, long after the person she cared for has died. (Related: How to Budget for a Wedding Without Going Broke...)

In 2024, America’s 59 million family caregivers provided 49.5 billion hours of care, work the AARP Public Policy Institute values at $1.01 trillion, more than total Medicaid spending that year. Three in five of those caregivers are women. But the trillion-dollar figure measures only what the care is worth. It says nothing about what the care costs the person giving it, or where that money ends up. The will joke does. It is the most precise line of economic analysis anyone has delivered on caregiving this year: she does the work, and the family’s wealth is split as if she hadn’t.

Meanwhile, she is spending down whatever she had. AARP’s 2026 out-of-pocket study, released in October, found that caregivers now spend an average of $11,508 a year on caregiving, nearly 60 percent more than in 2021 and about 31 percent of household income. Caregivers who retired earlier than planned spend more, $18,940 a year. Most of it goes to housing, food and medical bills: the mortgage on the parents’ house, the groceries, the copays. Research cited by the TIAA Institute found that nearly a quarter of caregivers have less than $1,000 saved for retirement. Caregiving doesn’t just stop a woman from building wealth. It eats the wealth she already had.

Her lost wages are a leave policy her employer never had to fund. Her unpaid hours are home-health services the state never had to buy. Her savings pay down the expenses of a household that isn’t legally hers. And her labor keeps her parents out of a nursing home, which keeps their house and savings from being spent on one. That intact estate is then divided, often equally, among children who did not do the work. The brothers in the sketch aren’t only getting their weekends back. They’re getting their raises, their 401(k) matches, their full Social Security records, and half the house.

According to recent reports, this is why caregiving is so rarely described as a financial arrangement, and so often described as love. Calling it love settles the accounts in advance. No one owes her anything, because what she gave was a gift. Employers that face no leave costs, governments that avoid paying for home care, and siblings who get back to “my life and the stuff I like to do” all benefit from keeping it that way. The rhetoric of priceless care conveniently treats it as worthless.

Some of the fixes are already written. The Social Security Caregiver Credit Act, reintroduced in April by Connecticut Sen. Chris Murphy and Upcoming York Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, would credit caregivers who provide at least 80 hours of care a month with up to five years of deemed earnings, so that those years stop counting as zeros. Congress should pass it, and extend it. Caregiving should earn retirement contributions, not just retirement credits. States can pay family caregivers through Medicaid at real wages, not token stipends. And families can do what the law doesn’t require: put the caregiving into the will. A sibling who gave years of labor to keep the estate intact has contributed to it, and an even split that ignores that contribution isn’t fair. It’s a transfer from her to everyone else who retires on full pensions and she retires on a three-digit Social Security check, in an apartment, while the house she cleaned for years is sold and split down the middle.
