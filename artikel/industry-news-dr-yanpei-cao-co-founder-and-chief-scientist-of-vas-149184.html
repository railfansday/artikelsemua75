According to recent reports, dr. Yanpei Cao is Co-Founder and Chief Scientist of VAST, where he leads research and technical direction for generative 3D and interactive world models. He holds a PhD from Tsinghua University and has worked across computer graphics, 3D reconstruction, and immersive content creation. Before VAST, he served as a principal researcher at Tencent and co-founded volumetric capture company Owlii, which was later acquired by Kuaishou.

In a recent development, VAST develops 3D foundation models and world models and is the firm behind Tripo, a platform for generating and editing 3D assets from text and images. Its work spans mesh geometry, topology, texturing, rigging, and animation, alongside Project Eden and real-time generative rendering through EdenLive. In this interview, Cao discusses native quad meshes, production workflows, the remaining challenges in generative 3D, and the research needed to build persistent interactive worlds. Before co-founding VAST and developing Tripo, you spent years researching 3D reconstruction and computer graphics, and immersive content at organizations including Tencent, Owlii, and Tsinghua University. What inspired you to make the leap from research to entrepreneurship, and what was your original vision for Tripo when you founded the tech giant in 2023?

In fact, after finishing my PhD at Tsinghua University, I went on to co-found Owlii to focus on volumetric capture. I co-founded the tech giant because there weren’t any around doing that type of 3D technology research. The company was later acquired by Kuaishou.

Sources indicate that to me entrepreneurship is never about leaving research behind. Rather, it is a continuation of the research I have been doing since college years.

In 2023, we founded VAST, the manufacturer behind Tripo AI, with the vision of democratizing 3D creation and building interactive worlds. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Recent findings suggest that as the Co-founder and Chief Scientist of Tripo AI, I am deeply involved in our core research, technical direction, and key technological decisions. Building a organization allows me to take fundamental research beyond the lab, translate technological breakthroughs into real products, and make advanced generative 3D capabilities accessible to a broader audience.

Since the company’s founding, we have developed powerful 3D-native foundation models, and we are working on our world model Project Eden, which released a preview in May and EdenLive, a real-time generative rendering, earlier this month.

Recent findings suggest that three years on, I’m happy to see that our 3D-native foundation models, including the P and H series, have been widely adopted in 3D content generation in gaming, film production, education, industrial design and much more. We have effectively lowered the bar for 3D creation. More importantly, our 3D-native foundation models have freed 3D professionals from the tedious work of 3D creation and help improve productivity of the industry.

And with the release of EdenLive as part of our world model, Project Eden, we are getting ever closer to realizing our vision of building interactive worlds.

Tripo recently introduced P2.0, positioning it as a major step toward production-ready AI-generated 3D assets. What are the most significant improvements over previous generations, and how does this release address the limitations that have historically prevented AI-generated models from being used directly in professional workflows?

Sources indicate that topology has been the barrier between AI-generated 3D and real production: game and film pipelines are built on quad meshes. Previously AI got there only through slow, unstable retopology. P2.0 removes that barrier by promptly generating clean quad meshes natively, with clean part separation and the edge flow an artist would lay out manually. Quad mesh topology also makes 3D assets easier to edit and deform, making them better suited for downstream production workflows.

It has been revealed that tripo P2.0 automatically separates body, clothing, and accessories into independent pieces during generation. This matters for game pipelines where characters need separated meshes for material assignment, level of detail (LOD) management, or modular assembly.

It supports a much broader polygon range: from 500 to 50,000 triangles, or up to 25,000 quads. This allows creators to generate assets for different production budgets—from lightweight mobile content to high-fidelity game hero assets.

It supports Mesh Edit, which lets creators select and regenerate any region of a mesh without altering the rest of the asset.

In a recent development, one of P2.0’s most notable advancements is native quad topology generation, which produces structured meshes suitable for editing, rigging, and animation. Why has generating clean, usable topology been such a difficult challenge for generative AI, and what technical breakthroughs made this possible?

The fundamental difficulty lies in how 3D geometry is represented. Many earlier generative approaches relied on autoregressive methods, representing a mesh as a sequence of tokens and generating those tokens one after another. However, unlike language, a 3D mesh does not have a natural linear order. Its vertices, edges, and faces form a connected spatial structure. When that structure is converted into an artificial sequence, generation becomes increasingly difficult as geometric complexity grows, and errors can accumulate.

To overcome the challenge, we developed a novel framework, Nexus, which uses diffusion processes for both geometry and topology.

Recent findings suggest that the Tripo P Series is based on Nexus. Our approach encodes discrete 3D topology into a continuous, diffusable representation, allowing the model to generate geometry through a representation better aligned with the spatial nature of 3D objects. We introduced this direction with Tripo P1.0 in March 2026, enabling structured, clean-topology mesh generation in seconds. As a major step-up from P1.0, P2.0 generates production-ready native quad mesh, a first for the industry.

Industry observers note that P2.0 introduces Mesh Edit, allowing creators to regenerate specific regions of a 3D model without affecting the rest, alongside the ability to generate multiple variants with different polygon counts. How do these capabilities change the creative workflow, particularly for game developers and professional 3D artists who need precise control over their assets?

Recent findings suggest that for professional creators, generating an asset is only the beginning. The ability to refine and control it is equally important. Edit Mesh in Tripo Smart Mesh P2.0 allows consumers to select and regenerate specific regions with defects, rather than regenerating the entire asset and risking the parts that already work. This localized editing saves time during iteration, though the repaired areas still require inspection.

Polygon-count control gives creators the flexibility to balance geometric detail with performance requirements. High-detail assets may need more polygons, while real-time applications such as games often require lighter meshes.

Together with native quad topology, Smart UV, and Auto Rig, these capabilities make generated assets easier to edit, deform, and integrate into downstream production workflows, reducing repetitive work and giving creators greater control over the final result.

Tripo P2.0 supports multi-view image inputs and can automatically separate elements such as clothing, accessories, and character bodies into independent components. How crucial is this structural understanding to producing genuinely usable 3D assets, and what challenges remain in teaching AI to understand the geometry and relationships between objects?

According to recent reports, structural understanding is essential for usable 3D assets. Tripo P2.0 addresses this through multi-view image inputs for more accurate geometry reconstruction and automatic separation of components such as clothing, accessories, and character bodies.

Industry observers note that the remaining challenges include accurately inferring how parts fit together, distinguishing meaningful components from arbitrary geometric divisions, and maintaining consistent topology across different shapes and levels of complexity. P2.0 provides solutions to some of these challenges, and we continue to improve our models to make topology more consistent and assets easier to edit, deform, and use in downstream production.

In a recent development, the industry has made enormous progress in generating visually impressive 3D models, but production environments demand much more than appearance alone. How does Tripo evaluate whether an AI-generated asset is truly production-ready, and what technical barriers still need to be overcome before AI-generated assets can consistently match professionally handcrafted models?

Tripo evaluates production readiness not just by visual quality, but by how easily an asset can be edited, refined, and animated for downstream use. P2.0 addresses this through low-poly mesh generation with clean quad topology and logical edge flow, making assets easier to modify and better suited for rigging and animation.

The remaining challenge is generating consistently clean geometry across diverse shapes and levels of complexity, while preserving fine details and ensuring reliable deformation. The goal is to reduce the manual cleanup and retopology still needed to bring AI-generated assets into professional production pipelines.

It has been revealed that your research has explored areas ranging from 3D reconstruction and neural rendering to automated rigging and generative geometry. How are these different research disciplines coming together within Tripo to create a more complete, AI-driven 3D production pipeline, and which parts of that pipeline remain the most difficult to automate?

In a recent development, currently, Tripo’s research brings together several disciplines to advance 3D generation, including diffusion-based generation, native mesh geometry and topology, texturing, rigging, and animation. The H and P model series address complementary needs: high-fidelity geometry and detail, and production-oriented assets that are easier to edit, refine, and deform. Further research explores how to make generated assets more consistent, structurally reliable, and useful in downstream workflows.

Sources indicate that project Eden takes this further, moving from individual assets to interactive worlds. It separates the evolving state of a world from its visual rendering, while EdenLive uses real-time generative rendering to turn reference inputs, such as graybox scenes, into detailed visuals.

The challenge is to move from generating individual 3D assets to understanding and maintaining coherent, persistent worlds. This is something we are actively working on. Ultimately, our goal is to develop AI that can not only create 3D content, but also understand and sustain the worlds in which that content exists.

Beyond individual 3D assets, VAST is expanding into world models through Project Eden, which aims to create persistent, interactive environments that can be shared by humans and AI agents. What motivated this expansion, and how does developing a world model fundamentally differ from generating individual 3D objects or visually convincing environments?

I wouldn’t describe Project Eden as an expansion into a latest research area. Along with democratizing 3D creation, building interactive worlds has been part of our research vision since day one.

Sources indicate that unlike 3D asset generation or conventional video generation’s approach of predicting visual frames, a world model must understand how an environment is structured and how it evolves through interactions. Project Eden addresses this by separating the evolution of the world state from its visual rendering, allowing the world to persist and evolve independently of how it is presented.

Sources indicate that edenLive, which we just launched, marks a major milestone in Project Eden’s development, bringing real-time generative rendering to interactive experiences. The next challenge is to build coherent, persistent worlds that both humans and AI agents can interact with.

Sources indicate that project Eden emphasizes persistent world states, physical consistency, and interactions between multiple agents. What are the biggest technical challenges involved in building AI systems that can understand how a virtual world behaves rather than simply predict what it should look like, and what potential applications do you envision beyond gaming and entertainment?

Industry observers note that the central challenge is separating how a world behaves from how it looks. Project Eden addresses this by maintaining an evolving, structured world state independent of visual rendering. This allows the world to persist beyond the camera’s view and remain consistent as interactions unfold. EdenLive is an important step toward this goal, bringing real-time generative rendering to interactive experiences.

In a recent development, beyond gaming and entertainment, this approach can be applied to embodied AI and robotics, where agents learn to navigate and interact with environments; industrial simulation and digital twins, for testing equipment and operational scenarios; architecture and urban planning, for exploring and evaluating designs; training and education, through realistic interactive simulations; and autonomous systems testing, where self-driving vehicles and drones can be evaluated in complex virtual environments. More broadly, any application that requires AI to understand and respond to a changing environment could benefit from this approach.

Looking ahead, as generative 3D models become increasingly capable and world models continue to evolve, how do you envision the relationship between human creators and AI changing over the next five years? Could we eventually reach a point where individuals can generate entire interactive worlds through natural language, and what breakthroughs would be necessary to make that vision a reality?

I think it is difficult to predict exactly where we will be in five years, given how rapidly the technology is advancing. Tripo has been releasing new models and capabilities every few months, and each generation opens up possibilities that were difficult to imagine before.

In a recent development, that remarked, enabling individuals to create entire interactive worlds through natural language is a longer-term goal that Tripo is working toward through Project Eden. The goal is to create worlds that persist indefinitely, evolving through ongoing interactions with humans and AI agents. EdenLive represents an vital milestone toward this goal, bringing real-time generative rendering to interactive experiences.

Realizing this vision will require breakthroughs in persistent world-state modeling, physical correctness, long-term memory, multi-agent interaction, and efficient real-time generation.

With the advancement of technology, I believe we will increasingly be freed from time-consuming and tedious technical processes. Instead, creators will be able to focus more on directing, refining, and shaping entire creative experiences. AI will broaden what is possible, but the vision and creative judgment behind the work will remain human.
