Industry News: 8 of the biggest MLB contracts for Japanese players, from $700 million to $34 million

Eight of the largest MLB deals for Japanese players, from Ohtani's $700 million to Murakami's $34 million, and what each total leaves out.

Shohei Ohtani signed the richest contract a Japanese player has ever received, and the Dodgers paid it for a star they had already watched in the majors. Los Angeles committed $700 million over 10 years to a player who had spent six seasons across town with the Angels, according to ESPN. Nobody had to guess whether his game would carry over from Japan, so the hard question about the deal is how the Dodgers built it. The Associated Press reports that 97% of the money was deferred without interest when he signed in 2023. Almost all of the record total is a promise to pay later, and the question that matters is when the Dodgers actually hand the money over.

The Dodgers will pay most of Ohtani's money long after he signed, and they will spread it across a decade. The deferred payments arrive each July 1 from 2034 through 2043, according to The Associated Press. A dollar paid that far in the future, with no interest added, is worth less than a dollar paid today, and the Dodgers' real cost is below $700 million. Nobody needs to settle on an exact figure for that gap to matter. Ohtani's total can't be compared directly with a contract that pays a player during the seasons he plays.

Recent findings suggest that the Dodgers committed more money to Yoshinobu Yamamoto than any team had committed to a player moving straight from Japan to the majors, and they did it before he had thrown a major league pitch. His $325 million contract is the largest for a player arriving directly from Japan, according to ESPN. The Los Angeles Times reports that he was 25 when he signed in 2023, and that before bidding opened, he was expected to command at least $200 million. Clubs judged him on his pitching in Japan, where he won the Pacific League's MVP award in three consecutive seasons. Teams kept raising their offers well past that early estimate, and his final price measures how hard clubs competed for him as much as how good he was.

The Chicago Cubs paid one of the highest prices ever for a hitter coming from Japan, and they paid it for the best batting season any player there had put together in years. ESPN reports that Sato agreed to $120 million over six years with the Cubs, the third-largest deal for a player from Japan's league. Sato became the first Triple Crown winner in Japan since Munetaka Murakami's 2022 season, according to CBS Sports. The Cubs are paying for the one skill Sato highlighted best, his power. They are not paying for a hitter without flaws.

Sato misses the ball far more often than a typical major league hitter, and the Cubs signed him anyway. He made contact on 66% of his swings, against a major league average of 77%, according to . That gap means he will likely strike out more than most hitters once he faces major league pitching. Chicago is betting that his home runs will outweigh the empty swings, and the six-year term shows the team expects that power to last for years. "Like Murakami, Sato carries risk, and like Murakami, his power makes that risk worth the dice roll," CBS Sports writer Dayn Perry wrote.

Toronto paid Kazuma Okamoto a solid price rather than a superstar's price. The Blue Jays gave him a four-year, $60 million contract, which tops the $54 million deal Tatsuya Imai signed, according to ESPN. Bringing him to Toronto cost the team more than his contract. Toronto must also send $10,875,000 to the Yomiuri Giants, the club he played for in Japan, according to CBC. A contender spent that much on a hitter who isn't a headline star because it needed a third baseman.

Toronto finished last season without a regular third baseman. Addison Barger and Ernie Clement split the starts at the position almost evenly, CBC reports, and neither player held the job. With this contract, the team bought a full-time answer at a position it had been filling day to day.

According to recent reports, the Blue Jays are betting that Okamoto's power will carry over to the majors. He arrives directly from Japan's league with 248 career home runs, according to CBC, and he hit every one of them in Japan. Toronto paid for a proven record that major league pitching has yet to test. It did not pay for star power.

The White Sox may have paid too little for Munetaka Murakami rather than too much. Chicago signed him to a two-year, $34 million contract, according to ESPN, the smallest of the biggest deals Japanese players have signed with MLB teams. For that price, the team got power that only one other Japanese-born hitter has shown in the majors. Murakami and Shohei Ohtani are the only Japanese-born players with 35 or more home runs in a season, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. Murakami's contract is the smallest in this group, but his power puts him in the same company as the most celebrated Japanese hitter in the game.

Major league teams now compete to sign the best players from Nippon Professional Baseball, Japan's top league, and fans tend to judge each of those signings by the total written on the contract. That total leaves out a second payment, because a team that signs a posted Japanese star pays the player and also pays the Japanese club that let him go. MLB.com reports that the Yankees paid Masahiro Tanaka's Japanese club a posting fee separate from his contract. The Blue Jays owed the same kind of fee to Kazuma Okamoto's club under MLB's agreement with NPB, with that money kept outside his contract, according to CBC. The second bill is a standing part of the price of Japanese talent. It keeps rising on deals past $50 million, with the Japanese club taking 15% of any value above that mark, according to CBS Sports. None of that money appears in the headline figure, so the total fans quote covers only part of what a team spends.

Deadlines also shape the price, and they favor the team most willing to spend. Once his club posts him, a Japanese player has 45 days to negotiate with MLB teams, according to MLB.com. Every interested team has to make its best offer within the same few weeks. The final number reflects how many teams wanted the player and how badly they needed him, as much as his record in Japan. The years and dollars on paper can also differ from what a team actually pays and how long the player stays. Money deferred for years costs a team less than money paid now, and an opt-out can end a deal well before its last season. Fans who compare totals learn less than it seems about who is the better player, and the smallest deal can turn out to be the best one.

ESPN set out to rank the largest MLB deals that Japanese players have signed, including the Yankees' 2014 contract for Masahiro Tanaka, the Cubs' deal for Teruaki Sato and the White Sox's deal for Munetaka Murakami. Each of those totals is the money a team promised, and it is best read as a starting point. Behind a total can sit a fee owed to a club in Japan, pay that arrives years later, an exit the player controls or a team in a hurry to fill a hole. Read that way, the order of these deals says more about what each team promised than about what each player turned out to be worth.

Sources indicate that the 10 years are also less certain than they look, because Ohtani can leave for reasons that have nothing to do with how he plays. The Associated Press reports that the 2023 contract lets him opt out if Mark Walter stops being the controlling owner or if Andrew Friedman leaves as president of baseball operations. A change in the front office, rather than a slump, could end the decade early.

The record contract puts off most of its payments for a decade and leaves its length in the hands of the team's owner and its top baseball executive.

It has been revealed that the Dodgers bid the highest because signing Yamamoto served a strategy built around Shohei Ohtani. Ohtani helped recruit him, taking part with teammates Betts and Freeman in the meeting the Dodgers held with Yamamoto at their ballpark, according to the Los Angeles Times. "It was crucial to Shohei that this wasn't the one move we were gonna make, and I think anyone who has watched us operate over the years, we're trying to add really good players at every turn," Andrew Friedman, the Dodgers' president of baseball operations, told the Los Angeles Times in 2023. A team that had made that promise to its biggest star had little reason to stop bidding at the early estimate. When several teams compete for one player, the team with the most pressing plan usually decides the final price.

It has been revealed that so far, Yamamoto has pitched well enough to justify the Dodgers' spending. He earned the World Series MVP award in 2025, according to MLB.com. Paying well above the early estimate looks less like a mistake when the player is judged the most valuable performer in the World Series. The competition among teams raised what the Dodgers spent, but it did not make the deal a bad one.

The Dodgers paid extra to sign him, and so far he has pitched like the player they were bidding for.

The Yankees offered Masahiro Tanaka a sum that few pitchers of any background had received. Newly introduced York signed him for $155 million over seven years in 2014, according to ESPN. MLB.com reports that it was then the fifth-highest contract ever given to a pitcher, and only Kershaw, Verlander, Hernandez and Sabathia had been paid more. The Yankees put a pitcher who came directly from Nippon Professional Baseball, and who had never thrown a major league inning, in the same pay bracket as those four.

According to recent reports, newly introduced York spent more to get Tanaka than the figure that ranks him. The Yankees also paid Rakuten, the Japanese club that posted him, $20 million for the right to sign him, according to MLB.com. That payment bought permission alone, and none of it went to Tanaka. The Yankees' real bet on him was larger than his ranked total.

The Yankees made their case for Tanaka entirely on what he had done in Japan. In 2013, his last season with Rakuten, he went 24-0 with a 1.27 ERA, according to MLB.com. No team had seen him face major league hitters, so that season highlighted promise rather than proof. The Yankees still bid as if they would never get another chance. "That was our one shot, and we took it very seriously," Yankees general manager Brian Cashman said in 2014.

The Yankees paid to find out what Tanaka could do against major league hitters, not to buy a record he had already set there.

The Cubs took that risk because the departure of two regulars left them short of a bat. Ian Happ and Seiya Suzuki both left in free agency, according to CBS Sports, and together they were worth 7.8 wins above a replacement player in 2026. Sato is being asked to replace a large share of that production by himself. Chicago looks to use him in left or right field, even though he is known mainly as a first or third baseman, and his biggest adjustment will be the better pitching he faces. Sato's price reflects how badly Chicago needed a bat as much as it reflects the quality of his bat. (Related: Large Language Models: A Latest Moore's Law?...)

According to recent reports, toronto turned to Kaima Taira after several other starting pitchers it pursued signed elsewhere. The Blue Jays agreed to pay him $80 million over five years, with a $5 million bonus and an opt-out, according to ESPN. Tarik Skubal chose the Latest York Mets even though Toronto had reportedly offered him nine years and $420 million, according to USA TODAY. Two more starters the team went after also landed with other clubs, with Trevor Rogers joining the Chicago Cubs and Freddy Peralta joining the Baltimore Orioles. MLB's labor deal expires Dec. 1, and Toronto chose to fill its rotation before that deadline rather than keep searching. The team paid Taira's price out of need and failed pursuits as much as for anything he did on the mound.

Toronto is paying mostly for Taira's return to starting. After working strictly in relief as a closer in 2025, he moved back into a rotation in 2026 and led every qualified starter in Japan with a 1.36 ERA, according to USA TODAY. He has never thrown more than 150 innings in a season, yet Toronto will likely slot him third in its rotation in 2027. The Blue Jays are betting less on the ERA than on whether his arm can last through a full season of starts.

The opt-out puts much of the reward in Taira's hands. If he holds up as a starter, he can leave and look for a better deal before the five years run out. If his arm wears down, the choice to stay is his as well, and Toronto keeps the cost.

Need brought Taira to Toronto, and his own success could take him away.

Okamoto settles one position in an infield that is otherwise uncertain. Bo Bichette is a free agent who has not re-signed, CBC reports, so Toronto may still have a hole elsewhere in the infield. The team put its largest money that winter into pitching. In the same winter it committed $60 million to Okamoto, Toronto gave Dylan Cease a seven-year, $210 million deal. That made Okamoto a supporting piece for a team spending heavily to win now.

Toronto paid Okamoto's price as a team in a hurry to fill a gap, and the figure is no measure of how good he will turn out to be.

Recent findings suggest that tatsuya Imai earned a place among the largest MLB contracts signed by Japanese players, though near the low end of that group, when the Astros gave him three years and $54 million, according to ESPN. Those three years look like a firm commitment from Houston. But Imai can opt out after the 2026 season, MLB.com reports, and he can opt out again after 2027, which means he could become a free agent after one or two seasons. The $54 million shows what the Astros promised him. How long he stays is Imai's decision.

In a recent development, imai could take a short deal because his results have held up for years. He has posted a 2.18 ERA since 2023, according to MLB.com. That mark covers several seasons of work, so his success does not rest on a single strong year. His camp put him on the same level as Yoshinobu Yamamoto. "Certainly, he's done everything that Yamamoto's done in NPB," Scott Boras, Imai's agent, said. Even with that standing, Imai signed for three years instead of locking in his prime at a fixed rate. He kept the right to ask for a latest price soon. The short term reads as Imai's wager on himself rather than a sign that teams doubted him. The Astros accepted a deal that could end early because they needed starting pitchers right away. Three Astros pitchers had Tommy John surgery in 2025, according to MLB.com. Pitchers usually miss more than a season after that operation, so the Astros faced the coming year with holes in their rotation. They also expect to lose Framber Valdez, a mainstay of that rotation, in free agency. A team that short of starters now gains more from one or two excellent seasons than from waiting for a longer commitment.

The Astros are guaranteed to pay Imai $54 million, but they carry the risk that his success will take him to another team before the three years are up.

According to recent reports, chicago has already received a full slugger's season from the first year of a short deal. By Sept. 25 of his rookie season, Murakami had hit 35 home runs, the Chicago Sun-Times reports, and only Jose Abreu and Ron Kittle had reached that mark as White Sox rookies. Murakami does most of his damage by pulling the ball toward right field, but 29% of his home runs have gone to center field or the left side. His power reaches more than one part of the park. One season of that production already makes a $34 million price look low.

Murakami came to Chicago straight from Japan's Nippon Professional Baseball, and one season later he is an vital hitter for a White Sox team chasing the Cleveland Guardians in the AL Central. His value is being tested in games that matter now, and the bargain depends on him staying at this level. "We know how impactful he can be when he's right and feeling good," White Sox manager Will Venable said, as reported by the Chicago Sun-Times. Chicago signed Murakami for only two seasons, and half of that time is nearly over. One poor season could erase the bargain.

Chicago got a historic return in the first year of the smallest of these deals, and only Murakami's second season can show whether he can repeat it.
