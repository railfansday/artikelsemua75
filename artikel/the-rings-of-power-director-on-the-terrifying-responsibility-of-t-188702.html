Industry observers note that with the last two episodes of Season 3, The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power has dropped a fair number of surprises for fans already. First, it was revealed that Episode 7 would be earning a theatrical release, allowing viewers to watch one of Middle-earth's darkest and most pivotal moments come to life on the big screen. Then, Prime Video had another twist in store when it was confirmed that the season's last two episodes would be dropping a day earlier than originally scheduled. It's a shift that feels only fitting for Sauron's (Charlie Vickers) infamous forging of the One Ring to Rule Them All — which, as the final moments of the episode confirm, forces the Elves to remove their own rings of power as the Dark Lord's deception is keenly felt. Before Episode 7's Prime Video premiere, Collider spoke with acclaimed filmmaker Stefan Schwartz (Dexter, The Americans, Star City), who joins The Rings of Power's directing team for Season 3, about the daunting task of bringing such an iconic Middle-earth moment to life, the surprising element that informed the scene's major beats, the experience of working closely with Vickers during the sequence's week-long shoot, and more.

COLLIDER: What was your reaction to finding out that Episode 7 was going to be screened in theaters and that fans were going to have the opportunity to see everything play out on the major screen?

STEFAN SCHWARTZ: I was super excited. In fact, last night I went to my local cinema and saw it with probably 200 people, and it was packed full. There was cheering at the end. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

And I went up to a few of them and went, "Oh, hi, I directed the show, and I wondered what you thought about it, and what you liked about it, and what you didn't like about it, and why you're here." And it was really fun hearing their reactions. They were all fans. They were all super excited. I listened to some of them, and they got a lot of what I was hoping they would get from that Ring moment.

Sources indicate that to talk about the One Ring itself, its forging is something we've never really seen depicted on screen before. It's such an iconic moment in Tolkien's lore and Middle-earth history. In preparing for the scene, what felt important to get right?

SCHWARTZ: For me, the only thing that exists is that Alan Lee sketch, and there's no other description of how the Ring is created. So, it's a terrifying responsibility to take that on, and it drives Bilbo and Frodo thousands of years later. A terrifying thing. Well, if I'm going to take it on, I really need to understand the elements that are notable to this, if I'm going to try and be as true to it as I can be. Tolkien created Middle-earth initially as a piece of music, which then had a dark element added to it — which is the Ring — and then Middle-earth was created from there. So I wanted music.

In a recent development, I also wanted this idea that Sauron would have to give himself up, some sort of sacrifice. That was really vital to me and to the team. Also, that Mount Doom wasn't just a place where he did it, but it was a character that responded to him in a realistic way. Mount Doom is important, this idea of music was important, and the idea of sacrifice was important, and then it's just Sauron's intention and his will to make it all happen.

It has been revealed that with that in mind, we had that earlier scene where he is arrogant and walks in, and the mountain just claims, "Get out!" and throws him against a wall — so, the second time, he comes in with humility. I wanted to really play a story because if you just create the Ring and two seconds later, there it is, and he's made it, it feels like it's not the One Ring. It needs elements to it. It needs a complexity and a depth and a backstory to it, rather than just hammering a bit of gold and silver.

How long was the entire process of pre-production planning and then filming the scene itself from start to finish?

Recent findings suggest that SCHWARTZ: It took a week to film. The amount of thought that went into it, though, was huge, because you spin around, and you go, "How can we make music be a part of this?" And then the idea of cymatics came in. The Ring isn't just created by hammering; it's created by the mountain as well and the vibrations that the mountain affects.

Industry observers note that it was a lot, a lot of thought, and because it was right at the end of the shoot, I kept on coming round to it and coming up with ideas and sketches and animatics and a story of all the beats — of offering up the hammer, and how a metal melts that's unmeltable, of how the lava would descend and go into these pockets, which actually do exist in volcanoes, and create superheated drops of lava. So a lot of thought went into it, and then a week of shooting with just a small set — well, quite a substantial set, but a small piece of Mount Doom, which is huge, and Charlie [Vickers], with me yelling at him, "Now, imagine that there's lava flowing down in front of you, and now it's really heavy! Put on the fans! More fans!".

Charlie is the sole actor in that scene, so he's really tasked with bearing the weight of the moment on his shoulders. This question also ties into something that you touched on earlier, which is that the crafting of the One Ring requires Sauron to empty himself — to literally die and then be reborn. How did you work closely with Charlie to find the right degree of intensity and how that would really manifest and crescendo? There's almost a musicality to the sequence itself.

Industry observers note that SCHWARTZ: Yes. We spoke a lot about it, and I broke it down into a multi-stage process — from walking in, to offering up the hammer, to falling to the knees, to the lava going down around him, to the lava coming up the sides and dropping down. So, for each of those, we talked about a level of intensity, coming back into the anvil and the hammering and feeling those beats moving around him, the waves going out and coming back in and hitting him. Together, we beat it out.

Sources indicate that I said, "Here are my significant 20 beats, and now let's talk about the intricacies of those." Because in amongst them, you want breaths. You have intensity, but then you need a breath so that more intensity can come, so you can keep rebuilding. Whereas if you just keep going up and up, it's much harder to keep that. So we had breaths in the hammering. We had breaths walking around the anvil, and before the hammering — and then a huge breath when Sauron died, and I wanted to hold that as long as we humanly could, before that hand comes up on the anvil. And then, just even from the walk from the anvil to the end of the promontory, to build in stature and power as that Ring and he bond together. He gives his life to the Ring, and the Ring brings his life back.

By that point, you really see the stark contrast between this golden, gleaming Ring and how dirty and bloody and drained Sauron looks. I have to ask: is there a special Ring filming technique? I assume there's probably some post-production magic involved with the script on the Ring itself, but was there anything special you wanted to do to emphasize the contrast in the moment when he's finally wearing it on his hand?

In a recent development, SCHWARTZ: I really wanted it to be real. The tricky thing is all the cuts and burns on Charlie's face took a long while to do, so I shot the whole sequence in order so we could build up his makeup and prosthetics. He really did look that way, and that's not augmented, particularly.

I tried where I could to use real elements and not rely on visual effects, so when I'm putting focus between him and the Ring, it's a beautiful shiny Ring. It doesn't have the words on it — those are added later, obviously — but, as much as I could, I really focused on Charlie looking terrible and bruised and like he's been through the wars. But also, he played so much of that just emotionally. In his walk-up, he gained ownership of the Ring and Sauron's power. It was fun to play.
