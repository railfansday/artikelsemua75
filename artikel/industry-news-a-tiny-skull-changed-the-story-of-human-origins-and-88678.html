The Taung Child is about 3 million years old, and a latest study suggests some fossils long grouped with it may belong to a different species.

In a recent development, A skull the size of an orange changed everything we thought we knew about human origins in 1924. Now, a century later, it’s doing it again.

New research has pinned the Taung Child – the original specimen of Australopithecus africanus and the first fossil proof that humans evolved in Africa – to between 2.96 and 3.03 million years old.

A more unsettling finding was that many of the fossils long grouped alongside it probably don’t belong there at all.

The study was conducted by an international team led by Gabriele Macho from the Vrije Universiteit Brussel.

The experts combined uranium-lead dating, 3D dental morphometrics, brain development modeling, and fossilized pollen to argue that southern Africa’s early hominin landscape was far messier than textbooks suggest.

Here’s an uncomfortable fact that paleoanthropology has been skirting for decades. Australopithecus africanus – one of the most cited species in human evolution – has never been properly defined.

Recent findings suggest that the Taung Child is its holotype, the original reference specimen. But no definitive diagnostic criteria were ever established for classifying other fossils into the same species.

According to recent reports, since the 1950s, researchers have lumped dozens of South African fossils under the A. africanus label. However, they used vague qualitative descriptors like “more rounded” and “less extreme.”.

Strictly speaking, the Taung Child remains the only unequivocal member of its own species.

“For over 100 years, we assumed the Taung Child was just one of many similar fossils from the era,” Macho noted.

“Our findings turn that on its head. The human family tree wasn’t a neat, straight trunk – it was a chaotic, tangled bush driven by climate shifts.”. (Related: Obsessed fan breaks into YouTuber’s home demanding marr...)

Sources indicate that the team used 3D geometric morphometrics of the dentino-enamel junction – the boundary between enamel and dentine inside first molars – to compare Taung against other supposed A. africanus specimens.

Recent findings suggest that this internal tooth structure forms early in development. Unlike bone, which remodels through life, it is under strong genetic control. (Related: Tech Report: Animal control called to rescue alligator ...)

According to recent reports, taung’s molars fell at the edge of the A. africanus cluster in shape space, equidistant between Sts 18 (a presumed A. africanus) and SK 61 (a Paranthropus robustus).

Other fossils traditionally lumped with Taung – MLD 2 from Makapansgat, Sts 24 from Sterkfontein – looked nothing like it.

The adult fossils assigned to Australopithecus africanus had braincase volumes ranging from 391 to 568 milliliters. That range is comparable to the variation seen within a single living ape species.

Industry observers note that braincase shape raised a different question. Using growth patterns in living apes as a guide, the researchers estimated how the Taung Child’s braincase might have changed by adulthood.

Taung could have developed a braincase shaped like those of two adult fossils, Sts 5 and Sts 60.

Sources indicate that three others were harder to explain using the same growth patterns, suggesting that some fossils assigned to A. africanus may belong to a different species.

According to recent reports, the team extracted fossilized pollen from cave infills at the Taung site. They found wetland sedges, grasses, and savanna indicators – but also Podocarpus, which is startling.

Today, Podocarpus forests are confined to the wet eastern Escarpment, roughly 400 kilometers northeast of Taung.

Their presence suggests ancient Taung was more wooded than the current dry grassland.

The area was likely a mix of riparian woodland and Bushveld-like savanna stretching across what is today semi-arid Thornveld.

The paper’s most ambitious move is linking all of this to climate.

Hydroclimate records from marine and terrestrial cores show that between 4 and 2.5 million years ago, southern Africa experienced pronounced wet-dry oscillations driven by orbital eccentricity cycles.

The region sits at a critical biogeographic junction, where the tropics bordering the temperate world meet.

That boundary shifted over millions of years as the Intertropical Convergence Zone migrated northward.

The authors propose that early human relatives followed shifting habitats.

As those habitats expanded and contracted, populations may have repeatedly split apart and reconnected, helping explain the diversity seen in the fossils.

Industry observers note that A century after its discovery, the Taung Child is still the only specimen that can truly be called Australopithecus africanus. Everything else might be something different.

The authors are blunt. Scientists should stop invoking “intraspecific variation” as a catch-all explanation.

Instead, they should re-examine the historical biases baked into how these fossils were sorted. They should also treat the eco-evolutionary dynamics of Plio-Pleistocene southern Africa as the primary engine of the diversity we see.

According to recent reports, the Taung Child isn’t just a museum piece. It’s a provocation and a clear warning against tidy narratives.

The study is published in the journal npj Palaeoecosystems.
