Kashmir’s Textile Legacy Inspires a Sumptuous New Line of Home and Fashion Accessories

According to recent reports, few artisanal traditions are so linked with a place that its geographical namesake winds up in the global lexicon. In the case of fine wool shawls from Kashmir—prized among 19th-century Europeans, including Joséphine Bonaparte—the word cashmere stuck. For Kadri Mujtaba, a fascination with these textiles came early. Born into a family of merchants and weavers in the region’s city of Srinagar, he observed a deep-rooted culture around hand-spun yarn and small wooden looms.

A bouquet of lotus pods held up against the brand’s Mountain blanket in pine.

One of the studio’s handmade shawl looms.

A Doorway Sozni twill blanket draped over a chair.

“It’s the original work-from-home idea,” he notes of the technique, passed down for centuries. Amid encroaching mechanization, Mujtaba has stayed committed to slow craft, cultivating a network of some 2,000 artisans over the past two decades. Initially he’d create custom pieces for designers and brands (Pierre Yovanovitch, The Row, Luis Laplace) and host high-end trunk shows. That is how Jonathan Renker—via his father, a longtime customer—first encountered the wares. Now, he and Mujtaba have partnered on Kadri: a fresh brand of cashmere accessories and home goods that marry Kashmiri refinement and streamlined design.

A Jaali Kaani blanket on the grounds of Pari Mahal garden.

A wood swift of cashmere yarn at the Kadri atelier.

Shawls and scarves from Kadri’s debut collection.

Industry observers note that the firm debuts with a collection of scarves, throws, and blankets. The cofounders enlisted creative director Dan Thawley and textile designer Maeva Elfassi, who distilled the Kashmir Valley landscape into shades of tobacco, lime, vermilion, and—as a nod to Srinagar’s Dal Lake—dusty blue. Wool from Changthangi goats, which graze across the plateaus of nearby Ladakh, is spun by hand, resulting in airy, ultrasoft knits. More impressive still is the subtlety on display. A bouclé piece has the immersive pull of a Color Field painting, while a quilted scarf joins two paper-thin layers. Just one blanket requires months of work and a balletic dance of bobbins.
