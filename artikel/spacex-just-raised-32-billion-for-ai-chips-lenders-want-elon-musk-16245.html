It has been revealed that the bonds are part of a more than $40 billion financing package SpaceX revealed in October. To try to keep the debt off its books, the financing runs through a separate entity -- a trust that sells the bonds, buys the chips, and leases them to SpaceX. Similar maneuvers are increasingly common across the AI data center market as companies look for ways to deal with ballooning debt balances.

Functionally, however, SpaceX is still the one borrowing, and its quarterly lease payments are what pay the bondholders back.

In a recent development, the largest piece, about $25.2 billion, was priced at a yield of 8.259%. The average yield on high-grade U.S. corporate debt was 5.95% that day, so investors are being paid about 2.3 percentage points more than usual for bonds that are supposed to be relatively safe. Bonds considered "junk" were yielding an average of 8.3% that day. (Related: See where votes are being counted in Senate and House m...)

According to recent reports, the loans are secured by the chips in the same way your mortgage is secured by your home. If SpaceX ever defaulted, the lenders' fallback would be to repossess the GPUs and sell them. That's obviously quite difficult if they're in space. It's not clear if the lenders were able to get anything binding in the final documents, but SpaceX says these chips are for its "terrestrial compute build-out.".

According to recent reports, the chips, destined for SpaceX data centers, are expected to start arriving in January. They're part of the massive build-out SpaceX's AI division is in the thick of, which has driven enormous growth on its top-line. The AI unit nearly tripled its sales between Q2 and Q3, growing revenue from $2.6 billion to $7.6 billion. Costs are high, however: the business spent $16.4 billion in capex in its Q3 -- the lion's share of the firm's overall $19.6 billion capex spend.

Recent findings suggest that while the terms are less favorable than past deals, SpaceX got the money it was seeking. CFO Bret Johnsen stated in the organization's October financing announcement that SpaceX is seeing "less than one year payback periods" on its infrastructure investments.

But I'm still skeptical long-term. Debt market investors are incredibly sophisticated, and watching spreads -- the gap between what a U.S. Treasury pays and what the bond yields -- widen for SpaceX doesn't do much to convince me that the economics make sense. Right now, the payoff looks incredible. I don't think that's going to hold up.
