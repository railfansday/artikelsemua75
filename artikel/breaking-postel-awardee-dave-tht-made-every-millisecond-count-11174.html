📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Dave Täht understood the Internet. And it was important to him that everyone else understand it, too. Which is why he spent decades going to ambitious and creative lengths to explain it. To regulators, to standard setting bodies, to the most influential tech companies in the world, to the next generation of Internet leaders, and to absolutely anyone who would ask him about it.

Sources indicate that this drive stemmed from his visceral understanding that for change to be possible, people need to both understand and care about the stakes of inaction. And what he desperately wanted to change was the Internet. Specifically, people’s ability to use it meaningfully. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

According to recent reports, täht saw every dropped video call, every slow loading page, and every frozen progress-bar—all symptoms of what’s known as bufferbloat—as a barrier to Internet connectivity. Bufferbloat is the time that data spends stuck in the hardware responsible for forwarding traffic. Täht went about identifying and advocating for solutions to that congestion, with the goal of delivering the full promise of the Internet to people. And also, he joked, so that he could jam remotely with his drummer who kept missing band practice. (Related: Few-shot learning in practice: GPT-Neo and the 🤗 Accele...)

Industry observers note that getting people to care about bufferbloat wasn’t always an easy pitch. Latency is the time it takes for data to travel across a network from one point to another. The lower the latency, the more seamless the experience, while high latency renders the network effectively unusable. Täht was adamant that latency, far more so than bandwidth, determined how much time people spent waiting for a page to load.

Recent findings suggest that dave’s communication style is the stuff of Internet lore. “He did not seek to monetize his performance work but simply applied himself passionately and articulately to solving problems,” remembers Internet pioneer and one of the Internet Society’s founders, Vint Cerf. Täht famously galvanized a conference roomful of people to enact traffic moving through the network, wrote songs about open-source software, and relentlessly pushed regulators and companies to take action.

Just as Jon Postel did, Täht unfortunately passed away prematurely. And, like Postel, Täht died having left the kind of mark on the world that is rare for any one person to accomplish.

Täht’s legacy can be found in community hubs in Nicaragua, where he worked to expand connectivity, and first discovered the urgency of solving bufferbloat. It can be found in every lag-free video call with a loved one. It can be found in outer space—Täht helped Starlink solve its latency challenges. And it can be found in the countless people he mentored and befriended over the years, who will not stop until bufferbloat stops getting in the way of band practice. “We are in milliseconds territory now,” stated Borsik. “But for the future we will need microseconds.”.

As much as Täht loved the Internet, and as notable as it was for him that all of us understand and can access it, he didn’t just sing about open-source, he sang about how we all need to log off and go outside, too.

Täht is remembered as a brilliant, passionate engineer. A generous friend. And an Internet troubadour.
