Tropical Cyclone Xavier launched the basin’s storm season with a bang.

Industry observers note that even as the unusually quiet 2026 storm season winds down in the North Atlantic, seas have begun to stir on the other side of the world in the Southwest Pacific. On November 15, 2026, the Fiji Meteorological Service began issuing updates about a slow-moving tropical disturbance northeast of Samoa and the Cook Islands. Within just a few days, it had become one of the most intense November tropical cyclones the region has seen in decades. (Related: Industry News: Take-Two CEO is shutting down questions ...)

Recent findings suggest that named Xavier, the system intensified rapidly, thriving in an environment with minimal wind shear and sea surface temperatures of 29 degrees Celsius (84 degrees Fahrenheit)—well above the threshold required to sustain and strengthen developing tropical cyclones. Xavier was the basin’s second named storm of the season, following only Wati, a short-lived storm that emerged in October. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

As Xavier developed, NASA satellite data showed the storm churning along the southwestern edge of a massive tongue of warm water spanning from the west coast of South America to the central Pacific—a telltale sign of the El Niño that has been present in the region since June.

When the MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer) on NASA’s Aqua satellite captured this image at 02:53 Universal Time on November 17, 2026 (4:53 p.m. Cook Islands Time on November 16), forecasters estimated Xavier had maximum winds of about 195 kilometers (120 miles) per hour. Winds reached a peak intensity of 220 kilometers (140 miles) per hour later that day, a Category 5 storm on the cyclone intensity scale used in the region or a Category 4 on the Saffir-Simpson scale.

The storm ranks as the strongest November tropical cyclone east of the international date line on record, noted Colorado State University hurricane expert Phil Klotzbach. Xavier’s formation east of the date line, near the northern Cook Islands, was unusual, but its easterly position aligns with research that indicates that storm activity in the region typically shifts northeast during El Niño years. Research also reveals that El Niño tends to fuel storms that strengthen at an unusually rapid pace.

According to news reports, Xavier damaged homes and caused power disruptions as it passed the northern Cook Islands. It then encountered dry air and a less favorable wind shear environment and slowly weakened as it pushed westward, reducing the threat that it posed to Samoa and American Samoa.

Recent findings suggest that xavier is likely one of the first of several storms to affect the region in the coming months. A seasonal outlook from the Fiji Meteorological Service projects that the region could see between 11 and 15 named tropical cyclones this season, with the greatest risk east of the international date line. Storm activity in the Southwest Pacific basin typically peaks in February.
