According to recent reports, there are many important big-picture questions about original meaning and the Free Speech, Free Press, Assembly, and Petition Clauses. This short Essay will leave those questions for others. Instead, it will offer as illustrations some specific, concrete questions—and, in some instances, answers—about original meaning and the freedom of expression. I hope these narrow illustrations can help us think about the broader questions. (Related: Dividends Aren't What You Think They Are...)

According to recent reports, I. Broad Evidence, and Silently Consistent Precedents A. "The Liberty of the Press Is Not Confined to Newspapers and Periodicals" B. Symbolic Expression C. Civil Liability II. Scant Evidence: Provoking a Riot III. Ambiguous Evidence: Enjoining Repetition of Libels.

According to recent reports, whether courts should shift the constitutional law of free expression to a more originalist footing is an important and difficult question. Perhaps it's a question that should be resolved entirely as a matter of constitutional theory—maybe such an approach is required by proper interpretive principles, or maybe it's forbidden by other principles (for instance, having to do with precedent).

Industry observers note that but it's also possible that the answer may rest in part on how practically usable an originalist framework is likely to be. And to consider that, it's helpful to have in mind some practical questions. Some are already familiar, for instance questions related to damages liability in libel cases, obscenity law, and the like. This short Essay has aimed to help supplement those familiar examples with some others that haven't been much discussed; I hope others who think about the topic can find them useful.
