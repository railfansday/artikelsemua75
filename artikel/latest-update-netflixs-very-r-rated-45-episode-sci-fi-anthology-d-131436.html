According to recent reports, in 1981, the animated cult classic Heavy Metal fused hard rock and metal music with sci-fi and fantasy storytelling into an anthology film full of rich hand-drawn animation and rotoscoping from a dedicated team of animators. Some of these stories were adapted from Heavy Metal magazine, which has been publishing boundary-pushing genre tales with explicit content since 1977.

In a recent development, almost four decades after the film’s release, filmmakers David Fincher and Tim Miller attempted to reboot Heavy Metal, which eventually morphed into the Netflix sci-fi anthology, Love, Death & Robots. Although the series inevitably carries the DNA of Heavy Metal and its contemporaries, Love, Death & Robots carves out its distinct identity over 45 exceptional episodes. While sci-fi is at the heart of these anthology tales, the show blends everything from horror to comedy to complement the bountiful art styles that are a celebration of animation as a medium. Black Mirror may be Netflix's most famous sci-fi anthology, but if you're looking for something a little more hardcore, Love, Death & Robots is the perfect show for you.

In a recent development, anthologies inevitably contain some middling episodes that miss the mark, and this is also the case with Love, Death & Robots. But the highs are so impressive that it's easy to overlook any episodes that are narratively shallow (despite still being gorgeously animated). An example of an exceptional episode is “Zima Blue,” based on Alastair Reynolds’ acclaimed short story about an enigmatic artist named Zima who uses a specific shade of cyan blue in his artwork. While “Zima Blue” works with a surprising twist, the episode is about the existential search for purpose in life. It is also beautiful to look at, thanks to a clever mix of 2D and 3D animation that makes everything feel surreal.

Recent findings suggest that another standout is “Jibaro,” where a group of armored conquerors stumbles upon a bejeweled siren in a lake. Except for a deaf knight named Jibaro, all the men are driven into a violent frenzy after hearing the woman’s piercing scream. Jibaro approaches the woman, and the two are drawn together in a moment of intense attraction. Unfortunately, Jibaro’s greed overtakes any semblance of empathy, turning the episode into a metaphor for the violation that comes with colonialism. This 15-minute short uses hyperrealistic CGI with keyframe animation and motion-capture to evoke a jarring, dream-like quality that is worth revisiting.

The most interesting aspect of Love, Death & Robots lies in its explicit, no-holds-barred tone and subject matter. Explosive violence and casual nudity aside, the show doesn’t shy away from exploring humanity’s troubling tendency to play god, which informs our relationship with machines. Episodes like “Three Robots” and “Bad Traveling” focus on our moral failings, while “Sonnie’s Edge” and “Snow in the Desert” dissect a post-humanist landscape and the blurred lines between flesh and machinery. Love and obsession are also major themes, along with mortality, revenge, grief, and cosmic terror.

This wide scope of storytelling makes Love, Death & Robots feel fresh and inventive, even when the odd episode starts feeling like an exercise in style over substance. The show’s fourth volume proves how far it has come in terms of prioritizing quality over quantity, overcoming animation and storytelling challenges with smart, impressive short episodes. Some episodes, like “Spider Rose,” are follow-ups of previous stories, piling on unexpected themes and character conflicts that are refreshing to watch. The deaths are still gory and ultra-violent, but this artistic excess pays off in the end.

Love, Death & Robots has something for everyone. You can expect hard science fiction, war horror, nihilistic drama, and comedy to weave a perfectly bingeworthy series that takes pride in its stylistic animation. Live-action segments are also thrown in for good measure for those who seek variety. Not every episode is a hit, but when the show does make an impression, it leaves an indelible mark by setting higher standards for animated anthologies of a similar mold.

Love, Death & Robots can be streamed on Netflix.
