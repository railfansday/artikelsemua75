Furniture designers contend with their personal experiences living in Latest York City through the objects in our most recent roundup.

Newly introduced York, New York has inspired filmmakers, writers and artists to make work since the city’s inception, and for designers, it’s no different.

The furniture and objects below explore aspects of moving to or living in Latest York City, whether it’s the difficulty of maintaining a career and community, the dissonance of missing home, the architecture, or dead, smooshed rats on the daily commute.

In many cases, the collections reflect on the designer’s own identities and heritages.

These include pieces by Kim Mupungilaï, Rafael Prieto and studio Tian & Teague, all of whom have lived or passed through Fresh York during their practice.

Read on below to see how the metropolis has shaped their work.

What Still Holds, by Bechara Maalouf. (Related: Redditor forced to bring desktop PC in checked luggage ...)

Bechara Maalouf created a series of lamps made out of wooden beams from Lower East Side buildings and intricate screens informed by the window guards at his family home in Lebanon. (Related: Obsessed fan breaks into YouTuber’s home demanding marr...)

The series, first displayed during NYC design week 2026 in May, merged Maalouf’s two homes as the designer grappled with the impacts of Israel’s ground invasion of Lebanon from afar.

Find out more about What Still Holds ›.

Pax New Yorkea, by Massimiliano Malagò.

Designer Massimiliano Malagò created an acrylic chair, covered in his own writings, that explores the challenges of maintaining a life in New York City.

“For me, this was making an object that is incredibly luxurious, incredibly grand, but it’s actually talking about something quite common and sometimes quite sad, because the hardships that people go through to stay in the city are just difficult,” he told Dezeen.

Find out more about Pax Newly introduced Yorkea ›.

Los Angeles and Lisbon studio Tian & Teague created the NYC Series based on their own time living in New York and some of the jarring aspects residents become accustomed to.

The collection, which includes a flattened-rat rug, came about from things seen on the studio founders’ daily commute.

Find out more about NYC Series ›.

White Picket Chair, by Han Seungmin.

Upcoming York-based designer Han Seungmin created this chair using modular components from the metallic fences found throughout Brooklyn.

First mass-produced in China in the 1980s, the fences are often used in Asian communities around the boroughs, and Han challenged “the idyllic image of the ‘American Dream’ from an immigrant perspective” through the piece.

Find out more about White Picket Chair ›.

HUE/AM/I-HUE/I/AM, by Kim Mupungilaï.

Belgian interior designer Kim Mupungilaï was prompted to design this collection of wood and fibre furniture after a 2018 visit to Newly introduced York that inspired her to explore her half-Belgian, half-Congolese roots.

“I just started thinking about my own cultural landscape and background and upbringing and wanted to dig deeper,” Mupungilaï told Dezeen.

Find out more about HUE/AM/I-HUE/I/AM ›.

The City Block, by Micah Rosenblatt.

Metalworker and designer Micah Rosenblatt looked to New York City’s historic architecture for his The City Block collection, which features thin, contemporary lines and geometric elements.

Sources indicate that “[The expressions] are based on the cityscape and my relationship to it,” Rosenblatt told Dezeen. “It’s the forms that you would look out and see on your horizon via whatever time period you’re in.”.

Find out more about The City Block ›.

All Other Passports, by Julio Torres and Sabai.

Comedian and actor Julio Torres worked with furniture company Sabai to create his first furniture collection, All Other Passports, debuted during NYC design week 2026.

The collection is influenced by literal and figurative aspects of Torres’ move to Latest York City from El Salvador, such as a curtain motif pulled from JFK‘s international Terminal 4.

Find out more about All Other Passports ›.

For The Future of the Past, by Rafael Prieto.

Designer Rafael Prieto integrated building fragments into several furniture pieces for an exhibition at Emma Scully Gallery earlier this year.

Prieto sourced the cast-offs, including pieces of a cornice, from a facade being demolished across the street from his Tribeca studio, which he had become “deeply interested” in.
