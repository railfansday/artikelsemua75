I'm always looking for books that foster a strong sense of community and connection with nonhuman animals. This is just what author Susan Beilby Magee does in her latest book Wild Conversations: The Wisdom of African Animals, in which she shares wisdom and illuminates a path toward resilience, purpose, and harmony for all of Earth’s inhabitants. She also offers pertinent data from detailed research. Many of her positive messages reminded me of an interview I did with George Bumann about how eavesdropping on the wild conversations with different animals can offer lessons on the remarkable beings they are and what they need from us. Marc Bekoff: Why did you write Wild Conversations?

Susan Beilby Magee: I wrote it because I made a promise to a lion. During a safari in northern Tanzania, I encountered a magnificent male lion walking through the bush. I greeted him silently and received a question in the intuitive way I have communicated with animals for decades: “Will you write a book about us? Will you share our messages with other humans?” I said: “Yes.”.

Sources indicate that that promise deepened over three African safaris. Their messages were wise, sometimes humorous, but grew urgent as wilderness habitats shrank. For example, lions have disappeared from more than 90 percent of their historic range, and research reveals that only about 25 percent of Africa’s savannahs remain within the range of free-ranging lion populations. I wanted readers to encounter these animals not merely as tourist attractions on a safari, but as fellow beings with their own families, awareness, and place on Mother Earth. This book honors that promise.

SBM: I hope to reach animal lovers, conservationists, travelers, people curious about spirituality, and anyone who has felt renewed spending time in nature. I want to reach people overwhelmed by modern life. You need not believe in animal telepathy to recognize the intelligence and dignity of the animals. Although pollution, poaching, and human expansion threaten them, conservation can reverse some of the damage. For example, in Chad’s Zakouma National Park, where poaching had devastated the elephants, only one calf was counted in 2011. In 2018, due to intervention and protection by African Parks, 127 calves were counted. I want my granddaughter’s generation to see what is at stake. I hope readers finish with wonder, affection, and a desire to protect the wild. (Related: 3D Asset Generation: AI for Game Development #3...)

MB: What are some of the topics you consider, and what are some of your major messages?

SBM: The book takes readers from the Serengeti and Tarangire to Ruaha and the Okavango Delta. I share personal encounters and photographs that explore animal wisdom, family life, interconnectedness, spiritual awareness, and conservation.

According to recent reports, our jeep once approached a herd of more than 200 elephants. The matriarch stopped, watched us, and directed the herd to surround the youngest calves. When I silently asked for her message, I heard: “Slow down. Don’t rush. Enjoy the moment. Love your family. Play together. Know that you, we, all life is eternal.” How many of us need a reminder to slow down? The message was not just a comfort, but also a warning: When we move as fast as so many of us do today, we often miss what really matters, whether it’s time with loved ones or confronting the harm we’re causing to the planet.

Other animals offered different lessons. A giraffe invited me to appreciate silence. A leopard encouraged spending time alone in nature and trusting our own inner resources. The hyenas urged purposeful work and devotion to one’s pack. And the lions reminded me to awaken my senses, appreciate beauty, and live with courage. Each message reflected its messenger.

There is also a harder truth: Life and death are interconnected. I watched wildebeest cross the Mara River as crocodiles waited, while predators, scavengers and insects each played a role. Death is natural. What we are doing to these animals is not.

In the book’s final section, Clarion Call, the messages become urgent. Habitat loss, pollution, and human expansion threaten these animals. I worry that lions, elephants, cheetahs and other magnificent creatures may disappear from the wild within my granddaughter’s lifetime. The population of Africa’s savanna elephants has fallen by at least 60 percent in 50 years, according to the IUCN. My central appeal is both spiritual and practical: Reconnect with Mother Earth, recognize that we belong to the same living community, and act to protect the places these animals call home.

MB: How does your book differ from others concerned with similar topics?

SBM: Wild Conversations is neither a scientific field guide nor a conventional safari memoir. I write as a modern mystic, sharing the messages I personally experienced through silent, intuitive communication with animals. Readers may interpret these encounters differently; I welcome that. I simply invite them to pay attention.

According to recent reports, the foreword is by Michael Lorentz, a wildlife photographer who has guided African safaris for 40 years. He writes that the animals are “far more present, far more aware, and far more communicative than most of us are conditioned to believe.” Frank Pope, CEO of Save the Elephants, has endorsed the book, calling it “A passionate love-letter to the wild.” You don’t have to be a mystic to appreciate the book’s photographs and messages.

MB: Are you hopeful that learning more about these animals will inspire greater action to save them and the African wilderness?

It has been revealed that SBM: Yes. I hope people will protect wild animals and wilderness worldwide. When we watch an elephant protect her calf or lion cubs tumble together in play, the distance between “us” and “them” begins to disappear. We see families, relationships, and individual lives. That recognition can lead to respect, and respect can lead to stewardship. I want my granddaughter and all future generations to inherit a world where wild lions still roar, elephants still roam, and the Serengeti remains alive with migrating herds.

In conversation with Susan Beilby Magee, an author, activist and modern mystic whose career spans politics, business, government and spirituality. A leader in Seattle’s women’s movement in the 1970s, she served as a White House Fellow under President Gerald Ford and later became an international business consultant. She holds an MBA from the Wharton School and a BA from Pomona College.
