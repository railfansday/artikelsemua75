AI can help find holiday deals — but it's not great at spotting scams, experts say

Artificial intelligence shopping tools can help consumers compare prices and find gift ideas, but experts say shoppers still need to verify unfamiliar retailers and sellers before buying.

Scammers can use AI to make fake stores, product photos and reviews look convincing. It can also make creating and deploying websites easier, mentioned Leyla Bilge, global head of scam research at cybersecurity manufacturer Norton.

"The biggest impact right now we're seeing in AI is on coding," Bilge said. Scammers can register "thousands and thousands of fake e-shops online," she said, which increases the chances shoppers will encounter one.

A Norton survey of 1,000 U.S. adults found that 16% of respondents had been scammed while shopping online during the holidays. Of those victims, 88% lost money, with an average loss of $394. The survey was conducted from an opt-in online panel between Sept. 10 and 21 by Dynata on behalf of Norton's parent company, Gen. In a recent development, scams can have impacts beyond financial losses. In a survey of 3,110 U.S. adults released in June, the Global Anti-Scam Alliance found that 82% of respondents had encountered a scam in the past year. More than half, 59%, stated the scam affected their mental well-being.

More than half of GASA survey respondents mentioned they had experienced repeat scams.

If you put your address or phone number in an AI tool that turns out to be fraudulent, "you might get reached out to again with a much more sophisticated, much more targeted and personalized attack and scam," Bilge said.

Among respondents who had used an AI shopping assistant or agent, 56% mentioned it had recommended or completed a purchase from a retailer they didn't recognize, the Norton survey found.

An agent focused on finding the lowest price may steer a shopper toward an unfamiliar seller, so experts say shoppers shouldn't treat its recommendation as vetted.

"You absolutely need to check with the retailer directly," said Matt Schulz, chief consumer finance analyst at LendingTree and author of "Ask Questions, Save Money, Make More.".

In a recent development, bilge noted many shoppers make purchases on their phones through social media, sometimes with a single click, leaving them little time or incentive to assess where the link goes. Then, some still buy even if they're unsure of the site's legitimacy.

The Norton survey also found that 35% of Americans had bought from a website or seller they thought might be a scam because the deal seemed too good to pass up.

While AI can narrow gift options and find the lowest prices, experts say shoppers should verify the store before handing over payment details — and, when possible, pay by credit card. Keep order details and receipts, too.

The FTC indicates cardholders can dispute charges in certain cases, such as when merchandise never arrives.
