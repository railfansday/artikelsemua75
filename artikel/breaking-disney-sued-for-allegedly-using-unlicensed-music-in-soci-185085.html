Breaking: Disney Sued for Allegedly Using Unlicensed Music in Social Media Ads: ‘Never Paid a Dollar’

The latest in a flood of lawsuits over music on social media is targeting the Walt Disney Co. — and claiming that the Hollywood giant should have known better.

In a case filed Tuesday (Nov. 24) in Detroit federal court, Zac Hiller and his LAA Music claim Disney illegally used three of the publishing company’s songs across seven social media videos even though the entertainment colossus “never asked for a license” and “never paid a dollar.”.

“For a hundred years, The Walt Disney Company has taught the world one rule: if you want to use what someone else created, you ask permission and you pay,” Hiller’s attorneys write. “Disney enforces that rule against everyone else. This case is about Disney exempting itself from it.”.

Sources indicate that the Disney posts were broadcast to “audiences in the millions,” the lawsuit says, including a promotion for Hulu that garnered 481,700 likes and 17,500 shares. The lawsuit claims the posts were removed after complaints, but that taking them down is not enough: “Taking a video down after getting caught does not undo the infringement.”.

The problem is rooted in the fact that sites like Instagram, TikTok and Snapchat offer libraries of licensed songs that make it easy for owners to add music to their posts. But those tracks are for personal use only; businesses or influencers must utilize a far more limited commercial library or get a synch license, just like any conventional advertisement on TV.

It has been revealed that while many of the lawsuits have come from Universal Music, Sony Music or Warner Music, Tuesday’s case was filed by Hiller, an NFL agent and founder and CEO of the agency Loyalty Above All, which counts players like James Cook, J.K. Dobbins, and Shemar Stewart on its client roster.

The case claims that Disney used three songs owned by Hiller and LAA Music, including “Take it Off ‘Lights Off’ Beat”; “New Opp (beat)”; and “Gang Gang.” Three songs were allegedly used by ESPN, one by Hulu, one by FX and two more by Disney Parks.

It has been revealed that “Disney negotiates synchronization licenses every day [and] employs people whose job is to clear music,” Hiller’s attorneys write. “Its infringement here was not the oversight of a organization too large to notice. It was a business decision: use the music now, take the promotional benefit, and make the rights holder chase it later.”. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Neither side immediately returned requests for comment on Tuesday.
