Reasoning Can’t Fix What a Model Never Read

🌐 Source: Unite • 📅 23 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1419 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The best model I tested scored 90 on grammar and calendar maths in 30 languages, and 48 on idioms. Thinking time explains the first number. It can’t move the second.

Ask a top AI model to put an archaic Italian verb into the present subjunctive, with both objects as pronouns, and it will probably get it right. Ask it for the Danish proverb that indicates the slow and poorly equipped get there in the end, and 16 of the 17 models I tested will fail. That gap is the most useful thing I’ve learned from building a multilingual benchmark, and it cuts against how progress in AI is usually sold. The last two years of model releases have been about reasoning: more compute at answer time, longer chains of thought, higher scores in maths and code. Those gains are real. But reasoning can only work on what a model already holds, and for most of the world’s languages, what it holds is thin. (Related: Tech Report: Leveraging Pre-trained Language Model Chec...)

Recent findings suggest that in September I wrote 150 questions natively in 30 languages. None were translated from English. Each language got five: two on local knowledge (a regional custom, a municipal rule, a local name), two on idioms and proverbs, and one on grammar, script or calendar arithmetic. Every answer is matched against a key taken from a dictionary, a statute or a calendar table, so no model grades another model’s work.

In a recent development, I ran 17 models from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, xAI, DeepSeek, Moonshot, Alibaba and Zhipu, each once, at low reasoning effort. The whole run cost me $9.84.

Five models scored 80 or more on grammar and calendars. None reached 50 on idioms, and 10 of the 17 scored under 25.

Single questions show the pattern more clearly than the averages. A deed from Cairo is dated on the tabular Islamic calendar; add 100 days and give the weekday and Gregorian date. Eleven of 17 models got it. Older Czech treated the word for “count” as neuter; rewrite a sentence so every agreeing word takes that old gender. Ten of 17. Put the rare Portuguese verb contrafazer into the European Portuguese conditional with the object pronoun inside the verb. Twelve of 17.

Now the proverbs, each asked by its meaning alone. The Malay saying for seeking profit from your own family or friends: no model got it. The idiom in the Hindi Shabdsagar dictionary for someone so used to a jarring noise that it no longer startles them: none. The Korean proverb for pestering someone with an unrelated favour while everyone else is frantic: none.

According to recent reports, the calibration rounds made the point sharper. Before writing the final questions, I ran development sets to tune the difficulty, aiming for the best models to land well short of perfect.

On grammar, I kept escalating: rare words, rare forms, then classical and literary morphology that a language editor would check in a reference grammar. GPT-6 Sol scored 92% on the development grammar items and 87% on the final grammar, script and calendar questions. Much harder rules cost it five points.

Recent findings suggest that local knowledge went the other way. When the questions asked for a clause in a national statute, GPT-6 Sol scored 100%. When I moved them to regional and municipal facts whose local Wikipedia article has no English counterpart, it fell to 78%. Idioms never needed hardening at all. Rare sayings given only by their meaning held the leaders down from the first development round, and GPT-6 Sol finished at 37% on the final set.

Recent findings suggest that the development and final sets used different questions, and the final set covered five times as many languages, so read those as directions rather than exact deltas. Both point the same way.

In a recent development, A rule compresses. One pattern covers every verb in its class, so a model that has seen it thousands of times can apply it to a verb it has seen five times. A calendar conversion is arithmetic on known inputs. Both reward working step by step, which is what reasoning models are trained to do.

It has been revealed that the research agrees about where the gains land. OpenAI’s announcement of o1 highlighted performance improving “with more time spent thinking,” and its headline jumps were in competition maths, programming and science. A meta-analysis of over 100 papers by Sprague and colleagues found chain-of-thought helps mainly on maths and symbolic reasoning. On MMLU, answering directly scored almost the same as reasoning first, unless the question or the answer contained an equals sign.

Industry observers note that an idiom has no equals sign. Nothing in “making one person pay for what a whole group lost” lets you derive the Spanish cargar las cabras. Either the model saw the phrase often enough in training, or it didn’t. Kandpal and colleagues showed that a model’s accuracy on a fact tracks how many pretraining documents mention it, and estimated that today’s models would need to be scaled “by many orders of magnitude” to answer well on facts the data barely covers. Mallen and colleagues found that scaling “fails to appreciably improve memorization of factual knowledge in the long tail.”.

In a recent development, thinking harder about a missing fact produces a fluent guess. In my calibration runs, several strong models answered rare idioms with near-miss wordings: plausible proverbs, in the right register, that don’t exist in that form. A bigger reasoning budget makes those guesses better formed. It doesn’t make them right.

A model can reason its way through a calendar. It can’t reason its way to a proverb it never read.

One limit on my own evidence: I ran every model at low effort to keep the run affordable, so my numbers can’t show what maximum effort would add. The case that it would add little on idioms rests on the mechanism and the research above, and I’d welcome a high-effort rerun that proves otherwise.

English makes up 42.6% of the pages in Common Crawl’s latest crawl. Turkish is 1.1%, Ukrainian 0.8%, Persian 0.6% and Bengali 0.1%. Grammar in those languages is learnable from a small sample because it repeats on every page. The long tail of each language, its proverbs and regional customs and local paperwork, is spread across far fewer pages.

Benchmarks written natively by other teams hit the same wall. MultiNRC, built from questions written by native speakers, found no model out of 14 scoring above 50%, and its authors point to “persistent challenges with culturally grounded knowledge.” Companies seem to be reaching the same conclusion: ElevenLabs’ initiative to acquire Kotoba for its Japanese voice push is a bet that local depth gets bought, not waited for.

Test what the model remembers. A model that writes flawless Ukrainian grammar can still miss a comparison every Ukrainian knows. Build your evaluation from the questions your users actually ask: the regional terms, the local regulations, the sayings.

Retrieve what it can’t remember. Mallen’s team found retrieval-augmented models “largely outperform” models orders of magnitude larger on long-tail questions. Put the dictionary, the statute and the local documents in the context.

According to recent reports, spend the reasoning budget on rule work. Date conversion, formatting, morphology and calculation benefit from more thinking. Local facts and idioms mostly don’t, and there the extra tokens buy confident wrong answers.

Treat a fluent long-tail answer as unverified. Fluency is the first thing models learn in a language, which makes it the weakest sign that an answer is correct.

Recent findings suggest that my bet, at about 75%: the next round of frontier releases raises the grammar and calendar scores on these questions by more than it raises the idiom scores. If a model closes the idiom gap through reasoning alone, without more native text in its training data, I’ll have been wrong.

Nour Abouskhroun is the founder of Whizi, a multi-model AI workspace, and builds its public benchmarks of AI models, including a 150-question test written natively in 30 languages.
