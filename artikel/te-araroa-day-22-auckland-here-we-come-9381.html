Te Araroa day 22: Auckland here we come!

27.5km, Mangawhai trail host to Pakiri Paradise.

It has been revealed that most of us (me, Lindsay, Sinead, Annabel and Anica) got going at around 7am again, although this time with good reason as we were expecting a bit of a longer day and needed to make a tidal river crossing later. The place we were staying was run by people who had previously done TA and provided everything hikers could need, at the very reasonable price of $10 per night. It was a great place to stay, and we walked out of the gate practically right back onto trail. We had been talking about coffee, but didn’t think that anything would be open at that time. But luck was on our side! Literally 50 metres down the street there was a coffee and smoothie van. What a great start to the day. While I was at university I was very much fuelled by coffee, but since then I’ve mostly switched to decaf. But I have been enjoying drinking it again while I’ve been hiking – it gives me a great energy boost, which puts me in a good mood, and I think the negative side effects I’ve experienced previously are reduced by having physical activity to funnel that energy into. I found a friend when I went to pack away my tent.

Coffee obtained, it was going to be a good morning!

Unfortunately, the day started with a decent road walk of about 7km, which I don’t think any of us had quite registered was coming. Eventually we made it to the beach we would be walking along for the greater part of the day. The signage was familiar to me and I didn’t quite realise why until I registered that it was Auckland Council signage. We had made it to Auckland region! The Northland/Auckland boundary is just south of Mangawhai so that morning, we had completed the first region of Te Araroa. Northland is one of the longest (perhaps the longest?) regions on TA, owing to its long and thin geography. So this was a significant milestone. It was also probably the region I was least looking forward to, yet I loved it. Not every minute, of course, but distance had made my heart grow fonder and I was able to appreciate the beauty of the place with fresh eyes. (Related: Course Launch Community Event...)

Industry observers note that the beach looked pleasingly short when we got onto it. That is, until I realised that the headland I could see was not the end of the beach section, but just a blip a few kilometres in. Still, the first section went pretty quickly and soon we were approaching the headland. There were hoards of surfers and foil board surfers, which looked pretty cool! There was also an interesting set up on the beach. Evidently a photoshoot was going on – a bed was set up, and a model posed on it in pajamas while a drone hovered and various involved people fluttered around. I’m sure the six dirty hikers walking through the background didn’t improve their shots!

The first part of the beach, looking towards Te Arai point.

Up on Te Arai Point, there were awesome views along the coast. The tracks weren’t super well-marked though, and the route was a bit un-intuitive. I was with Lindsay and Sinead at this point, and eventually we reached a wooded section that popped out on a road back to the beach. Just before the road we stumbled (almost literally) upon Zoe, who was taking a break in what she pointed out might be the last shade for a while. We plopped down to join her, and collectively decided that the rest of the beach necessitated podcasts. I realised I had forgotten to download more episodes when I last had wifi, and scrambled around for something to listen to. I tried an audiobook but I wasn’t really getting into it, and stopped to switch to music and try to find the specific song I had in my head that morning (u + me = <3 by Olivia Rodrigo, in case you were wondering). Tunes acquired, I set off again.

According to recent reports, this beach was actually perhaps the most annoying we have had to walk down so far. The tide was fully in at this point and the available area to walk on was soft, slopey, and narrow in places. There was one spot where the waves had created a bit of a cliff about 75cm tall, with very little space between the top of the cliff and the dunes behind. My feet were already wet from a stream crossing earlier, so I just decided to commit and walk in the water below the cliff. Apparently some of the others had taken their chances with the top of the cliff, with fairly unsuccessful results. It was a shame they were behind me, as the descriptions they gave of their falls were pretty amusing!

Walking over Te Arai point, with the surfers in the background.

Sinéad heading to the tidal crossing.

The other notable thing about the beach was the number of dead birds we came across. I really haven’t seen that many before. I hope it isn’t bird flu, which has recently arrived in NZ – perhaps it’s just something to do with the ocean currents that lots of dead stuff washes up there?

It has been revealed that at about 11:45 I was hungry. I was going to grab a snack and keep going, but realised I might as well stop for lunch. Who says lunch can’t happen before 12, anyway? I was hungry so I might as well eat properly. I enjoyed my wraps with fresh salad, although I kept dropping things that then got covered in sand. Annabel had told me about these sun-dried tomatoes that come without oil, and I had bought some in Mangawhai. They were great, 10/10 reccommend those. While I was lunching, the others all passed me. After a few more kilometres of walking, I came across everyone sat on the beach eating their (late) lunch. We were nearly at the tidal estuary, which the trail notes mentioned could be crossed 90 minutes either side of low tide. It was 2pm, and low tide wasn’t until nearly 5. But the others reckoned someone else had crossed outside of the reccommended window, and we decided to go and have a look.

Recent findings suggest that the butterflies were having a good dance in my stomach as we approached the estuary. I find rivers quite scary, especially when I don’t know how deep they’ll be. We went to the mouth, as that’s where the smaller streams on the beach had been shallowest. But the water here looked quite fast-flowing, so we went further up to a wider, slower bend. It was impossible to tell how deep it was, but at least we wouldn’t get swept away. Sinéad offered to be the guinea pig. She went up to her ankles, then her knees – and then she was basically across. No problem! We all followed without difficulty. Sometimes the trail notes really overstate the difficulty of a thing. I guess it can be deeper sometimes – but we crossed 3 hours before low tide with no bother, when the notes suggested 90 minutes. One of the most valuable pieces of advice I got about TA, which I tell to everyone, came from a couple of NOBO hikers that I picked up near Nelson Lakes last season. They remarked that there is a lot of scare-mongering from other hikers, but you have to see for yourself and not put too much stock in what everyone else is saying. And apparently the same goes for the trail notes, too!

The crossing was so fine! Zoe wading across with me, Lindsay, Annabel and Anica waiting. Photo by Sinéad.

Putting shoes and socks back on after the beach.

Sources indicate that we walked up the beach towards a holiday park. We were planning to go a bit further for the day but hoped they would sell us some cold drinks, and maybe fill some water bottles. It was a bit unclear where to go, but there was a man mowing the grass. ‘You lot look like an invading army!’ he called down to us. I suppose the six of us did look like we were plotting how to storm the castle! He invited us to come through the campground and rinse out our shoes and socks. After my sandpaper socks the other day, this was a very welcome invitation. He also said that the notes don’t give good advice about where to cross the river we had just been over. It’s much shallower inland, but the area is home to rare nesting birds, so DOC won’t allow the TA trust to tell people to cross further inland. Personally, I’m happy to wait a bit for the tides if it means not disturbing the birds. But I can see that it’s frustrating for the locals to see people being given less-good safety advice.

It has been revealed that after washing our feet, we all headed over to the shop where we bought ice creams and cold drinks, and checked out the next bit of the route. Everyone kept saying it was really steep, but for some reason I didn’t think it looked too bad on the map. Then I decided to look at a different map app, the one I normally use, and the contour lines looked way denser! I realised that the contours on the TA app are really light and narrow compared to the ones on the map I’m used to, so things don’t look as steep. I guess that could be helpful, in some ways.

It has been revealed that after a while, we headed off down the road. It was a fairly quiet road, and it wasn’t too long before we reached some track. The hill began immediately, and it was steep but not impossible. I ended up passing the others at the first flattish bit and continued on up. The views were pretty good and I felt happy about my fitness. I had a full 4 day resupply on my back but I found the hill ok, and I was able to keep plugging on without having to stop too often. Towards the top, there was a large fallen tree that I had to scramble over. Then I realised I was at the turn off for where we would be staying, and stopped to wait for the others. I watched them all clamber over the tree with varying levels of elegance. I would have taken photos but I didn’t want to walk back down the hill.

You can just see the others lower down the hill.

The house we were staying at was a couple of hundred metres along a side-track. Annabel and I got there first, and the couple came out and introduced themselves, and asked where we were from. Annabel is also from southern England, to which the guy responded that we were both ‘bloody Southerners’; he’s from Yorkshire! The rest of the group arrived and they showed us where we could pitch our tents. It wasn’t as luxurious as the previous night, but we had everything we needed. And, they showed us the view of Auckland city from their garden! This was the first time we had seen the city, and although it still looked very far away, it was cool to know we were getting closer. Everyone was planning to take some time off or visit friends in Auckland, and it’s also the first major city on the route. So it seems to have become a big milestone in everyone’s minds.

You can’t see it, but trust me when I say that the Auckland city skyline was visible in this view.

The weather was threatening rain so we pitched our tents, which were soaked from the morning dew still. I managed to snag a decent spot, but some of the others were less lucky and Annabel realised she had pitched her tent in a bit of a bog. Our hosts came out with a packet of scorched almonds, which was very nice of them! A little while later, Xavier showed up. He was at the campsite the previous night, but I didn’t end up speaking to him. Some of the others knew him from before, but I hadn’t met him so I took the chance to introduce myself. Then we carefully prepared dinner on the tables and chairs that kept sinking into the mud, and sat around talking for a while before calling it a night.
