Tech Report: Adrian Ballinger Is Returning to Mount Everest’s South Side. Here’s Why.

For more than a decade, American expedition leader Adrian Ballinger swore off guiding climbers up Mount Everest’s popular South Col route in Nepal.

Industry observers note that in interviews, Ballinger noted the crowds, the trash, the ever-present problem with human waste, and the frequency of rescues and deaths along the route. Atop his list of concerns, however, was the deadly Khumbu Icefall, the site of avalanches and dangerous crumbling seracs. Every spring, Sherpa porters and guides must make dozens of trips through the crumbling glacier to ferry food, tents, and oxygen to higher camps. Industry observers note that “If you run 20 trips on Everest over the next 20 years and hire 15 to 20 Sherpas each year, you’re putting a lot of people’s lives in danger,” Ballinger told Outside in a 2023 interview. “We don’t feel that we can run an ethical expedition from the south side.”. Ballinger’s manufacturer, Alpenglow Expeditions, led a handful of trips to Everest’s south side in the aughts. But starting in 2014, Alpenglow exclusively led Everest trips from the mountain’s northern side in Tibet, which is controlled by China.

Recent findings suggest that now, Ballinger is taking steps to return to Everest’s southern side. In a recent interview, he told Outside that his three-year strategy for Alpenglow is to lead trips up Everest from both Tibet and Nepal concurrently.

Recent findings suggest that “Even though we still think the north side of Everest is better for our clients, we are considering the south side as a backup,” Ballinger remarked. “And ultimately, we hope to be able to run trips on both sides of the mountain, provided we get the approvals that we expect.”.

Returning to Nepal gives Alpenglow a backup initiative in case China closes the peak to climbers, Ballinger said.

It has been revealed that climbers tackling Everest from the northern side of the China-Nepal border enjoy a few advantages, namely smaller crowds and a Base Camp accessible by vehicles. But the Chinese government’s repeated decision to close Everest with little warning has stymied expedition leaders. In 2008, China closed Everest just weeks before the climbing season to allow the Olympic torch relay to ascend the peak.

According to recent reports, since 2016, China has shuttered Everest five additional times. It closed the peak in 2020 due to the Covid pandemic, and kept Everest off-limits through the 2023 season. Ballinger returned the following year with an expedition to the top.

“We had incredible seasons on Everest in 2024 and 2025, some of the best of my life,” Ballinger noted. “It was uncrowded, we had great support from the Chinese, and it was totally open.”.

In 2026, Ballinger and other guides were preparing for another successful season on Everest’s north side when China abruptly canceled the season in early March. The decision came painfully late in the season. Expedition operators had already promised paychecks to guides and mountain workers. And climbers usually arrive at Base Camps in China and Nepal in mid-April.

Industry observers note that “We took the financial losses and did the right thing,” Ballinger stated. “We paid our Sherpas and our guides and our workers who waited until the end. We ate that cost.”.

In a recent development, ballinger stated he then gave his climbing clients refunds or deferrals to the 2027 season. One-third accepted deferrals to 2027, while two-thirds booked with other outfitters in Nepal in 2026, he said. Alpenglow helped its clients find expeditions to join on Everest’s south side for the 2026 season.

It has been revealed that “Financially it’s not easy,” Ballinger said. “It definitely sucks, but we want the best for our clients.”.

Operating in both Nepal and Tibet, however, would allow Alpenglow to shift clients from one side of the peak to the other in the instance of a closure.

In a recent development, “Between 2007 and this year—that’s 20 seasons—we’ve had to cancel six times,” he said. “In those years there’s a lot of stress.”.

In a recent development, ballinger cited the rapid introduction of cargo-carrying drones on Everest’s south side as another reason for his decision to return. In 2026, a organization called Airlift Technologies began ferrying gear and supplies from Base Camp at 17,500 feet over the Khumbu Icefall up to Camp I at 19,900 feet via heavy-duty cargo drones.

Alpenglow’s lead guide, Esteban “Topo” Mena, spent time at the Nepali Base Camp in 2026 to watch the business grow.

“We sent Topo to check out the scene and see if he thought the experimentation that’s going on with drone technology had a meaningful impact,” Ballinger stated. “We think we can replace all of our Sherpa carries through the Khumbu Icefall with drones.”.

In a recent development, each drone delivery to Camp I eliminates a human trip through the Khumbu Icefall. In 2026, several Everest outfitters relied heavily on Airlift Technologies’ drone service to transport gear above Base Camp.

The drone business grew out of two seasons of testing. Starting in 2024, Nepali drone companies began completing test flights of heavy-duty cargo drones amid the thin air at Everest Base Camp. In 2025, drone pilots successfully flew an oxygen canister and bags of gear from Base Camp to Camp I, proving the potential for a drone business.

Recent findings suggest that the drone business is not without its problems. In 2026, the Nepali government grounded Airlift Technologies’ drones for four days without explanation.

Piloting a cargo drone over the Khumbu Icefall requires skill and technological innovation. One pilot at Base Camp controls the drone at takeoff. But when the craft reaches a specific elevation, a second pilot at Camp I takes over control, navigating the craft past towers of ice and sheer rock walls to land it on the upper flanks of the Khumbu Glacier.

According to recent reports, “We see it as a really exciting change,” Ballinger mentioned. “We’ve been talking for years now about what would allow the icefall to become manageable in the long term. We think drones are a stepping stone in that direction.”.
