Recent findings suggest that over the years, corporations and filmmakers alike have eagerly sought to revisit the origins of horror classics (e.g., Bates Motel and Psycho, Welcome to Derry and IT, etc.) for modern audiences. It’s an easy way to find successwhen you already have an audience that loves the IP you’re reimagining.

Not even the Friday the 13th franchise has escaped the trend of revisiting the past. Following the release of the genre-defining slasher Halloween, Cunningham and writer Victor Miller faced a real challenge: how to stand out in a subgenre flooded with horror movies (Black Christmas, Texas Chainsaw Massacre, to name just a few) trying to ride the slasher wave. Friday the 13th had to be memorable, interesting, and stand out. (Related: Jimmy John’s reveals Christmas Vacation meal with exclu...)

It has been revealed that on the surface, Friday the 13th doesn’t feel too different. It’s full of horny teens, heavy suspense, and a sense of isolation as a deranged killer hunts and kills them off, one by one. However, unlike other slashers like Halloween, Cunningham takes the brutality one step further by asking two very different but meaningful questions. Careful now, spoilers for a 46-year-old movie ahead.

According to recent reports, first, what if the killer were not a man in a mask but a vengeful mother driven mad by society’s disregard for her disabled son? Second, what if Cunningham and Paramount hired Tom Savini, famous for his work on George A. Romero’s Dawn of the Dead, to create some of the most gruesome deaths audiences had yet seen on the silver screen?

The result is one of the bloodiest slashers of all time, one that shook up perceptions of how grotesque the horror subgenre could be and who, exactly, could be the one dealing out those kills in the first place.

Slashers like Halloween and Black Christmas have some gnarly kills—who could forget Michael Myers pinning Bob to a pantry door with a large knife or Barb being stabbed with a unicorn figurine’s horn? Savini, however, elevated Friday the 13th’s kills into a bloody art form with shocking, realistic gore.

Industry observers note that savini did more than deliver, too. Just as he used practical effects for Dawn of the Dead, he did so again for several of the film’s kills. One especially memorable kill is counselor Jack’s, in which he’s stabbed in the neck as he lies in bed. Using a prosthetic neck piece and a pump, fake blood was pumped to create a blood-spurt effect that, for its time, looked remarkably realistic.

The most significant thing that set Friday the 13th apart from its predecessors, however, was its killer: Pamela Voorhees. The slasher genre was no longer defined by masculine rage, but by a mother’s love. Even in the modern era of horror, slashers still feature far more male than female killers, but in 1980, Mrs. Voorhees was undoubtedly one of a kind.

Furthermore, for as deranged and murderous as she was, she exerted a strange, empathetic pull on audiences. Yes, murder is wrong — but you can’t deny that she loved her son. Unlike Michael Myers, who is often depicted as evil incarnate, Mrs. Voorhees has a complexity that made her brutal motives more compelling.

According to recent reports, 46 years later, Mrs. Voorhees's story begins anew with Crystal Lake. It’s unclear whether the series will get a second season, but even if it doesn’t, fans of Jason and his diabolical family tree needn’t worry. You can never go wrong with the original.
