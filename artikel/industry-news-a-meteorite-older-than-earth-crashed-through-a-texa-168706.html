Industry News: A meteorite older than Earth crashed through a Texas woman’s roof and landed near her daughter’s bed

The 4.5-billion-year-old meteorite that pierced a home in Spring, Texas is now on free display at the Houston Museum of Natural Science.

According to recent reports, on the afternoon of March 21, 2026, Sherrie James heard a thunderous boom at her home in Spring, Texas, north of Houston. A rock from space had just come through her roof.

Eight months later, that rock is on display at the Houston Museum of Natural Science, in a free exhibition called Fall To Earth that opened on November 6.

Industry observers note that on the day it fell, it pierced an upstairs bedroom and came to rest near her daughter’s bed. It was a meteorite about 4.5 billion years old, dating back to the birth of the solar system. (Related: Breaking: Porting fairseq wmt19 translation system to t...)

James was not the only one who noticed. People across Texas heard a low boom and felt their houses shake, under clear skies, according to the museum. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

Recent findings suggest that NASA’s analysis says the meteor became visible 49 miles above Stagecoach, northwest of Houston, at 4:40 p.m. Central Daylight Time. It moved southeast at about 35,000 miles per hour and broke apart 29 miles above Bammel, just west of Cypress Station.

In a recent development, the space rock that entered the atmosphere weighed about a ton and measured about 3 feet across, NASA estimates. When it broke apart, it released energy equal to 26 tons of TNT. That created a pressure wave that reached the ground and caused the booms.

More than 180 eyewitnesses reported the fireball, from as far away as Dallas-Fort Worth, Austin and Rockport, the museum noted.

According to NASA, the Geostationary Lightning Mappers on the GOES weather satellites also detected it, and Doppler weather radar revealed meteorites falling to the ground between Willowbrook and Northgate Crossing.

Radar has helped track falls before, as researchers showed when they found that weather radar systems can detect meteorite falls.

Recent findings suggest that at first, nobody knew what had hit the house. The Ponderosa Fire Department initially looked into whether the object might be debris from an aircraft.

It has been revealed that its unusual weight and appearance soon pointed to something else. Most of the original space rock burned up before it reached the ground, but some pieces survived.

By late October 2026, 19 meteorite fragments had been recovered around the Houston area, according to the museum.

Researchers at Rice University have proposed naming the meteorite “Houston” as part of its formal classification, which still needs approval from the Meteoritical Society.

According to recent reports, it is not the first time a meteorite has hit a home in the United States. A similar rock crashed into a man’s house in Georgia.

Recent findings suggest that within 24 hours of the fall, experts and enthusiasts were finding small chunks on public property, Rice University stated. That is rare: meteorites seldom land in a major city, and samples are seldom collected so soon after landing.

Rice scientist Bidong Zhang walked 23,000 steps searching but found no samples himself. Two other searchers, David Gonzales and Marc Fries, found several pieces and donated them to Rice.

Those samples became the first meteorite tested and catalogued by Zhang’s new Rice Astromaterials Research and Exploration lab, known as RARE.

The analysis confirmed that the Houston meteorite is a chondrite, a type of stony meteorite. Its oxygen isotopes matched what scientists call high-grade H chondrites.

But there was a mystery. Chondrites usually contain small round grains called chondrules, and the Houston meteorite had none.

The scientists found clues in a mineral called feldspar, which was in an unusual sodium-rich form. That suggests the rock was heated to nearly 1,100 degrees Celsius (about 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit) while it was still part of its parent asteroid, hot enough to melt the chondrules.

At some point after that, a high-speed impact likely broke up the asteroid and set the meteorite free. “We think this meteorite was impacted in space,” Zhang said. “It truly is a unique specimen.”.

Some results are still unexplained. The levels of one oxygen isotope varied within the samples, when scientists would normally expect them to be fairly consistent, and the team notes more work is needed.

Industry observers note that the museum calls it one of the most remarkable, documented meteorite falls in recent Texas history. Because the rock dates to the birth of the solar system, it offers scientists a rare chance to study material older than Earth itself.

Sources indicate that “A 4.5-billion-year-old fragment of our solar system’s original formation crashed through the roof of a Houston-area home,” said Carolyn Sumners, vice president of astronomy at the Houston Museum of Natural Science. “That’s not something you expect to happen in your own neighborhood.”.

Industry observers note that james sees it in a more personal way. “That little thing has traveled by planets and stars, there’s no telling how far it’s come,” she remarked. “So, it’s very exciting. I just feel blessed it came to see me.”.

Fall To Earth is free and open to the public at the museum, at 5555 Hermann Park Drive in Houston.
