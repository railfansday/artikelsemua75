📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, U.S. investors are facing a meltdown in tech stocks and the possible end of the AI boom, but the bubble has already popped in China, where an even worse market crash threatens the economy and President Xi Jinping, according to Yardeni Research.

China’s tech-heavy STAR 50 index has tumbled 44% from its high this summer, while the Nasdaq is down 18% from its record—even after U.S. stocks suffered their worst week since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. (Related: Summer at Hugging Face...)

That’s thrown a wrench into Xi’s aims for the economy, Yardeni noted in a note earlier this month. While the Chinese leader was riding high as host to global leaders this week, the market’s downturn jeopardizes his plan to turn China’s savers into stock investors who can finance the AI buildout and de-emphasize the property market.

Recent findings suggest that real estate accounts for roughly 70% of household wealth in China and was traditionally a place to stash savings. But the housing crash has weighed on consumer demand, even producing intermittent deflation. Business investment is also falling, with October data showing property investment sank nearly 22%.

With the domestic market still weak, Beijing has instead relied on a flood of cheap exports to keep the economy growing while also deploying the “national team” of state-backed banks to pump money into the stock market.

But according to Yardeni, Chinese stocks collapsed this year due in part to cutthroat competition, heavier tax burdens, higher energy costs, tariffs, and the risk that China’s trading partners will block exports being sold at ultra-low prices. (Related: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Faster Language M...)

“To keep AI funding flowing, Xi needs a buoyant stock market,” the note mentioned. “Otherwise, AI risks being folded into the state-owned enterprise (SOE) universe that his team has pledged to shrink since 2013, further crowding out the private sector.”.

In a recent development, retail investors are also skittish about Chinese stocks after Xi’s government cracked down on the tech sector in 2020. The market’s reputation as a rigged casino that favors politically connected insiders doesn’t help either. In fact, the Shanghai Composite remains well below its October 2007 peak despite China’s GDP exploding nearly sixfold.

Many households now have “financial PTSD” after repeated stock busts, including a crash in the summer of 2015 that required an aggressive national team rescue, Yardeni added.

To be sure, China remains a leader in AI, and mom-and-pop investors still drive most of the trade, even as they treat the market more like high-stakes gambling than a place to grow retirement savings.

Recent findings suggest that “Yet this is a moment of real danger for Xi. A bursting AI bubble would not merely erase wealth or dent China’s technological prestige. It would weaken Beijing’s attempt to replace a broken property model with a stock-market-led one,” Yardeni warned.

“China can build world-class AI models, train engineers, and spend billions on chips. But if its households still view Shanghai as a casino with odds set by the Politburo, Xi’s dream of creating a Chinese version of Wall Street may remain just that: a dream.”.

Underscoring its importance to Beijing, Premier Li Qiang told a State Council executive meeting earlier this month that stabilizing capital markets was the top priority for the rest of the year.

It followed securities regulators sharply easing requirements for firm buybacks that help lift stock prices and discouraging IPOs and large share sales to prevent stock dilution that triggers selloffs.

Recent findings suggest that but the spy scandal that dethroned Jensen Huang as CEO of Nvidia represents a fresh risk to China’s AI ambitions. Backlash over the advanced U.S. chips and computing capacity that China had access to has spurred the Trump administration to impose new technology curbs.

“By targeting both indirect chip shipments to China and its access to overseas computing power, the new controls threaten to slow the catch-up of China’s leading AI models and to slow China’s AI rollout,” analysts at Capital Economics wrote on Tuesday.
