Industry observers note that the dystopian genre started taking shape around the early 20th century as a response to the political revolutions, widespread industrialization, and rising totalitarianism of the time, soon becoming one of the most prominent subgenres of science fiction. As such, it's no surprise that dystopian sci-fi has been one of television's most interesting genres for years, producing highlights as fascinating as Black Mirror.

Recent findings suggest that one of the greatest anthology series since The Twilight Zone, Black Mirror began its life as a widely acclaimed Channel 4 series in the UK, until moving to Netflix for its third season and rapidly becoming an international pop culture sensation. But as enrapturing, intelligent, and thematically nuanced as its dystopian elements may be, there are 10 particular shows which take dystopia even further. They're even darker, even more reflexive on the future of humanity, and even more harrowing examples of the power of this genre.

Richard K. Morgan's 2002 novel Altered Carbon is a modern classic of hard-boiled cyberpunk noir, easily one of the best of its subgenre as far as 2000s sci-fi literature goes. As such, expectations were high when a Netflix TV show adaptation was declared. Even though it's widely agreed that only the first of the show's two seasons really lived up to the source material's quality, this is nevertheless one of the most compelling dystopian sci-fi shows in recent memory. Those visual effects are in service of a vibrantly realized transhumanist dystopia that explores ideas of immortality, class, and technology's erasure of human identity in ways more than complex enough to satisfy the fans of Black Mirror's greatest dystopian episodes. According to recent reports, after making Better Call Saul, television history's greatest prequel and a series which managed to live up to a predecessor that many consider the best show of all time, the bar was set sky-high for whatever Vince Gilligan decided to do next. In the end, it was a post-apocalyptic alien invasion series starring Rhea Seehorn, Pluribus; and so far, it has definitely lived up to the hype.

It has been revealed that it's one of the best sci-fi shows since 2020, and it constructs its dystopian concept out of what may at first seem like a utopia: a world where there's no war, hunger, or violence, but which strips humanity of individual freedom and free will. It's such a layered and masterfully slow-burning depiction of a dystopia that there would be no way for any one episode of any anthology series to compete.

In a recent development, one of the best cyberpunk anime series of all time, Texhnolyze is a surrealistic, experimental show directed by Hiroshi Hamasaki. Its story deals with the decaying underground city of Lux, where a stoic prize fighter is fitted with cybernetic limbs and dragged into a brutal territorial war for the city's survival. The result? Some of the most philosophically dense cyberpunk storytelling that the small screen has seen throughout the 21st century.

Where most dystopias rely on some kind of oppressive regime or overarching power to create conflict and tension, Texhnolyze does something different by setting its story in a world that has already rotted well past the point of having any need for a functioning system, including tyranny. Without any structure to bring the viewer any sort of comforting familiarity, it studies a dying human race in all sorts of engaging ways.

It has been revealed that based on the eponymous trilogy of novels by Hugh Howey — one of the best dystopian book trilogies of all time — Silo is proof that when it comes to streaming platforms, none are producing sci-fi series more consistently high-quality and fascinating than Apple TV. With its tremendous production values and a tour-de-force leading turn by Rebecca Ferguson, it's a must-see for any fan of the dystopian genre.

Much like many of Black Mirror's standout episodes, Silo also deals with how the powers that be manipulate humanity, but it approaches that angle by studying how society's entire history and reality can be manufactured from the ground up, making for a particularly thought-provoking premise. Like all the best dystopian sci-fi reveals, Silo is able to make any viewer question their own reality.

Russell T Davies has long been one of the kings of British television, constantly pushing the envelope with demonstrates as inventive and fascinating as the BBC-HBO co-production Years and Years. It's one of the best BBC sci-fi shows to rewatch on streaming, and what makes it so special is that, instead of exploring a dystopian distant future, it takes place between the years of 2019 and 2034.

Sources indicate that by having its narrative kick off in the modern day, and then following a real-time domestic collapse as it becomes a true authoritarian dystopia (one which society seems to simply sleepwalk into), Years and Years proves that there are plenty of original ways to approach this genre. For people whose favorite part of dystopian reveals is sociopolitical complexity, this is definitely essential viewing.

Recent findings suggest that there are plenty of remarkably dark anime shows that are definitely not for kids, and Ergo Proxy is one of the best of the 21st century so far. Directed by Shūkō Murase and inspired by philosophical ideas like Gnosticism, it's set in a post-apocalyptic world where humanity survives inside isolated cities managed by god-like beings. There, a group uncovers a dark conspiracy surrounding the return of the planet's original human creators.

Whereas many dystopian stories see the discovery of the truth as a form of liberation, Ergo Proxy builds a world so dystopian that the characters' discovery of its hidden truths actually triggers existential crises. It's a fascinating concept, one that dives deep into the very concept of what makes us human and interrogates whether existential purpose is but an illusion.

Alex Garland kicked off his career as a novelist, later becoming a successful Hollywood screenwriter and then making the ambitious jump to the director's chair. Then, just six years ago, he created, wrote, and directed his very first small-screen production: the miniseries Devs, one of those thriller miniseries that are absolutely perfect and deserve to be watched by anyone who loves the genre.

Recent findings suggest that supported by an exceptional cast and a brief eight-episode length that makes it abundantly easy to watch over the course of a single weekend, Devs explores themes like free will and determinism in uniquely nuanced ways. It's a techno-thriller unlike any other, one that dares to wonder what would happen if fate were inevitable and technology were capable of proving such a thing.

As great as they are at producing great sci-fi reveals, there are arguably no Apple TV sci-fi series better than Severance. Every generation seems to get its own pop-culturally massive mystery box sci-fi series that refuses to answer gripping questions; and just like people in the 2000s got Lost, and people in the 2010s got Dark, today's TV viewers are being treated to Severance.

It's one of the best sci-fi mystery demonstrates of not just the last decade, but arguably all time. It's a show that sometimes feels remarkably similar to Black Mirror in a few key ways, but all in all, it takes the concept of dystopia substantially further. By exploring a theme as timely as how corporate capitalism seeks to eradicate individuality and the human soul, it crafts a masterpiece that's terrifying just as often as it's surprisingly humorous.

Recent findings suggest that there are plenty of great sci-fi reveals where humanity is doomed. That is, after all, what dystopian science fiction is all about at its core. However, in few dystopian masterpieces does humanity feel more doomed than in The Handmaid's Tale, a bona fide modern classic based on one of the 20th century's greatest dystopian novels, Margaret Atwood's book of the same name.

Industry observers note that if Black Mirror is a cautionary tale against the dangers of technology and humanity's relationship with it, The Handmaid's Tale proposes a dystopian world where the scariness of the future comes not from technology, but from societal structures that actually exist right now: the patriarchy, ecological chaos, and people in power twisting religion to keep people in check. Though Atwood herself rejects the idea of labeling Handmaid's Tale as sci-fi, it's widely accepted by the rest of the genre's fans, and that makes this one of the best sci-fi demonstrates of the 2010s.

Industry observers note that A dystopian series titled Utopia feels like a dark twist of ironic humor, and that is exactly the kind of masterpiece that this British cult classic is. A conspiracy thriller unlike any other that the small screen has ever seen, Utopia follows a group of online comic book fans who go on the run after acquiring the unpublished manuscript of The Utopia Experiments, a cult classic graphic novel rumored to have predicted the worst global catastrophes of the past century.

According to recent reports, though the series was controversial back in the day for its extreme graphic violence and refusal to put any makeup on its treatment of taboo topics, Utopia is nevertheless one of the best-ever sci-fi shows shorter than 20 episodes. It's precisely through its intentionally radical and subversive nature that it takes the concept of dystopia further than any TV show in history, weaponizing its themes and graphic violence to constantly keep the viewer on edge.
