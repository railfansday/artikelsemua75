One serial swearing offender, Jacqui Lambie, says it wouldn’t be an Australian parliament ‘without a few swear words thrown in’.

When Liberal MP Garth Hamilton stormed out of parliament in June, slamming his hands on his desk and calling out “fucking bullshit”, the speaker of the house, Milton Dick, called it “a latest low” in federal politics.

Dick may have been on to something.

Newly introduced analysis from Guardian Australia has found profanity and foul language has risen significantly in Australia’s parliament over the past 20 years, with some of today’s most prominent politicians among the chief culprits for swearing. Before the year is even out, 2026 is the worst year on record for the use of swear words and language deemed “unparliamentary”. That number has risen by 78% since 2006. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Analysis of Hansard debate and procedure records has found that per million words spoken in parliament this year, approximately 37 have been profane – ranging from milder (“bloody”, “damn” and “crap”) to language considered strong, or even extreme.

While entitled to “parle hard”, language deemed unparliamentary by the speaker or Senate president is required to be withdrawn, or else MPs face ejection from the chamber.

Beyond the impact of pushing against regulation and etiquette, there is something to be remarked for the currency that outbursts gain when clipped and shared online – recently demonstrated by Greens senator Peter Whish-Wilson, who called for the country to “wake the fuck up” to One Nation’s use of social media algorithms in June.

But despite the politicians reaching for profanities at a record rate, Dick – the Labor MP who has been speaker of the house since 2022 – said he felt the tone and tenor of debate in parliament has gotten better in his time in the chair.

“Debate is fierce and strong and, apart from a few incidents, it has improved,” he said.

“Maybe it hasn’t appeared so because of the volume [of swearing], but when you analyse the words used … there are outbreaks of course, but I believe in everyone showing respect.”.

And, to be fair, most of parliament’s most swear-y politicians are in the Senate, not the house.

Industry observers note that described as “the great Australian adjective” in 1894 by the Bulletin magazine, “bloody” appears to have suffered no fall from grace in the subsequent years, serving as the most commonly used swear word in parliament. Along with “damn”, “crap” and “bugger”, mild curses constitute 92% of all recorded bad language, with more severe terms such as “prick”, “shit” and “piss” used more sparingly.

Recent findings suggest that excluded from this figure are rare cases of colourful and creative terms that have been called for withdrawal. Harnessing the speaker’s unilateral power to flag unacceptable language, terms such as “bin chickens – used by Julian Hill, the MP for Bruce, in 2022 to describe the opposition in the house – have been ruled as unparliamentary.

Recent findings suggest that much of this potty-talk comes from the Senate, which since 2006 has seen a 177% increase in the use of bad language. Among active parliamentary participants (those who have spoken at least 100,000 words in this period), Bill Heffernan was the lead offender. Despite speaking just 141,000 words between 2006 and 2016, the former Liberal senator had an expletive use-rate of 877 per million words spoken, 43 times the rate of all other parliamentarians.

It has been revealed that jacqui Lambie is the second-most sweary politician, responsible for 6% of total swearing in parliament at a rate of 406 per million words spoken – more than 19 times the average. But, like most of the politicians on this list, Lambie’s swearing is overwhelmingly mild – mostly words like “crap” or “bloody”.

According to recent reports, “I try really hard not to swear, especially in the Senate chamber. It upsets my mum, she doesn’t even like me saying ‘bloody’,” Lambie said when we came to her with the statistics.

It has been revealed that “It’s important to be respectful. But I am a straight shooter and sometimes things are just crap – and it’s hard not to say so. I don’t think it would be an Australian parliament without a few swear words thrown in.”.

According to recent reports, not all occurrences of swearing in parliamentary records are “own-use” or, rather, swearing with intent. Quotes, such as reading out abuse a politician has received, or a quote from a document, account for some of it.

However, when excluding these cases our analysis indicates that own-use swearing in parliament has nearly tripled over the last 20 years, from 6.4 per million words in 2006-9 to 18.9 in 2022-5.

Broken down by party, One Nation are the worst offenders, swearing for the sake of it at 7.4 times the rate of Labor – perhaps unsurprising, given that the party leader, Pauline Hanson, ranks fourth among discursively active politicians for use of foul language.

Dick said he had strived to improve the tone of discussion in parliament, believing that greater diversity in politics and in the chamber – with more women, more First Nations MPs and people from multicultural backgrounds – had also changed dynamics of debate.

Asked about recent heated moments in parliament, he said he recalled only two major moments in the lower house in recent years: Hamilton’s June storm-out and fellow Liberal MP Dan Tehan’s demand to “cut the crap” in August.

“I want to ensure, among our people and our democracy, that we fight hard to disagree more civilly … But I’m a substantial believer in upholding traditions and standards. When things get a bit heated, I’ll take firm action,” Dick stated.

In a recent development, politicians’ speeches from both houses of parliament were analysed for the years 2006 to 2026. For each speech, the number of swearwords were counted using the these categories:.

Mild: bloody, damn, crap, bugger, bastard, bulldust, arse, mongrel, boofhead, bollocks.

Strong: prick, shit, bullshit, piss, piss off, dickhead, arsehole, shithouse, wanker.

Extreme: fucking, cunt, fuck, fuck off, fucker, fucked, motherfucker.

To control for politicians’ tenure length and verbosity affecting swearing totals, swearing numbers were converted to a rate per million words by dividing the total swearwords by the total number of words spoken for that politician in that year, or for the total time period.

To separate swearing in a politician’s own voice from quotes, idioms and literal uses, an LLM was used to read the speech text surrounding each swearword and sort these into categories. A random sample of the results were manually checked, to achieve an agreement of 97% on whether a use was the speaker’s own. Therefore these figures are approximate.
