Mary Gauthier has always had the innate ability to make her listeners confront some hard truths in her songs, often without them realizing they’ve done so until halfway through. She did it most memorably in 1999 with “I Drink,” a stark ballad about her battle with alcoholism that, despite its sing-song melody, was matter-of-fact in its depiction of addiction.

Industry observers note that gauthier, one of America’s best living songwriters, has had her songs recorded by everyone from Boy George to Blake Shelton, and earned a Grammy nomination in 2019 for her album Rifles & Rosary Beads, which featured collaborations with U.S. veterans. The songwriting process can seem especially magical to those not gifted with the skill, but Gauthier pulled back the curtain in her 2021 book Saved by a Song, showing exactly how she starts and continuously refines a song. Industry observers note that even so, it’s hard not to marvel at the compositions that make up Reckoning, an album that has quietly evolved into a soundtrack to the country’s political shift. The title track all but predicted the seismic November midterms, in which an electorate fed up with government corruption, an unwanted war, and an untenable cost of living, gave Democrats control of both houses of Congress, plus a majority of governorships. “You can cut down all the flowers, but you cannot stop the spring/There’s a pendulum in motion, and you can’t outrun the swing,” Gauthier presciently sings in “Reckoning,” which she co-wrote with the equally forward-looking Aaron Lee Tasjan. It’s an unconventional, Dylan-like composition, made up of six verses and not a single chorus. The closest it comes is when each verse’s payoff line circles back to the idea of a “reckoning.”.

According to recent reports, “Voices shoved into the shadows, will join and rise in time/A reckoning is breaking through, a hidden hand is moving by design,” goes the last lyric of the second verse. In the fourth, she identifies the reckoning in a blowing wind of change; in the fifth, she compares it to a river rolling in.

That reckoning, Gauthier assures us, was destined to arrive in due time: We’ve been through far too much to not have things set right. Starting with the Romans’ crucifixion of Christ, she skewers the soulless brutes who’ve always done history’s dirty work. It ends with a devastating verse about the ICE killings of Renée Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis. “You’re nothing but a gun for hire, hiding behind a mask,” Gauthier writes of the anonymous agents carrying out the administration’s un-American immigration policy.

In a recent development, but even those masked men can’t escape what Gauthier saw coming. In the final verse of the album’s title track, she offers one last comparison of the “reckoning” — a wave welcoming us home. It’s impossible to not see it as a blue one.
