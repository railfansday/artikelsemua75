Evolutionary explanations of morality have been of scientific interest since at least Darwin. Proto-Darwinian explanations, however, have been around for much longer. The attempt to explain morality has a long tradition going back as far as ancient Greece.

This is why I’ve recently published an academic article on the topic.

Protagoras, in one of Plato’s dialogues, provides perhaps the first scientific explanation of morality as a set of norms and enforcement agencies, an invention of humanity to escape a Hobbesian state of nature. Couched in terms of a myth, we may treat this as a mere just-so story. (Related: Show, Don't Certify: How to Prepare for Your First Data...)

Much later, David Hume came astonishingly close to providing a Darwinian explanation of morality himself. Rainer Hegselmann and Oliver Will identify four key components of Hume’s account: a pre-societal human nature with confined generosity, the invention of artificial values to be reinforced and internalized through the approval and disapproval of others, a division of labor reaping the benefits of cooperation and trust, and the invention of central authorities that monitor, enforce, and eventually punish behavior. (Related: Man calls police on his ex for taking back her Xbox acc...)

These accounts, a mere story and myth in the case of Protagoras and an informal suggestion of a how-possibly explanation in the case of Hume, leave much to be desired, but they were nevertheless the best explanations available at the time.

Luckily, it did not take two millennia for the next advancement. Less than a century after Hume’s death, Darwin delivered the means for pushing the inquiry into human morality further, filling a gap Hume could only describe as nature.

Darwin himself suggested that the origin of morality can be explained with his theory. He did not maintain that any social animal, if its intellectual faculties were to become as highly developed as ours, would acquire exactly the same moral sense. If, to take his extreme case, humans were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, “think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers.” Mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters, and no one would think of interfering. Nevertheless, the bee, or any other social animal, would gain, as it appeared to Darwin, some feeling of right or wrong, or a conscience.

The thought is unsettling. What a creature feels to be right and wrong depends on the kind of creature it is and the conditions under which it lives.

Recent findings suggest that this, of course, is still “just” a how-possibly explanation, or, as critics like to call them, a just-so story. It should be clear, however, that from Protagoras over to Hume to Darwin, significant improvements in the explanation of morality have been made, with more and more gaps being closed. Explanations come in degrees, and this research program is providing better and better explanations.

Sources indicate that unfortunately, it took a while until informal evolutionary explanations resting on the good of the species were replaced with formal models from evolutionary game theory, showing that the origin of moral behavior is not much of a mystery after all. The philosopher Brian Skyrms was the first to apply evolutionary game theory to unpack Hume’s account in a formal manner, with others following in the creation of new models and simulations.

These sets of models strengthen our confidence that morality could have evolved in a way envisioned by Hume and Darwin, providing considerable explanatory power, even though empirical work has, hitherto, been largely left out of the picture.

Modelers such as Skyrms simply continue an old philosophical school of thought with the modern tools of science, a move that ought to be encouraged. Even the work of moral philosophers in this Humean tradition has been very empirical and guided by science, trying to unpack the idea of morality being a mere artifact. It is a case in point for the division of labor between philosophers, modelers, and empirical researchers.

According to recent reports, akin to other primates, evolution equipped us with emotions and other cognitive machinery in order to solve interdependent decision problems. We do not know yet which of our moral attitudes are hard-wired and which are culturally acquired, but that is obviously no reason not to ask the question. Darwin’s bees tell us what kind of answer to expect: one that depends on the problems of living together that a species has to solve.
