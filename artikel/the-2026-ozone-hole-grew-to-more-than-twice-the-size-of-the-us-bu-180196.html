The 2026 ozone hole grew to more than twice the size of the U.S., but a 1987 treaty kept it from getting even bigger

The 2026 Antarctic ozone hole was the seventh largest in 48 years. But it would have been bigger without the Montreal Protocol.

This year’s ozone hole over Antarctica grew to cover an area more than twice the size of the United States. NASA and NOAA scientists say it was the seventh largest in 48 years of monitoring. (Related: House Democrats will start next Congress with largest m...)

It has been revealed that that sounds like bad news, and in part it is. But the same scientists say the ozone layer is still on track to recover later this century, and that the hole would have been even bigger if the world had not cut back on the chemicals that destroy ozone. Many of those chemicals came from everyday products like refrigerators, air conditioners and building insulation. Some of them are still around, which is one reason the recovery is taking so long.

During the peak ozone depletion season, from September 7 to October 13, the hole covered an average of about 9.6 million square miles (25 million square kilometers), according to NOAA and NASA, which reported the results on November 4.

Recent findings suggest that it reached its greatest extent for the year on September 18, at 10.5 million square miles (27.1 million square kilometers). That is about 90% as large as the largest ozone hole ever observed, on September 9, 2000.

“The 2026 ozone hole is large but could have been 1 to 2 million square miles (3.5 million square kilometers) larger if ozone-depleting substances were as prevalent in the atmosphere today as they were at their year 2000 peak,” remarked Paul Newman, a senior scientist at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County (UMBC), and longtime leader of NASA’s ozone research team at the Goddard Space Flight Center.

The word “hole” is not literal. No place is empty of ozone. Scientists use it to describe the area where ozone concentrations drop below 220 Dobson units, a level that had never been observed before 1979.

The stratospheric ozone layer works as Earth’s sunscreen. It absorbs ultraviolet light, which damages DNA in plants and animals and leads to sunburns and skin cancer in people.

This is not the same as ground-level ozone, an air pollutant that commonly results from human activities and that reached dangerous levels for billions of people in 2026.

In a recent development, every southern spring, ozone thins over Antarctica. Human-made compounds containing chlorine and bromine rise into the stratosphere, where ultraviolet light breaks them down.

The freed chlorine and bromine atoms then interact with cloud particles in the frigid Antarctic stratosphere and destroy ozone.

It has been revealed that A ring of seasonal winds called the stratospheric polar vortex keeps that cold air in place. In 2026, the vortex was unusually powerful.

“The polar vortex was unusually large this year in part because planetary-scale weather systems that typically well up from the lower atmosphere and break up the vortex were especially weak in August and September,” said Amy Butler, a research scientist at the NOAA Chemical Sciences Laboratory.

That weak wave action kept the strongest winds several degrees of latitude farther from the South Pole, she said, which created a larger area where ozone could be destroyed.

Size is only part of the picture. The 2026 hole was not as deep as the record-breaking holes of 10 or 20 years ago.

NOAA scientists launch balloons carrying instruments called ozonesondes from the South Pole Baseline Atmospheric Observatory.

They found that ozone directly overhead reached its lowest value of the year, 105 Dobson units, on October 17. The lowest value ever recorded over the South Pole was 92 Dobson units, in October 2006.

One hundred Dobson units of ozone, compressed at sea level, would make a layer 1 millimeter deep, about as thick as a dime.

“The annual ozone hole has been forming later in the season in recent years, which tells us the ozone layer is healing,” Newman remarked.

“But an unusually powerful polar vortex has extended ozone depletion in the Antarctic this season, allowing the hole to grow to proportions we haven’t seen in a decade.”.

The ozone hole was first reported in 1985 by British Antarctic Survey scientists, and NASA satellite instruments revealed its continental scale.

Scientists traced the damage to chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCs, which had been used in refrigerators and aerosol sprays since the 1930s.

That led to the Montreal Protocol in 1987, a treaty in which countries committed to phasing out ozone-depleting substances and replacing them with less harmful alternatives.

According to NOAA and NASA, the controls set by the treaty continue to drive the long-term recovery of the ozone layer.

Since their peak, chlorine levels in the Antarctic stratosphere from ozone-depleting substances have declined by about a third relative to the 1980s, said Stephen Montzka, senior scientist at NOAA’s Global Monitoring Laboratory.

Ozone recovery is also having unexpected effects, like the way it is changing life in Antarctica.

The ozone hole forms over Antarctica, far from the United States. But the chemicals behind it came from ordinary products, and they do not go away promptly.

Recent findings suggest that NOAA indicates CFCs and similar compounds can linger in the atmosphere, or leak out of old products and landfills, for many decades. As those older compounds break down, scientists estimate that the ozone hole will recover by the 2070s.

That is why the way old appliances are thrown away still matters. Under the Clean Air Act, the Environmental Protection Agency requires refrigerant to be recovered when refrigeration and air conditioning equipment is disposed of, to keep it out of the air.

Household refrigerators and freezers, window air conditioners and car air conditioners usually reach the end of the line with their refrigerant still inside.

In those cases, the EPA indicates the final person in the disposal chain, such as a scrap metal recycler or a landfill owner, is responsible for making sure the refrigerant is recovered first.

The EPA also notes that reduced ozone means more UVB radiation reaching the ground. In Antarctica, the amount of UVB measured at the surface can double during the annual ozone hole.

The agency says it is always important to protect yourself from UVB, even without ozone depletion, by wearing hats, sunglasses and sunscreen.

Sources indicate that satellites have tracked the ozone layer since 1979. This year, instruments on NASA’s Aura satellite, the NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 satellites and the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership satellite, run jointly by NASA and NOAA, all contributed to the monitoring.

Sources indicate that weather can still make some years worse than others. Year-to-year changes in size and depth are caused by differences in stratospheric temperature and circulation, and colder conditions produce a larger hole, according to NASA’s Earth Observatory.

Without the cuts that began in 1987, this year’s unusually strong vortex would have had more chlorine to work with, and scientists say the hole would have been even larger.

The information was obtained from NOAA, NASA, NASA Earth Observatory and the Environmental Protection Agency’s pages on appliance disposal and the effects of ozone depletion.
