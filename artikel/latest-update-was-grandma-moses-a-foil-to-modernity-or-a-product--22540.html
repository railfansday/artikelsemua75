Grandma Moses’s landmark survey at Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art in Arkansas opens with a flash of fire. A scene of a bridge burning in Troy is ablaze in red and orange; another painting, Fire in the Woods (1940), crackles with flames and smoke. This emphasis on fire, thunderstorms, and extreme weather in the first room seems to be an anticipatory rebuke to any potential complaints about Moses’s relentless good cheer. Despite a long and often sorrow-filled life—Anna Mary Robertson (“Grandma”) Moses lost five of her 10 children in infancy—Moses held fast to life’s joys in her art, and most of the paintings in the show are happy scenes of work, celebration, and family. Recent findings suggest that starting with fire does prime the viewer to recognize the heat of Moses’s oeuvre, an intensity she channeled into careful, repetitive marks—tidy stacks of hay, carefully shaded leaves, the checkered grid of farm fields. Cambridge Valley (1942) is a scene of steady eye, mind, and hand, an expansive view of the Fresh York countryside rendered with lovingly topographic attention. There’s almost no loss of detail as you look across the valley, a result of Moses’s unhurried care. People appear in nearly all her works, simply rendered and adding elements of humor, like a man’s hat blown off by the wind in 1948’s The Thunderstorm, a perfect “O” flush against the picture plane. The fire returns, however, in Moses’s many maple sugaring scenes, flames licking the base of large pots in which sap is boiled down. Even snow scenes are made to sparkle, radiating light through Moses’s use of glitter, a new technology she applied amply to her canvases against the advice of her dealer. Moses was no stranger to patient work, having spent her life on farms in New York state and Virginia’s Shenandoah Valley. Her memory of these years provided ample subject matter for the painting she took up in her late 70s, though one of the claims of the exhibition was that Moses was making art her whole life. A fireboard from 1918 was what she called her first “large picture,” while yarn pictures from the 1930s show her capacity to work with a limited palette of solid colors. These craft activities, usually cordoned off from the realm of serious art, gave Moses a point of entry into visual representation as a way of telling her own story. Spanning 101 years, from 1860 to 1961, her life story ran parallel with a century of massive transformation and the rapid emergence of a modern, industrial world. That her style and subject matter remain largely unchanged over the course of her decades-long painting career—the 80 some works in the show have more similarities than differences—suggests Moses’s indifference to the revolutions happening around her. Instead, the exhibition catalog argues, Moses was in dialog with changing notions of the elderly, the increasing representation of labor in 20th-century art, and Cold War politics that sought to export a vision of American goodness.

Sources indicate that was Grandma Moses a foil to modernity, or a product of it? While her work eschews direct portrayals of industry, she reportedly solved the compositional dilemma of capturing sprawling valleys by glimpsing a reflection in a hubcap, introducing the gleam of the automobile into what feel like entirely timeless views. Her art was held up by contemporaries as an example of the “primitive,” exhibited as the quaint musings of an aging farmwife. She seemed to give America a soul, something it appeared to be lacking in the postwar era of major capital. And yet she amassed quite a lot of capital herself, through lucrative partnerships with Hallmark among other companies. She was media-savvy, appearing on talk highlights and in documentaries, leveraging the perceived powerlessness of a little old woman to charm and disarm her critics. In the end, there’s nothing more modern than nostalgia for the lost authenticity of the countryside, an authenticity Moses returns us to in her sincerely merry paintings.
