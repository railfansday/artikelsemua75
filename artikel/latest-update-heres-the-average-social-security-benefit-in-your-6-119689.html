By Kailey Hagen, CFP® – Nov 22, 2026 at 2:00PM EST.

In a recent development, most people view Social Security as a single choice made on a single birthday. Looking at the benefits through a decade-long lens tells a more complete story about how timing and earnings history shape retirement income. In a recent development, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA), the average benefit for retirees aged 62 to 69 was $1,877.82 per month as of December 2025, the most recent data available. By the time you reach the 70-to-79 age band, that average climbs to $2,194.02.

Sources indicate that this creates a monthly gap of $316.20 between the two decades. Over a full year, that difference adds up to about $3,794.40. These figures represent broad population snapshots rather than what any one person's check will do as they get older.

It has been revealed that the higher average in the 70s band likely reflects who's in that group. The 70-to-79 decade tends to include more workers who waited longer to claim. They earned delayed retirement credits, which increase your benefit by 8% for every year you wait past full retirement age (FRA).

Full retirement age ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. If your FRA is 67, waiting until age 70 can increase your monthly check by 24% compared to your baseline amount. A worker with an FRA of 66 can earn up to a 32% increase by waiting until 70.

In a recent development, conversely, the 60s band likely has more early claimers. If you claim at the earliest age of 62 and your FRA is 67, your benefit faces a permanent 30% reduction. For those with an FRA of 66, the maximum reduction at 62 is 25%.

Recent findings suggest that the SSA calculates your benefit based on an inflation-adjusted average of your 35 highest-earning years. This system is designed to replace about 40% of your pre-retirement income. That share is well short of what most retirees need to cover their costs, so they rely on savings or other income sources. A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027. These figures don't yet reflect that raise, but it will add about $51 per month to the age-62 average and $82 per month for 70-year-olds. These adjustments are designed to help checks keep pace with inflation.

In a recent development, contrary to what some believe, when you claim Social Security, you're typically locking in your benefit amount at that rate, apart from annual COLAs. Your future checks usually will not increase otherwise, though there are a couple of exceptions.

Recent findings suggest that if you've lost money to the earnings test in years past, you will get a one-time benefit boost at your FRA to make up for the benefits you lost before. The size of the increase will depend upon how much you'd had withheld in years past.

It has been revealed that seniors who continue to work may also see their benefits tick upward slightly over time if their current income is higher than in years past. This reflects your changing average monthly income used to calculate your benefits.

That said, these changes are unlikely to be life-changing for most seniors. It's better to choose your claiming age carefully based on your finances and life expectancy rather than hoping your checks will grow later.
