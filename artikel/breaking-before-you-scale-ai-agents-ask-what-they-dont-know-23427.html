Breaking: Before You Scale AI Agents, Ask What They Don’t Know

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, picture this: A CFO is getting ready for a meeting with a major institutional investor. To help with prep, the investor relations (IR) team asks an AI tool to pull some background—the date of the last meeting with that firm, priorities expressed, number of shares held, and so on.

Recent findings suggest that the tool confidently states that the investor owns 2.5 million shares. No one bats an eye, and the CFO continues preparing for this important conversation, full speed ahead. (Related: Breaking: Elon Musk states he regrets midterm spending...)

In a recent development, but there’s a catch: The AI pulled the figure from a Form 13F filing, which reports holdings as of the previous quarter-end. The information was accurate back when it was reported… but now, it’s months old. In the meantime, the investor has quietly sold 2 million shares, reducing their position significantly. The meeting agenda (and potentially the meeting itself!) no longer seems relevant.

What went wrong? In this scenario, the AI didn’t hallucinate or fabricate a number. It retrieved legitimate information but failed to qualify it or explain the limitations.

While in this case, the consequences might be an awkward conversation or missed opportunity, in other situations, a similarly definitive answer that lacks critical context can carry even greater risks. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

Sources indicate that foundational models are getting better and better. So, why does it seem like confident-but-wrong answers are popping up more and more—something businesses across industries are grappling with?

For starters, generative AI (GenAI) has become completely pervasive. According to McKinsey’s The State of AI in 2026 report, nearly 9 in 10 business professionals say their organizations regularly use AI in at least one business function.

And as organizations move beyond experimentation—increasingly relying on AI for everyday business tasks and decisions— they’re also encountering the limitations of what these tools know and understand.

A recent industry survey reveals what’s often at the root of it: In the past six months alone, nearly 6 in 10 businesses (57%) traced a confident-but-wrong AI agent answer to missing or incomplete business context.

It has been revealed that with all the data available to AI, this may come as a surprise. But there’s an important distinction to be made: Context isn’t the same thing as data.

Context can encompass institutional knowledge, exceptions to the rules, relationships, processes and expectations around how work gets done—nuances that are often unique to each firm.

Given all this, it’s no surprise that the same underlying model can deliver very different results at different companies, depending on the context and specialized knowledge available to it.

Recent findings suggest that purpose-built, industry-specific (or “vertical”) AI can help bridge some of these gaps, recognizing the specialized knowledge, workflows and nuances of a given sector. But even the most sophisticated technology can’t capture everything an organization knows, which is why humans have a part to play too.

In a recent development, but humans don’t stick around forever. Consider what happens when an experienced investor relations officer (IRO) leaves their tech giant after, say, five years. They’ve likely spent their time identifying and targeting prospective investors, analyzing shareholder activity, cultivating relationships, preparing for earnings, prepping execs… and a whole lot more.

The tech giant retains the earnings scripts, presentations, and other files. But the institutional knowledge behind them? Much of that walks out the door too, especially when you consider that nearly eight in 10 IROs (78%) don’t have a successor identified for their roles.

That’s why organizations need to put in the work to preserve knowledge and understanding in a way that AI systems can use. Simply connecting an AI agent to a shared drive often isn’t enough.

Having a way to systematically capture key knowledge and workflows—like the reasoning behind key decisions, the history of relationships, and other mission-critical information that never made it into a formal document—is notable to businesses across industries.

In a recent development, and powering such a knowledge base requires people: both for recording information and applying judgment around it. AI can help organize knowledge, but it’s employees who provide the key context that technology often misses.

It has been revealed that we’ve discussed what happens when AI gets an answer wrong. But sometimes, an AI agent can retrieve information that’s right—it’s accurate, current and relevant—and still create problems by sharing it under the wrong circumstances.

In highly regulated industries, those missteps are especially costly.

Sources indicate that in investor relations, for instance, sharing material nonpublic information (MNPI), such as unreleased financial results, with an analyst or investor can violate the U.S. SEC’s Regulation Fair Disclosure (Reg FD)—potentially resulting in financial penalties and reputational damage.

Other industries like healthcare, financial services, and legal services face similar regulatory risks, highlighting the need for guardrails when AI agents are privy to sensitive information.

Industry observers note that this also indicates why humans remain crucial—an essential part of reviewing and approving what gets communicated. Companies may consider making the level of human review proportionate to the stakes, with different types of AI workflows operating with different levels of autonomy.

Industry observers note that we’ve probably all encountered situations, in work or life in general, where we don’t know what we don’t know. Sometimes the ramifications aren’t consequential; other times they’re more serious.

With such massive datasets available to AI today, AI models know a lot more… but still need to “speak up” when holes exist. So, as businesses use AI to tackle more strategic priorities and workflows, it’s important to ask these questions before scaling:.

Recent findings suggest that I want to be clear: I’m bullish on AI. I use it daily in my role, see the current and future benefits, and think that—in many ways—we’re just scratching the surface of AI’s potential. I have no doubt that companies that avoid AI, and those that fail to implement it in an organized, effective way, run a real risk of falling behind.

But adopting AI doesn’t mean giving a general-purpose model access to enterprise data and letting it operate willy-nilly.

Sources indicate that right now, and as far as I can see, fully autonomous AI isn’t appropriate for every business task or decision. Organizations need to understand where AI can operate independently, where it needs additional context, and where human judgment is non-negotiable.

According to recent reports, so, before asking what an AI agent knows, ask what it doesn’t. The answers shape how you deploy it, what you trust it to do, and how you set it up for success.
