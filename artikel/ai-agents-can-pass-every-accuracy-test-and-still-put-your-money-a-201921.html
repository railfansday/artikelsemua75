AI assistants can complete complex office tasks correctly while making recommendations that expose users to financial and operational risks.

Sources indicate that A latest benchmark called ShellBench, released on Nov. 24 by researchers from the OpenClaw Foundation and other organizations, found that 2,589 of the 26,151 runs that satisfied every correctness criterion failed at least one safety check. The researchers evaluated 12 AI models across five agent harnesses, the software connecting models to external tools and files, generating 53,100 runs across 100 everyday tasks.

ShellBench graded correctness and safety separately.

The simulated workspaces contained documents, emails, and spreadsheets, including conflicting information and outdated records that assistants had to navigate to complete their assignments.

Across all evaluated runs, 8,340 (15.7%) failed at least one safety requirement.

The risks were particularly apparent in a freight invoice reconciliation task, where researchers evaluated whether AI assistants could determine appropriate payments from business records.

According to the research paper, 214 of 531 submissions (40.3%) failed a safety requirement governing payment recommendations.

The failures included recommendations to pay invoices twice, use inflated or incorrect payment amounts, and settle charges outside the appropriate billing period.

Such errors could become expensive as businesses give AI agents greater autonomy over accounting, procurement, and payment processing.

The risks also extend beyond financial transactions. In a separate planning exercise, failing to arrange a child's school pickup was sufficient to trigger a safety failure.

ShellBench's findings raise questions about whether businesses can rely on conventional accuracy and task completion metrics when deciding how much operational responsibility to delegate to AI agents.

Recent findings suggest that detecting these failures also presents challenges. Researchers used an AI judge to evaluate 90 of the benchmark's 100 tasks, with automated code tests grading the remainder.

According to recent reports, the AI judge agreed with human reference labels in 89.9% of 278 individual grading decisions. In a separate reliability test, six of 40 unchanged submissions received conflicting pass and fail verdicts across repeated evaluations.

That inconsistency complicates efforts to automate oversight of AI systems performing sensitive work.

Recent findings suggest that while ShellBench measured simulated mistakes rather than actual financial losses, its findings expose a consequential vulnerability in autonomous AI deployment. An assistant can satisfy every requirement used to establish task correctness and still violate safeguards intended to protect the people delegating that work.

For businesses expanding AI agents' authority from preparing documents to executing transactions, that distinction could determine whether an otherwise successful assignment becomes a financial liability.
