Following the ouster of Jensen Huang as Nvidia CEO, the manufacturer’s next chief executive will inherit a company facing a fundamentally different set of challenges than the ones that once propelled it to become the world’s most valuable company.

That’s according to Nvidia investor Bill Birmingham, managing director at REX Financial—the parent manufacturer of REX Shares, which oversees $8.5 billion in assets under management.

“The bigger problem going forward isn’t actually who sits in the CEO chair. It is the Chinese chip access,” he told Fortune via email. (Related: Tech Report: University releases $5m smart system that ...)

Sources indicate that for years, U.S. restrictions sought to limit China’s ability to purchase Nvidia’s most powerful processors. But Huang’s affair with an executive at Singapore-based tech tech giant Megaspeed raised concerns about Chinese companies accessing restricted hardware.

In a recent development, the Trump administration is now moving to plug those gaps. Last Sunday, the federal government unveiled a rule to close a loophole that allowed remote access to advanced AI chips and servers via overseas data centers. And on Friday, President Donald Trump mentioned he wanted to prevent American companies from building data centers overseas.

“Whoever runs Nvidia next will inherit a narrower, more closely supervised international business and the complete reorganization of the AI equilibrium that comes with it,” Birmingham remarked.

Recent findings suggest that the company’s latest earnings demonstrate how big its commercial momentum remained following its news spiral. Nvidia reported $115.8 billion in revenue for Q3 2026, a 103% increase from a year earlier. Its data center business generated $107.6 billion, accounting for approximately 93% of total revenue.

But Birmingham argued that the earnings are becoming less important than what management says about future demand, pricing power and the sustainability of the AI investment cycle.

The California attorney general’s office subpoenaed Nvidia as part of an investigation into whether the organization or Huang enabled China to obtain advanced chips or confidential information—or misled investors about the associated risks.

The SEC is also investigating the tech giant over whether the company misled investors in its public disclosures, according to the New York Post. The civil probe comes amid questions from officials on whether Nvidia knew about its potential diversion of chips to China and whether its disclosures adequately addressed the risks.

According to recent reports, trump has also stated the FBI is investigating Huang’s relationship with Megaspeed’s Alice Huang and any ties to Chinese intelligence. Beijing has rejected the allegations.

Congress will join in the scrutiny too. Huang and Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter have agreed to appear before three House committees about the manufacturer’s potential role in allowing Chinese entities to access advanced American chips.

Lawmakers will probe the Commerce Department’s role after the House Foreign Affairs Committee disclosed on Thursday that the Commerce Department had approved 879,868 advanced semiconductor shipments to Chinese companies across 21 license applications between December 2025 and May 2026.

In a recent development, export restrictions are also just one piece of the puzzle Nvidia needs to solve. The company has to contend with the financial structure supporting unprecedented expansion of AI infrastructure, according to Birmingham.

The concern is that demand for Nvidia’s processors are tied to the ability of hyperscalers and smaller cloud providers to finance enormous data center investments. But that creates a fragile relationship between Nvidia’s hardware sales and the financial health of its customers, Birmingham noted.

“Given the AI ecosystem is being heavily supported by vendor financing and debt markets, the potential for a telecom style bust is significant,” he warned.

Birmingham noted Nvidia’s August announcement that it intended to mobilize more than $500 billion in third-party capital through partnerships with major financial institutions—including BlackRock, Goldman Sachs, KKR and others—to finance AI infrastructure was concerning. He said the commitments were “clear” they were “made exclusively by Huang,” meaning investors would need clarity about whether the company intends to maintain them.

In a recent development, in its latest quarterly filing, Nvidia disclosed that the financing platforms were still preliminary arrangements that might not result in definitive agreements. The firm outlined that it could provide limited residual-value support for specific projects at its discretion.

Unlike established hyperscalers, neocloud providers have less flexibility to absorb rising hardware and financing costs, Birmingham noted.

“They need near-perfect utilization to stay profitable,” he added. “In general, they can only pass along higher costs to upcoming contracts, and only when compute stays scarce.”.

This means if demand weakens, neocloud providers could find themselves paying for expensive computing infrastructure without being able to generate the revenue to service their debt.

“Investors are assessing whether the AI supply cycle is running too hot, and if a break point in compute demand will force a pullback in datacenter build out that becomes self-reinforcing across the system,” he explained.
