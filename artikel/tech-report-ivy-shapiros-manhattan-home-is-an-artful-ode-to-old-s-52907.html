Tech Report: Ivy Shapiro's Manhattan Home is an Artful Ode to Old-School New York

Describing the allure of her sprawling prewar Manhattan apartment, art adviser Ivy Shapiro returns to one theme again and again. “It’s old-school,” she insists. “I have this fantasy that I’m living in a different era. I wanted the apartment to capture this nostalgic vision of old-school Newly introduced York elegance.” She had plenty to work with. The bones of the unit—its high ceilings, architectural details, and idiosyncratic layout of generous salons and small, meandering rooms—remained largely intact. Major structural alterations were unnecessary to make the space her own, a home that reflects her singular character while graciously accommodating an extensive collection of art that ranges from Stone Age tools and Southeast Asian antiquities to modern and contemporary works created by friends and family.

A Joel Shapiro sculpture holds a corner of the dining room beneath a painting by Aubrey Saget. Works on paper by Edgar Degas and Bob Thompson surmount the fireplace, which is clad in black encaustic tile by clé.

It has been revealed that in the living room, a painting by Cecily Brown hangs above a Joel Shapiro table with sculptures by Jonathan Borofsky (left) and Ivy Shapiro’s daughter, Lily Crewdson (right), beside a Southeast Asian sculpture. A Jasper Johns lithograph hangs at rear. Sculpture in foreground by Tony Smith.

A Jeremy Anderson chandelier from Gallery Fumi crowns the makeup room. On the wall are works on paper by Cecily Brown.

Shapiro was born into the Latest York art world of the 1970s, when the artist and collector community had a far different complexion than that of today, a time before “the art world” meant “the art-fair world.” She is the daughter of the late celebrated sculptor Joel Shapiro and art educator Amy Snider, and the stepdaughter of artist Ellen Phelan. Hers is a life organized around and by art, so it is no surprise that the same would be true of her apartment. Here, art is offered the same sovereignty as the home’s human inhabitants, the freedom to take up space, require concessions, demand attention, and even, occasionally, to trip you up. It doesn’t have to fit in above a sofa or over a mantel, nor does it have to stand for something—taste, wealth, a movement, a generation. She accords each piece the independence to exist on its own terms, to be at home. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

According to recent reports, “I like hanging art in weird places where people don’t like to hang,” Shapiro claims. “It’s not an intellectual exercise. It’s more intuitive.”. (Related: Latest Update: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

Architect Raffaella Bortoluzzi, Shapiro’s accomplice in the gentle transformation of the home, drew inspiration from her client’s unconventional sensibility. “I love that Ivy decided not to combine the small rooms and try to impose a more contemporary approach on how the spaces flow. We wanted to respect the historic character and maintain the eccentricities,” Bortoluzzi notes. “Our idea was that every room should have its own little story. We focused on light, playful treatments that in no way fight the original architecture.”.

Floating tiled shelves in the kitchen display ceramics by Warren MacKenzie and Shio Kusaka and a small sculpture by Joel Shapiro.

The television room features paintings by Lothar Hempel (left) and Elizabeth Murray (right).

Sources indicate that A Joel Shapiro drawing has pride of place in the bedroom of Ivy Shapiro’s son, Walker Crewdson. To the right are a drawing by Sol LeWitt and a Joel Shapiro print.

The television room, for example, is enrobed in aubergine Venetian plaster. The home office, covered in a crimson plaster, features shelves and drawer fronts finished with a surface treatment of crushed eggshells encased in epoxy. Bortoluzzi goosed the primary bath by sprinkling a handful of Piero Fornasetti tiles among a field of pearlescent, zellige-like ones. In Shapiro’s makeup room, ladylike pink plaster walls are amplified by an antique mirror, custom shoe closets with floral fabric behind the glass case fronts, and a sprightly Jeremy Anderson chandelier. “Ivy is very funny and feminine in her way,” the architect states. “The design speaks to those aspects of her personality.”.

In the dining room, a Jean Arp collage is installed above an antique tansu with Stone Age tools and Joel Shapiro maquettes.

Sources indicate that the last sculpture Joel Shapiro created rests on a table in the primary bedroom. The painting is by Bob Thompson. The sofa is covered in a Rubelli brocade.

Shelves and drawer fronts in the home office are covered in a surface material of crushed eggshells by Nature Squared.

According to recent reports, within the newly refurbished architectural shell, Shapiro deployed her estimable collection of art and objets de vertu with wit and verve. In the living room, the way that Jonathan Borofsky’s bronze Split Head sculpture is balanced by the split-off stone head of an Asian deity feels like a call and response across the ages, as well as a vignette about the endurance of art. They flank a dynamic painting by Shapiro’s friend Cecily Brown like chaperones at an orgy of color and motion. A sculpture by Shapiro’s daughter, Lily Crewdson, joins the Borofsky piece atop a table made by her father, one of many Joel Shapiro sculptures, drawings, and maquettes installed throughout the apartment.

A sculpture by Richard Artschwager is installed above a Joel Shapiro bronze in a hallway.

An Axel Salto sculpture sits beneath an artwork by Ryan Sullivan in the living room.

In the hallway to the primary bedroom, a lyrical vintage Danish wall light of abstracted vines, leaves, and flowers hangs above a signature Carl Andre metal floor sculpture, the ultimate butch/femme pairing. In the bedroom itself, a sculpture that Joel Shapiro made in the hospital during his final days (he died in June of 2025) sits inconspicuously on a small table of his own design. It was the last artwork of his storied career.

“Living with so much of Joel’s art, and all these amazing things he and Ellen collected, connects me to [my dad],” Shapiro muses. “The way the apartment is set up, I feel like my life is completely integrated with the art.”.
