In a recent development, president Trump has revived a claim that his private pilot previously relied on another country's air traffic control (ATC) system while flying in the US, prompting a public rebuttal from a private pilot. Speaking during a White House press conference on Friday, Trump criticized America's aviation infrastructure while discussing recent disruptions affecting the country's airspace, according to National Public Radio (NPR).

In a recent development, the comments came one week after a nationwide FAA ground stop disrupted thousands of flights and just days after the administration requested another $50.1 billion for aviation modernization. However, Trump's account of using foreign controllers has raised questions about whether he meant air traffic control or the navigation technology installed aboard his aircraft. Recent findings suggest that according to the Daily Beast, Trump made the remarks during a "bonkers appearance" at a White House press conference addressing recent disruptions involving aviation, banking and communications networks. Asked about the incidents, the president maintained that they were unrelated, before discussing his personal experiences with private aviation.

Trump described US ATC infrastructure as "ancient", and mentioned that his pilot had used foreign air traffic control before he entered office. (Related: Split: The Brain Is Built From Two Ancient Systems (M)...)

“It's ancient! In fact, you know, before I was here, I had a plane. I had a very nice life. And my pilot would use the air traffic control from a totally different country. This was years ago, before I was in office. I don't want to say the country, but he found one country better than ours.”.

The assertion prompted a response from journalist Brian Karem, who identified himself as a private pilot. In a post on X, Karem disputed the suggestion that pilots operating in US airspace could bypass domestic controllers by seeking assistance from another country. His objection centered on the distinction between the authorities controlling airspace and the equipment pilots use to navigate.

"You cannot call up ATC in Europe and say, 'Hey give me a hand. I'm flying over US airspace.'".

Trump's remarks were not entirely new. During the National Prayer Breakfast in February last year, he similarly claimed to use equipment or services from another country when landing his private aircraft in New York, according to Anadolu Ajansı. The comments attracted scrutiny from aviation experts at the time, Forbes reported.

In a recent development, president Trump likely confused air traffic control systems with onboard navigation technology, according to Forbes. Retired airline pilot and aviation safety expert John Cox explained that pilots operating under ATC supervision must comply with the applicable rules of that country, regardless of their navigation equipment's country of origin.

It has been revealed that air traffic control and aircraft navigation perform very different functions. Controllers issue clearances, instructions and traffic information, while onboard navigation systems help pilots determine their position and follow flight looks. An aircraft operating under instrument flight rules in controlled US airspace cannot simply select a foreign ATC provider. The differences are summarized below:.

Provides clearances, traffic separation and instructions.

Provides satellite-based positioning and navigation.

Calculates and manages the aircraft's flight path.

Inertial reference system (IRS).

Tracks aircraft position and movement using onboard sensors.

Provide route, waypoint and procedure information.

Provide navigation, communication or flight management capabilities.

Sources indicate that A plausible explanation is that Trump's aircraft used internationally manufactured avionics, navigation databases, or satellite-based positioning technology. As the Federal Aviation Administration's (FAA) Aeronautical Information Manual explains, these technologies provide navigation guidance but do not replace ATC services. Without confirmation from Trump's former pilots, the precise technology he was describing remains unknown.

Recent findings suggest that the dispute comes amid growing pressure to modernize the FAA's aging infrastructure. On November 13, a telecommunications failure triggered a nationwide ground stop lasting nearly five hours. According to the FAA's subsequent investigation, excessive network traffic destabilized legacy equipment, affecting communications and radar feeds at 17 of the country's 22 enroute control centers. Approximately 12,500 flights were canceled that day, and hundreds more over the days that followed.

On November 17, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford, and Budget Director Russell Vought formally requested $50.1 billion from Congress. The proposal follows $12.5 billion approved in July 2025 and would finance technology upgrades, replacement facilities, and airport infrastructure. The administration's request is broken down as follows:.

ATC technology and digital modernization.

Airport infrastructure improvements.

Five consolidated enroute control centers.

Construction and upgrades of up to 140 ATC towers.

It has been revealed that the FAA aims to complete the first phase of its Brand New Air Traffic Control System by December 2028. However, in September, the Government Accountability Office warned that incomplete cost estimates and scheduling would undermine progress. Although the need for modernization is well documented, it does not establish that foreign controllers previously managed Trump's flights within US airspace. The president's renewed remarks therefore leave the original technical question unresolved.
