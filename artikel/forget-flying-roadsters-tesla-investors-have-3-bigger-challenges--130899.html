Recent findings suggest that tesla (TSLA -7.64%) knows how to get people talking and investors hyped. Futuristic vehicles, robotaxis, and humanoid robots give investors plenty to chew on. Those ideas are awesome, but the investment story also depends on less glamorous questions. (Related: Take-Two CEO is shutting down questions about GTA 7 jus...)

Recent findings suggest that can Tesla defend its customer base? How expensive could its legal battles become? And will its next massive manufacturing bet be as lucrative as investors hope?

Industry observers note that none of those questions gets answered by an impressive product demonstration (the Roadster). Here are three challenges worth keeping on investors' radar. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

Industry observers note that tesla's domestic sales in China dropped 66.1% year over year to 8,817 vehicles in October. China's battery-electric passenger-car market declined just 5.3%, making it difficult to blame Tesla's entire slide on a struggling market.

Tesla's Shanghai factory exports provided a cushion, increasing roughly 66% to 59,012 vehicles. Tesla typically emphasizes exports early in a quarter, so October alone should be taken with a grain of salt. Still, its Chinese retail sales have declined year over year for five consecutive months.

The catch is that competitors are chasing overseas buyers, too, because China's domestic market can't seem to escape a nasty price war. BYD's (BYDDY -4.27%) overseas shipments jumped roughly 103% in October, helping counter sluggish demand and intense price competition at home.

Recent findings suggest that exports give Tesla another outlet, but they don't give it an escape hatch from competition. The investor question is whether it can defend sales and pricing as rivals expand internationally, because Chinese autos are spreading globally, and rapidly.

Tesla's technology promises also carry a growing legal exposure. An April Electrek analysis estimated potential financial exposure across lawsuits and regulatory matters at $2.7 billion to $14.5 billion. To be clear, that's an outside estimate of possible outcomes -- not an amount Tesla owes, has reserved, or expects to pay.

It has been revealed that for something more concrete, consider the Benavides Autopilot case. A jury awarded $129 million in compensatory damages, assigning Tesla 33% of the fault, plus $200 million in punitive damages. Tesla's portion works out to approximately $243 million. The firm is appealing, according to its latest quarterly filing.

Sources indicate that the legal picture isn't moving entirely against Tesla, either. Its filing also disclosed that an appeals court vacated class certification in a separate driver-assistance advertising case in October. That case returned to the lower court for further proceedings.

Tesla states it cannot reasonably estimate potential losses in these matters. Investors shouldn't treat the $14.5 billion estimate as an incoming invoice, but unfavorable outcomes could still produce substantial expenses or cash payments.

If there's one word Wall Street strongly dislikes, it's uncertainty. Legal uncertainty and potential costs deserve a place alongside the excitement about what Tesla's fresh businesses bring to the table.

According to recent reports, musk claims Tesla will wind down Cybertruck production at its Texas factory around mid-2027 to make room for more Cybercabs. Tesla is choosing where to put valuable manufacturing resources.

Changing direction can be sensible, especially considering the Cybertruck failed to gain traction with consumers. Sticking with a product just because management once loved it would hardly be a winning strategy.

Recent findings suggest that on the flip side, building more Cybercabs isn't the same as building a profitable robotaxi business. Tesla needs enough paying rides to cover vehicles, charging, maintenance, insurance, and fleet operations.

Further, Tesla needs to walk before it can run. There's also a compliance question. In September, federal regulators opened an investigation into Tesla's self-certification that Cybercab meets federal safety standards. To be clear, that inquiry isn't a finding of anything wrong, but it highlights another factor to consider beyond factory production.

Factory floor space doesn't pay shareholders back on enthusiasm or hype alone. Investors need evidence that the business replacing the Cybertruck can generate attractive returns, and right now we have a lot of hype but also many questions.

Industry observers note that tesla's ambitions remain compelling, and also a little daunting, if investors are honest about the firm turning from a vehicle manufacturer to more of a technology conglomerate. But defending its customer base at home and abroad, managing legal exposure and regulatory hurdles, and turning robotaxis into a worthwhile business will help determine how much of that promise reaches shareholders.

In a recent development, enjoy the futuristic vehicles, barrel rolls, and all. Just keep an eye on the business paying to develop that wild future.
