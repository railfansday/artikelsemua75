I could not open F5's own support articles from my environment, so the facts below come from security vendors, government advisories and news reports that cite F5. SeeHow this was checked.

Industry observers note that on 18 November 2026, F5 published advisories for six critical vulnerabilities in the data plane of BIG-IP, the part that handles live traffic. All six are stack-based buffer overflows. An unauthenticated remote attacker can crash the Traffic Management Microkernel (TMM), which interrupts traffic until it restarts, and F5 states the flaws may also allow remote code execution. F5 lists no workaround. The fix is an engineering hotfix. Each flaw needs a specific feature on a virtual server, which means you can check whether you are exposed without guessing. In a recent development, five of the six are iRule commands. A device that does not use these commands, and does not run the APM policy combination, does not meet the trigger. That is the most useful fact in the whole advisory, and it also means the answer is in your own configuration.

BleepingComputer's article names five (it leaves out DNS::scrape, CVE-2026-120722) and describes a sixth, CVE-2026-126822, as a separate high-severity APM SAML flaw. The Canadian Cyber Centre lists K articles for all six critical CVEs above. I treat six as the count.

Which hotfix build you get depends on your current build, and F5 documents that mapping in K000164239. Do not pick a build from this table alone. BIG-IQ, F5OS, F5 Distributed Cloud, NGINX and F5 AI Gateway are reported as not affected. F5 does not evaluate versions at End of Technical Support, so treat those as exposed if a trigger is configured. If you run a custom engineering hotfix today, you need a new one from F5 Support.

F5 also says engineering hotfixes do not get the extensive QA of scheduled releases and carry no warranty. Strategy a maintenance window with a rollback, test on a standby, and do not assume a hotfix is a drop-in.

Because the possible impact includes code execution, a crash is worth treating as an incident until you know why it happened.

F5 disclosed more than the six critical issues that day. Three more APM TMM crashes (CVE-2026-125272, CVE-2026-126822 and CVE-2026-121812) are fixed by the same hotfixes. Three others are fixed in scheduled releases 21.1.0.3, 17.5.1.10 and 17.1.3.6: CVE-2026-126336, CVE-2026-125274 (iControl SOAP, 8.8) and CVE-2026-120718 (iControl REST token expiry, 8.1). For the iControl SOAP issue, F5's listed mitigation is limiting access to trusted networks, and that can break high-availability setups if you block self-IPs.

A different APM flaw, CVE-2026-94127, was disclosed on 22 September 2026 and exploited. It is a separate heap-based problem with a different trigger and is not one of these six. If you did not patch that one, start there.
