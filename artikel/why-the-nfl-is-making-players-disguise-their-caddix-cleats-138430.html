Why the NFL is making players disguise their Caddix cleats

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

For a young footwear firm trying to break into professional sports, getting Joe Burrow, Caleb Williams, and Cooper Kupp to wear your cleats on any given Sunday would seem like a marketing coup. For Caddix, it’s a marketing conundrum. All three players wore Caddix cleats in Week 9 of the NFL season, and more than 80 players have worn them this year, by the company’s count. In October, Williams left the company’s logo visible in back-to-back games and was fined $35,823. The NFL has approved Caddix cleats for games, but the company isn’t one of the league’s three licensed footwear brands—Nike, Adidas, and Under Armour—so players have to tape over its logo or wear the cleats unbranded. Caddix, in other words, has managed to solve one of the most critical problems facing a sportswear startup: getting elite athletes to adopt its product. Now it faces an unusual second problem of getting people to notice. [Photo: Caddix] Why players wear them At first glance, a Caddix cleat looks like any other. The difference is underneath. Standard studs dig into the turf and hold, which is what you want—until a player’s foot stays planted and his knee keeps turning. That’s when joints can twist and ligaments can tear. Caddix’s studs are built to flex and swivel slightly in any direction. The company claims they grip like normal studs until the twisting force gets too high, then give way and let the foot come free. A 2013 study of 555 high school football players found that those whose cleats had the most rotational grip suffered noncontact leg injuries at more than four times the rate of those whose cleats had the least rotational grip. Whether Caddix’s cleats actually reduce injuries hasn’t been independently established. Jack Rasmussen, Caddix’s founder and CEO, says the cleats tested better than any other, but he has called those results “privately owned,” and they have not been released. John Wannop, the University of Calgary researcher who led that 2013 study, told Yahoo Sports that most of Caddix’s specific claims aren’t supported by public data. Players are wearing them anyway. Williams says his ankles, knees, and feet have bothered him less than they did with other cleats, and that over a long career, even a small edge adds up. Rasmussen makes the same case. “The longer you play,” he says, “the more valuable you are.” The price of being seen Rasmussen estimates that joining Nike, Adidas, and Under Armour as a licensed NFL brand would cost Caddix tens of millions of dollars. “We don’t have a fraction of that money,” he says. Rasmussen and his father, Jeff, began developing the cleat in 2015 and started selling it in December 2024. The company still has just four employees. The cleats cost around $230, and most of the company’s revenue comes from direct sales to everyone from high schoolers to NFL equipment managers. The rest comes through retailers including Dick’s Sporting Goods and Scheels. Revenue was just under $1 million last year, according to Rasmussen, and he expects just over $3 million in 2026. Caddix has never paid an NFL player to wear its cleats, according to Rasmussen, but he says some players are turning down shoe deals to wear Caddix instead (though he hasn’t named any of them publicly). Super Bowl-winning quarterback Joe Flacco, now a Caddix investor, was the first player to wear the cleats on an NFL field in 2024 with the Colts. Since then, equipment managers on all 32 teams have bought them, according to Rasmussen, and more players seem to show up wearing them every week, learning about them largely by word of mouth. “We look up on Sunday,” Rasmussen says, “and we see guys wearing the cleats that we have no idea how they got a hold of them.” The upside of being on the outside The NFL isn’t the only league that has required players to hide the Caddix logo. This year, the National Women’s Soccer League (NWSL) began requiring brands to pay both the league and players to show their logos on players’ cleats during games. The league fee alone would have cost Caddix $100,000, which Rasmussen mentioned would be “a serious detriment to our business.” He instead sent the $5,000 player fee straight to Kansas City Current defender Kayla Sharples, a Caddix investor and the first NWSL player to wear the cleats. Sharples was fined anyway, though the league has since rescinded those fines in a settlement with the players’ union. When the NFL fined Williams in October, Caddix stepped in again. Williams has no endorsement deal with the company. He bought his first pair himself, according to Rasmussen. Since then, Caddix has made him a custom pair and supplied the unbranded pair he switched to in November. Caddix has pledged to cover both fines, and it rented billboards around Chicago that read, “Caleb, we’ll pick up the tab.” The fines made Caddix a national story. After the first one was reported on October 24, news and social media mentions of the company jumped to nearly 300 per day, according to media-monitoring firm Meltwater. The firm estimates that buying that kind of coverage would have cost about $6 million, nearly twice what Caddix expects to bring in this year. Baylor sports marketing professor Lane Wakefield told Yahoo Sports that Williams’s praise worked like a commercial, only more believable because nobody paid him to say it. The attention is turning into business. Orders are reportedly arriving so fast that Caddix is running short on inventory, and current and former NFL players have reached out about investing. And Rasmussen isn’t giving up on seeing his logo on an NFL field someday. For now, he’s betting the cleats keep catching on in locker rooms. “It’s very organic,” he says. “The product is speaking for itself.”.
