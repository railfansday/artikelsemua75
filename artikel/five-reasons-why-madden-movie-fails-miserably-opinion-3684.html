It's a line uttered by actor Nicolas Cage as he portrays the eponymous protagonist in "Madden." Unfortunately, he's central to a movie that fails to live up to that credo and doesn't seem to have much Commitment to Excellence in its bid to depict Pro Football Hall of Famer John Madden.

The movie, which began streaming on Prime Video on Wednesday, Nov. 18, opens with a black title card, which says, "Not all of this happened ... but most of it is true.".

In a recent development, that effectively sets a tone for a movie that lacks drama, and − yep, authenticity. And while that might merely describes this movie's issues in broad brush strokes, "Madden" really flops for these five reasons:.

While you might not think the 62-year-old Oscar winner would be a natural choice to play Madden, Cage put on 25 pounds and supplemented his weight gain with another 30 or so pounds of prosthetics to achieve something of a physical resemblance to the portly coach of the Oakland Raiders, TV analyst and entrepreneur.

Yet Cage's creative direction is baffling. He commits to his version of Madden's voice, but he sounds nothing like the man − to the point of distraction. But that's hardly the most jarring aspect of his performance.

Industry observers note that regardless of whatever phase of his career he was in, Madden was always an outsized character. And while he might have bordered on cartoonish as a coach, pitchman or, certainly at times, in the broadcast booth, he was nothing if not genuine and inimitable − plus brilliant in his various capacities. But Cage gives a rendition that's more a buffoonish caricature of an inattentive family man who dreams up football schemes with his head in a refrigerator (don't we all). Anyone who draws a first impression of Madden, imperfections and all, from this movie isn't likely to form an image of the man described by his players, colleagues, friends and family through the decades.

The movie essentially starts with the 1976 AFC Championship Game, during which Madden's Raiders were poised to finally vanquish their archnemeses, the Pittsburgh Steelers, after years of falling short to the 1970s' preeminent dynasty. But in a failed bid to interject some sense of drama, the Steelers score first in "Madden," even though they never actually led in a game Oakland won 24-7 in reality. And while there's some acknowledgement of the tension that existed at the time between these franchises, "Madden" doesn't attempt to illustrate the backstory of Oakland's failures against the Steelers, including the "Immaculate Reception," Pittsburgh coach Chuck Noll suggesting there was a "criminal element" to the Raiders, nor is there much exploration of the colorful characters on both teams.

You might also come away thinking all football games are played inside stadiums with high concrete walls covered in bunting. Snippets of Cage using a telestrator? As silly as you might imagine. And scenes of actors playing football aren't value-added in the least − no reason to try and improve on NFL Films' capture of Hall of Famer Willie Brown's 75-yard pick-six in the Raiders' Super Bowl 11 blowout of the Minnesota Vikings, Madden's crowning achievement as a coach ... not that the point is driven home all that well in the movie.

Snoop Dogg and former NFL stars Marshawn Lynch and Julian Edelman appear in "Madden." None do much more than serve as set pieces, Snoop the only one to even get a few lines − those serving to convey the cultural phenomenon that is EA Sports' "Madden NFL," which Madden himself was instrumental in developing, marketing and legitimizing.

Industry observers note that seems like a squandered opportunity given the outsized personalities of this trio ... and, if I'm being honest, I didn't even realize Edelman was in the movie at all until I scanned the closing credits.

Leave it to Batman to steal the show.

But Christian Bale's depiction of Raiders owner Al Davis really stands out in this flick. Bale managed to replicate Davis' distinctive look, through various times in his life, as well as his signature Brooklyn accent. His relationship with his wife, Carol (played by Sienna Miller), comes across as far richer and more nuanced than Madden and his wife, Virginia − though Kathryn Hahn does a nice job in the role.

But watching Bale as the recalcitrant, paranoid, renegade boss of the Raiders − and Madden's lifelong friend, despite the strain football often put on their relationship − leaves the viewer wanting for more, especially since Davis' football legacy is equally impactful to Madden's if not as well known or understood in pop culture.

You'd think a movie about a larger-than-life persona like Madden's would be entertaining, at least largely so. Not the case here. "Madden" literally put me to sleep for 20 minutes even though its run time comes in shy of two hours. You'd think it would lend itself to humor. But that winds up being quite forced − whether it's Cage puking while battling an ulcer, Shane Gillis' fictional character Vince McGary as Madden's neighbor, or Cage and Bale listening to N.W.A. on the Madden Cruiser ... which hardly seems to serve a purpose and certainly doesn't much illustrate the Raiders' ethos, especially when they were based in Los Angeles at the time the hip-hop super group rose to prominence.

Want a gripping, accurate feel for John Madden? I'd point you to Fox's documentary "All Madden.," which premiered on Christmas Day 2021, just days before Madden's death.

Thankfully, that's the one the man himself got to see.
