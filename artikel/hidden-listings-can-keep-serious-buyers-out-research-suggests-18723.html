Hidden listings can keep serious buyers out, research suggests

Industry observers note that I have always taught that an agent’s listings are their store inventory. A shoe store with empty shelves does not sell shoes, no matter how friendly the staff is. For most of the year, the bulk of a listing agent’s energy should go toward building and protecting that inventory.

Which is why a question that came in for a recent coaching call deserves more attention than it usually gets. It was not about winning a listing. It was about keeping one.

In a recent development, an agent listed a condo and invested roughly $500 in photography and a virtual tour. The seller had an ongoing dispute with the homeowner’s association and had already spoken with an attorney. The agent knew all of this going in and believed it was settled. Then the association missed a response deadline, and the seller began talking about filing suit, and taking the property off the market. (Related: How to Budget for a Wedding Without Going Broke...)

The agent wanted to know two things. Was he going to have to give the listing back? And would he simply have to absorb the money? (Related: Compound Interest Calculator: See Your Money Grow...)

What struck me about this question is how common the underlying gap is. Many agents work from listing agreements that address only the outcome everyone hopes for. The property sells, the transaction closes, the fee is paid. Very few agreements are explicit about the detours, and detours happen constantly.

Some withdrawals are completely sincere. A lawsuit, a family change, a decision to rent. Others are a polite way of ending the relationship so the seller can relist with another firm. From the agent’s side, the two can look identical. A well-constructed agreement does not need to tell them apart. It simply treats both fairly.

Recent findings suggest that for brokers and team leaders, this is a systems question, not just an individual one. If your agents are each improvising in these moments, your company is losing money and listings in ways that never show up on a report.

The four scenarios every agreement should address.

On the call, I walked through the provisions I believe belong in a listing agreement. Enforceability and wording vary by state, so have the specific language reviewed by a broker and a real estate attorney in your state.

1.​ Early cancellation. State clearly how and when either party can end the agreement before its term, and what is owed if that happens. Clarity in advance prevents arguments later.

According to recent reports, 2.​ Expense reimbursement. When the seller withdraws after the agent has paid for marketing, those documented costs are repaid by the seller. This is not a penalty. It keeps the agent from subsidizing a decision the seller made.

3.​ Rental conversion. If the owner chooses to rent during the term, the agent represents the rental on the same timeline. The agent built the relationship and the exposure, and that work should carry forward.

In a recent development, 4.​ Conditional release. The property comes off the MLS, but the agreement remains in force for the balance of the term. If the owner sells or chooses to list again during that period, the original agent remains the agent of record or is still owed the fee, depending on how the agreement is structured.

In a recent development, of the four, the conditional release is the one I find agents know least about, and it does the most work. It separates two things sellers tend to bundle together: removing a property from public marketing and ending a professional relationship.

When those are separated, the seller with a genuine reason gets exactly what they need, a quiet pause. The seller who was looking for a side door finds that the door is still connected to the original agreement. Either way, the agent’s investment and the relationship survive.

In a recent development, agents sometimes worry these provisions will feel adversarial. In practice, a calm, plain explanation tends to land well.

Recent findings suggest that of course. Let us pull it from the MLS while you work through the association matter. The release will be conditional, which means that should you list again while our agreement is still running, we simply continue where we left off. Per our agreement, the photography and tour expenses are repaid, and if renting becomes the proposal, I will represent the rental as well.

Most sellers respect structure. It tells them they hired someone who runs a real business.

Recent findings suggest that notice what is missing from that script. There is no argument, no guilt, and no attempt to talk the seller out of the decision. The agent acknowledges the seller’s reason, confirms the next step, and restates the terms that already exist. That sequence keeps the emotional temperature low, which matters, because a seller who leaves feeling respected is far more likely to come back, refer a friend, or call again when the association issue is resolved.

In a recent development, if you lead agents, review your standard listing agreement for these four scenarios this quarter. Then train your people to explain them at the listing appointment, not only when a seller is walking away. A provision the seller hears about for the first time during a withdrawal feels like a trap. The same provision introduced up front feels like professionalism.

A note on the buyer side as well. Agents representing buyers who know an association has a difficult or litigious history should share that with their clients, factually and with care. Protecting the client is the job on both sides of the table.

It has been revealed that agents who rebuild their inventory after every surprise withdrawal are always starting over. Agents whose agreements plan for every turn in the road keep their shelves stocked, season after season.

Serve don’t sell. Coach don’t close.
