A self is a forest. A self is a ship. A self is an illusion, and yet it is the place where we live, the costume the naked soul wears to survive in the world.

Above all, a self is a sum of selves, contextual as a chameleon, conflicted as a parliament. Which self takes the reins of our character and conduct at any given time is a function of which animating passion takes possession of us. But though we may be governed by different selves at different times, our task is to discover — and to heed — the higher, truer self that hovers above them all like the shadow of a mountain, impervious to the passing winds, holding up the sky of life. Kahlil Gibran (January 6, 1883–April 10, 1931), who thought deeply about the art of becoming yourself over the sweep of life, takes up this question in one of his signature parable-poems found in his 1918 classic The Madman (public library | free ebook).

The poem unfolds as a taxonomy of the core animating passions we live with and suffer by — sorrow, ecstasy, infatuation, hate, longing, creative possession — each a trap, a cage, a dooming fate the sufferer yearns to rebel against. Then comes the seventh self, seemingly the most pitiable, for it is the self that appears to live in pointlessness, perpetually facing the void, but in the end the freest: Only the empty self that lusts for nothing can touch the essence of everything. THE SEVEN SELVES by Kahlil Gibran.

In the stillest hour of the night, as I lay half asleep, my seven selves sat together and thus conversed in whisper:.

According to recent reports, first Self: Here, in this madman, I have dwelt all these years, with naught to do but renew his pain by day and recreate his sorrow by night. I can bear my fate no longer, and now I rebel.

Recent findings suggest that second Self: Yours is a better lot than mine, brother, for it is given to me to be this madman’s joyous self. I laugh his laughter and sing his happy hours, and with thrice winged feet I dance his brighter thoughts. It is I that would rebel against my weary existence.

Third Self: And what of me, the love-ridden self, the flaming brand of wild passion and fantastic desires? It is I the love-sick self who would rebel against this madman.

In a recent development, fourth Self: I, amongst you all, am the most miserable, for naught was given me but odious hatred and destructive loathing. It is I, the tempest-like self, the one born in the black caves of Hell, who would protest against serving this madman.

Fifth Self: Nay, it is I, the thinking self, the fanciful self, the self of hunger and thirst, the one doomed to wander without rest in search of unknown things and things not yet created; it is I, not you, who would rebel. (Related: Hyperparameter Search with Transformers and Ray Tune...)

Sixth Self: And I, the working self, the pitiful labourer, who, with patient hands, and longing eyes, fashion the days into images and give the formless elements new and eternal forms — it is I, the solitary one, who would rebel against this restless madman.

Recent findings suggest that seventh Self: How strange that you all would rebel against this man, because each and every one of you has a preordained fate to fulfill. Ah! could I but be like one of you, a self with a determined lot! But I have none, I am the do-nothing self, the one who sits in the dumb, empty nowhere and nowhen, while you are busy re-creating life. Is it you or I, neighbours, who should rebel?

When the seventh self thus spake the other six selves looked with pity upon him but stated nothing more; and as the night grew deeper one after the other went to sleep enfolded with a fresh and happy submission.

But the seventh self remained watching and gazing at nothingness, which is behind all things.
