Stewart's appearance in Frasier remains one of the sitcom's most entertaining celebrity cameos. In the season 11 episode "The Doctor Is Out," Stewart portrays Alistair Burke, a renowned opera director who mistakenly believes Frasier Crane is gay. Rather than immediately correcting the misunderstanding, Frasier lets Alistair continue pursuing him because he enjoys the exclusive social opportunities that come with their supposed relationship.

It has been revealed that stewart's performance is particularly memorable because he so effortlessly embraces Alistair's extravagance. His character showers Frasier with attention, lavish experiences, and the promise of a glamorous lifestyle, leaving the psychiatrist increasingly reluctant to admit the truth. The resulting comedy becomes even more effective because Stewart plays Alistair with complete sincerity rather than treating him as a caricature.

While Frasier was already known for its guest stars, Stewart's chemistry with Kelsey Grammer makes this appearance particularly remarkable. His sophisticated demeanor complements Frasier's personality, while Alistair's romantic confidence creates increasingly uncomfortable situations for the show's central character. Stewart's performance even earned him an Emmy nomination, demonstrating how thoroughly he could steal the spotlight in a single episode.

Few Stewart guest appearances demonstrate his willingness to ridicule his distinguished reputation quite like his performance in Ricky Gervais' Extras. Appearing as a fictionalized version of himself in the first season's finale, Stewart encounters aspiring actor Andy Millman, who hopes the established star might help advance his career. However, their conversation quickly takes an unexpected turn when Stewart begins enthusiastically describing a screenplay he's developing.

Recent findings suggest that rather than presenting himself as the sophisticated actor audiences recognize, Stewart portrays an absurdly immature version of himself whose screenplay revolves around increasingly ridiculous scenarios involving women's clothes mysteriously disappearing. His unwavering enthusiasm for these juvenile ideas becomes the centerpiece of the scene, especially as Andy struggles to understand how such an accomplished performer could find them so entertaining.

What makes Stewart's appearance particularly effective is his refusal to acknowledge the absurdity of his character's behavior. His theatrical delivery and seemingly genuine excitement transform an outrageous conversation into one of the show's funniest celebrity encounters. By enthusiastically undermining his own public image, Stewart demonstrates a remarkable sense of humor that makes his brief appearance unforgettable.

The surreal character emerges as Shah faces intense scrutiny after becoming a potential candidate to play James Bond, forcing him to confront questions surrounding his identity and career.

Stewart's involvement becomes particularly memorable when the series initially presents him as an interviewer questioning Shah about his ambitions. However, the conversation takes a disturbing turn when the audience discovers that Shah is actually speaking to the pig's head, which embodies his internalized insecurities and criticism. Stewart's authoritative delivery makes the character both intimidating and unexpectedly humorous.

Recent findings suggest that long before celebrity voice appearances became commonplace in animated television, Stewart delivered one of The Simpsons' most memorable guest performances. In the season 6 episode "Homer the Great," Stewart voices Number One, the mysterious leader of the Stonecutters, a secret society whose members enjoy considerable influence throughout Springfield. When Homer Simpson discovers the organization's existence, his attempts to join inevitably threaten its carefully maintained traditions.

According to recent reports, stewart's distinctive voice proves particularly effective in portraying Number One, whose commanding presence makes the Stonecutters appear far more vital than their ridiculous activities suggest. His character initially struggles with Homer's disruptive behavior, only to discover that Homer possesses a birthmark identifying him as the organization's prophesied Chosen One.

The humor largely comes from the contrast between Stewart's dignified delivery and the increasingly absurd circumstances surrounding the Stonecutters. Rather than exaggerating his performance to match the show's chaotic energy, Stewart approaches Number One with the seriousness audiences associate with his dramatic roles. This contrast makes his character especially entertaining and highlights why his recognizable voice was such an asset to animated comedy. Even surrounded by The Simpsons' established characters, Stewart makes Number One an unforgettable addition to the episode.
