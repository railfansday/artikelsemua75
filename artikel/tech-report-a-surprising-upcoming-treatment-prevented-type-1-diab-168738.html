📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, an experimental treatment using tiny particles released by stem cells has delayed the onset of type 1 diabetes in mice, offering a potential way to protect insulin-producing cells from immune attacks. In a 90-day study, 55% of mice receiving the particles through a slow-release gel remained diabetes-free.

Developed by UCLA researchers, the approach targets the immune response responsible for type 1 diabetes rather than simply controlling blood sugar. The findings, published in Nature Nanotechnology, suggest that naturally occurring particles called extracellular vesicles could help preserve pancreatic function. The treatment remains experimental and has not been tested in humans.

The research drew inspiration from pregnancy, when a mother’s immune system typically tolerates a developing embryo despite its genetic differences. “Nature has designed something amazing,” said senior author Song Li, a professor of bioengineering at UCLA and member of the UCLA Broad Stem Cell Research Center. “If we can better understand how an embryo avoids rejection by the mother’s immune system, it could be very useful for a lot of immune-related diseases.”.

Sources indicate that li’s team investigated extracellular vesicles, microscopic particles that transport proteins, lipids, and genetic material between cells. They compared vesicles from embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells, or iPSCs, with those from mesenchymal stem cells, which are known for their immune-regulating properties.

Experiments involving human and mouse immune cells showed that vesicles from pluripotent stem cells had stronger anti-inflammatory effects than those from mesenchymal stem cells.

The particles also promoted the development of regulatory T cells, specialized immune cells that help prevent attacks against healthy tissue. Further analysis identified distinctive proteins, lipids, and genetic material that may contribute to these effects.

To test whether the vesicles could protect against diabetes, the researchers transferred immune cells capable of attacking insulin-producing pancreatic cells into mice, then treated the animals with iPSC-derived vesicles.

Because extracellular vesicles swiftly disappear from injection sites, the researchers developed an injectable hydrogel that gradually releases them over several days. The treatment was administered beneath the skin near the neck, allowing the particles to interact with immune cells in nearby lymph nodes.

Those immune cells could then circulate throughout the body, potentially reducing attacks on pancreatic tissue.

Industry observers note that two treatments with the vesicles, either alone or in the hydrogel, significantly delayed diabetes onset compared with untreated mice. After 90 days, 55% of mice receiving the hydrogel formulation remained diabetes-free. They also had more regulatory T cells in their lymph nodes and retained more insulin-producing pancreatic tissue. “One thing that surprised us was that a local injection could provide protection throughout the body,” Li said. “That’s the beauty of this approach.”.

Producing enough extracellular vesicles for future therapies presents a major challenge. To address this, researchers grew stem cells in three-dimensional clusters suspended in gently moving liquid instead of on flat laboratory surfaces.

The method increased vesicle production 3.4-fold per million cells while preserving key particle characteristics. Because iPSCs can multiply extensively in the laboratory, they could provide a more reliable source of therapeutic vesicles than adult stem cells.

The treatment may be most effective during the early stages of type 1 diabetes, when some insulin-producing cells remain functional. Suppressing the immune attack could help preserve these cells and maintain natural insulin production.

Sources indicate that “In the early stages, you still have some pancreatic cells left,” Li said. “If you can regulate the immune cells, you may be able to keep the remaining cells functional.”.

It has been revealed that for patients who have already lost most of these cells, immune regulation alone would not restore insulin production. However, the approach might help protect transplanted insulin-producing cells from rejection.

Sources indicate that researchers strategy to evaluate the treatment in larger animal models, investigate dosing and long-term effectiveness, and determine whether it could weaken defenses against infection. The findings may also inform future treatments for other autoimmune diseases and strategies to protect transplanted cells.
