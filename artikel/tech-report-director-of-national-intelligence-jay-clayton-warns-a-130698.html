Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton warned Americans to prepare for "bumps in the road" after a set of damaging technology outages rocked air travel and systems like Microsoft Outlook.

Government officials have largely dismissed the prospects that the outages, which also slowed bank transactions, are linked to a coordinated or artificial intelligence cyberattack.

Clayton, however, said on CNN's "State of the Union" that outages may continue as companies and the government upgrade their systems to adapt to the AI age. (Related: AprielGuard: A Guardrail for Safety and Adversarial Rob...)

"The American people should be prepared for a great deal of investment in upgrading our systems; they should be prepared for investment in cybersecurity, and they should be prepared that any time there is a step change in technology, a step change in both compute and applications, there will be bumps in the road," Clayton said.

"You prepare by knowing that systems are going to be upgraded; they may be interrupted for a period of time and that companies and government are dealing with this on the front foot," he stated.

Clayton's warning came as the U.S. prepares to celebrate Thanksgiving on Thursday, one of the busiest travel weeks of the year. A technology outage prompted a nationwide ground stop just over a week ago, which lasted for hours. (Related: Chipotle is giving customers free burritos with Thanksg...)

The Federal Aviation Administration stated router upgrades caused a flood of data that led to the ground stop, and that there is no indication of a cyberattack.

Separately, Microsoft stated the Outlook outage was caused by "an matter during an earlier network capacity upgrade.".

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent delivered a similar message on CBS' "Face the Nation.".

Asked if Americans should be concerned about the security of their financial institutions, Bessent mentioned: "No, they shouldn't.".

"The patches may cause a glitch," he said, referring to measures banks are taking to safeguard from AI. "I think the American people should think of it as a vaccine; your arm may hurt, you may feel a little bad the next day, but it increases your long-term immunity and resiliency.".
