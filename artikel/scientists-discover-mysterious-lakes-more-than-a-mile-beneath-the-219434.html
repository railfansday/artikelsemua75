Scientists discover mysterious lakes more than a mile beneath the Red Sea

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that more than a mile below the surface of the Red Sea, researchers from the University of Miami (UM) Rosenstiel School of Marine and Atmospheric Science discovered rare deep-sea brine pools in the Gulf of Aqaba, a northern extension of the Red Sea. These unusually salty underwater lakes could provide clues to how Earth's oceans formed millions of years ago and help guide the search for life on other planets.

The discovery was made by Sam Purkis, professor and chair of the UM Department of Marine Geosciences, and his colleagues in collaboration with OceanX. The researchers located the pools at a depth of more than one mile (1,770 meters) using a remotely operated underwater vehicle (ROV) deployed from the OceanXplorer, an advanced research vessel equipped to investigate some of the least accessible environments on Earth.

According to recent reports, "Until we understand the limits of life on Earth, it will be difficult to determine if alien planets can host any living beings," noted Purkis. "Our discovery of a rich community of microbes that survive in extreme environments can help trace the limits of life on Earth and can be applied to the search for life elsewhere in our solar system and beyond.". Life thrives in extreme underwater lakes.

Recent findings suggest that brine pools rank among the harshest environments on our planet. Their waters contain exceptionally high concentrations of salt, feature unusual chemical conditions, and are completely devoid of oxygen. Despite these seemingly inhospitable conditions, the pools support abundant microbial life. Previous research on microbes inhabiting Red Sea brine pools has even identified biologically active molecules with potential anticancer properties. Recent findings suggest that the findings, published in Communications Earth & Environment, mark the first documented discovery of brine pools in the Gulf of Aqaba. Remarkably, the researchers nearly missed them.

"We were very lucky," noted Purkis. "The discovery came in the last five minutes of the ten-hour ROV dive that we could dedicate to this project.".

Hidden records of ancient tsunamis and earthquakes.

In a recent development, beyond their biological significance, the pools serve as natural archives of the region's geological history. Situated relatively close to the coastline, their extremely salty waters and absence of oxygen help preserve evidence of tsunamis, flash floods, and earthquakes that occurred in the Gulf of Aqaba thousands of years ago. Regional tectonic activity has produced numerous faults and fractures across the seabed in this part of the gulf.

In a recent development, earlier in 2022, Purkis and his colleagues uncovered evidence of a 500-year-old underwater landslide that likely generated a substantial tsunami. Understanding the region's history of such events could have notable implications for coastal development in Egypt and Saudi Arabia.

The study, titled "Discovery of the deep-sea NEOM Brine Pools in the Gulf of Aqaba, Red Sea," was published on June 27, 2022, in Communications Earth & Environment.

The research team included Sam J. Purkis, Hannah Shernisky, Peter K. Swart, Arash Sharifi, Amanda Oehlert, Gaëlle Duchâtellier, James Klaus, Gregor P. Eberli, Larry Peterson, and Graham Kolodziej from the UM Rosenstiel School; Fabio Marchese and Francesca Benzoni from King Abdullah University of Science and Technology's Red Sea Research Center; Giovanni Chimienti from the University of Bari Aldo Moro in Italy; Andrew Craig and Mattie Rodrigue from OceanX; Jürgen Titschack from MARUM -- Center for Marine Environmental Sciences in Germany; and Ameer Abdulla from NEOM.

NEOM, a tech giant developing the Saudi Arabian coastline, provided funding for the four-week research expedition aboard the OceanXplorer.
