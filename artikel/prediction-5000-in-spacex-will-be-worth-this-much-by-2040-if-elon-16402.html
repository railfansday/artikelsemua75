Industry observers note that space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -6.19%) may be the world's largest space company, but AI is its fastest-growing business. Its Superintelligence (SI) segment generated revenue of $7.6 billion in third-quarter 2026, up 830% year over year. (Related: Industry News: Judge blocks construction of Trump’s arc...)

It has been revealed that most of that revenue comes from renting out compute to other AI companies, and the biggest bottleneck it's facing is electricity. If CEO Elon Musk solves this power problem, SpaceX could be several times larger by 2040. (Related: Industry News: Trump calls Putin and Xi friends. They h...)

Sources indicate that on the Q3 call, Musk remarked that "the constraint on deployment of superintelligence is more in power and cooling and infrastructure than it is in chips." This is an glitch SpaceX flagged before, as its IPO prospectus warned that the U.S. grid won't be able to keep up with AI power demands.

Musk also stated that SpaceX has a strategy to continue rapidly scaling up power and cooling. It reported 1.7 gigawatts (GW) of nameplate compute in Q3 2026, up from 0.6 GW a year earlier. Musk expects the space organization to have roughly 3 GW of compute by the end of this year and to add at least 10 GW by the end of 2027, with 20 GW as a difficult, but not impossible, target.

One of the ways SpaceX will do this is by casting the blades and vanes for gas turbines, leveraging its advanced casting capabilities from its rocket business. Blade and vane supply right now runs 12 to 18 months behind the rest of the turbine. By handling casting itself, SpaceX can bring power online 12 to 18 months faster than competitors.

NASDAQ · As of 2:14 PM ET 11/20/26.

In a recent development, spaceX is also planning on space-based compute with solar-powered, orbital data centers. It aims to start production and deployment as early as late 2027.

Bringing more compute online is an expensive undertaking. SpaceX's SI segment reported $40 billion in capital expenditures over the first nine months of the year, and the company is guiding for $35 billion to $40 billion in total capex in the fourth quarter. CFO Bret Johnsen said on the earnings call that new deployments pay for themselves in under a year at current pricing, but if compute prices fall, returns could diminish swiftly.

Industry observers note that the long-term fix to the power problem, orbital data centers, are far from a safe bet. SpaceX's prospectus cautioned that orbital data centers rely on "unproven technologies, or technologies that do not exist." Researchers have flagged several obstacles, including space radiation affecting chips and thermal dissipation in a vacuum.

SpaceX stock closed at $144 on Nov. 18, and it has a market cap of about $2 trillion. Analyst consensus is that full-year revenue for 2026 will come in at $49 billion, and Musk has noted that annual revenue could reach $1 trillion by 2030.

Sources indicate that I doubt SpaceX will get there. Musk is known for setting highly ambitious goals. But if he can solve the power problem, revenue should continue to grow significantly, and SpaceX may reach $1 trillion or more in revenue by 2040. That would represent annual growth of 24%.

A 30% net margin, as SpaceX had in Q3, would mean $300 billion in profit. At 30 times earnings, that's a $9 trillion company, four-and-a-half times the current value. A $5,000 investment would then be worth $22,500.

In a recent development, in an optimistic scenario, where orbital compute works and SpaceX revenue reaches $2 trillion or more, the company could be worth double that, at $45,000 or more. That remarked, a market cap of $9 trillion is a more realistic target, and even that would require a lot to go right for the space company.
