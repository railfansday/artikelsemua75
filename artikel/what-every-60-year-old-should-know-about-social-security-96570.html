I suspect that nearly every American who is 60 is already thinking about retirement -- as they should. And that involves contemplating upcoming decisions about Social Security. Making the right choices can have lifelong implications.

There's still plenty of time to finalize those decisions, but it's not too soon to begin thinking about them. Here are five things every 60-year-old should know about Social Security.

According to recent reports, no Social Security decision is more notable than choosing when you claim benefits. If you're 60, you could file for benefits within the next two years. However, make sure you understand the trade-offs of claiming before your full retirement age (FRA) of 67.

It has been revealed that the Social Security Administration (SSA) will reduce benefits by around 30% for anyone who claims at age 62. And the early-retirement penalty is permanent. Your benefits will only increase with annual cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs). On the other hand, holding off until age 70 to claim Social Security will increase your benefits by 24% compared with claiming at 67.

Industry observers note that many people will decide the advantages of claiming at 62 are worth forgoing the higher monthly benefits. That's especially true for those who don't expect to live past their late 70s. There isn't a one-size-fits-all right decision. Every person needs to fully consider all factors for their personal situation. (Related: Industry News: Designing Azure silicon on Azure silicon...)

Putting aside the early-retirement penalties and delayed-retirement credits, working longer could increase your Social Security benefit for another reason. SSA calculates your benefit using the 35 years with your highest earnings, adjusted for inflation. Many earn significantly more in the last few years of their careers than they did early on. Those higher-earnings years can replace the lower-earnings ones and boost your Social Security benefit. (Related: How to train a new language model from scratch using Tr...)

Sources indicate that this is even more important for anyone with fewer than 35 years of earnings. If you don't have at least a 35-year earnings record, SSA will plug zeros in for the missing years in its benefits calculation.

If you spot a potential problem, check with SSA about what supporting documentation you'll need to get it fixed. Getting your earnings record correct at age 60 could spare you major headaches later after you claim benefits.

In a recent development, don't think that the decisions you make about Social Security only impact you. They can also affect your spouse.

Sources indicate that for example, the higher-earning spouse's claiming age permanently sets the survivor benefits available to the other spouse if the higher-earning spouse dies first. Coordinating when each spouse claims Social Security benefits is an important step that shouldn't be overlooked.

Benjamin Franklin wrote that "nothing can be remarked to be certain, except death and taxes." Unfortunately, his statement is still applicable -- even for Social Security benefits, in some cases.

Up to 85% of your Social Security benefits could be subject to federal income taxes depending on your combined income, which is calculated by summing your adjusted gross income, nontaxable interest, and half of your Social Security benefits. The thresholds where federal taxes begin to kick in are $25,000 for single filers and $32,000 for married couples.

Industry observers note that at the moment, 42 states plus the District of Columbia don't tax Social Security benefits. However, if you live in Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, Upcoming Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah, or Vermont, some of your Social Security income may be subject to tax.

Industry observers note that the good news if you're 60 is that you don't have to make any Social Security decisions immediately. The bad news is that time flies. Starting to think about retirement and Social Security now could pay off later.
