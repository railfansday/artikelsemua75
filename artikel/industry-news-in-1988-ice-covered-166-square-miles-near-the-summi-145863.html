Landsat images show the ice near the summit of Puncak Jaya in Indonesia has dwindled to small patches that no longer flow like glaciers.

In 1988, glaciers covered about 1.66 square miles (4.31 square kilometers) near the summit of Puncak Jaya, on the island of Fresh Guinea. By September 2025, about 0.03 square miles (0.09 square kilometers) were left.

That’s roughly 2 percent. What’s left would fit on about a dozen soccer fields.

It also no longer moves. The patches are too thin and too broken up to behave like a glacier, NASA Earth Observatory reported on November 16.

It has been revealed that puncak Jaya stands in the Sudirman Range, in the Indonesian part of Fresh Guinea. Very few places this close to the equator have held ice all year round. (Related: Hugging Face Reads, Feb. 2021 - Long-range Transformers...)

In a recent development, next door is the largest stretch of warm ocean water on the planet. So the glaciers here sit near the warmest conditions in which year-round ice can survive at all.

According to recent reports, scientists have called them high-altitude canaries in the coal mine. Other members of that small club are what’s left of Humboldt Glacier in Venezuela, the ice fields of Kilimanjaro in Tanzania and the Quelccaya Ice Cap in Peru, a country that has lost more than half of its glaciers in six decades.

Sources indicate that NASA compared two false-color pictures of the range. Landsat 5 took one on November 3, 1988. Landsat 9 took the other on October 19, 2026.

Ice shows up light blue, bare rock brown, clouds white and plants green. In 1988, blue caps several of the highest ridges. In 2026 there are only a few small blue fragments, and brown rock covers most of the old icy ridgetops.

The area figures come from Donaldi Permana, a climate scientist at Indonesia’s Agency for Meteorology, Climatology, and Geophysics (BMKG). The 2025 number is based on satellite and aerial data. (Related: Latest Update: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

According to recent reports, an aerial survey is planned for December. It should show how much ice is left in 2026, Permana noted.

The ice got thinner, too. At the main monitoring site, on the East Northwall Firn, it was 105 feet (32 meters) thick in 2010, Permana remarked.

According to recent reports, in 2023 it measured 13 feet (4 meters). By November 2024 there was no ice there at all.

Thinning ice also cracks apart. Each upcoming piece has fresh edges where it can melt, and so the losses feed on themselves.

In a recent development, in the 2026 image, newly bared rock sits between the remnants. Snow still falls on the mountain. It just doesn’t stay on the bare rock long enough to turn into new ice.

Sources indicate that “It’s a house of cards problem,” stated Chris Shuman, a retired University of Maryland glaciologist who has followed Puncak Jaya’s glaciers in satellite images. “Once this process gets going, there’s really no turning back.”.

To count as a glacier, a body of ice generally has to do two things. It has to be thick enough to creep downhill under its own weight. And it needs a zone where snow piles up faster than it melts.

On Puncak Jaya, the ice is now so thin, and so split into small, stagnant patches, that it has stopped flowing.

“While small ice remnants persist in high-altitude crevices around Puncak Jaya, their functional life as active glaciers is effectively over,” Permana mentioned.

El Niño years are especially rough on these glaciers. Warmer air pushes the freezing level above the summit, so rain falls instead of snow.

Fresh snow is white. It bounces sunlight away and protects the ice underneath. Without it, the darker surface soaks up heat and melts faster.

During the 2015-2016 El Niño, the ice thinned about five times faster than normal, a finding NASA links to a study in PNAS. In one year it lost roughly 17 feet (5.3 meters) of thickness.

Then came the 2023-2024 El Niño. The ice thinned again, by about 7 to 13 feet (2 to 4 meters) a year.

Recent satellite studies suggest the main ice areas could be gone completely around 2030. For this, NASA links a study published in The Cryosphere.

BMKG’s own estimates suggest the ice could disappear much sooner, Permana noted, with the current El Niño speeding things up. Either way, Puncak Jaya’s ice looks set to vanish well before Africa’s remaining glaciers, which may be gone by 2040.

NASA links the 2 percent figure to a World Meteorological Organization report, State of the Climate in the South-West Pacific 2025.

The information was obtained from NASA Earth Observatory.
