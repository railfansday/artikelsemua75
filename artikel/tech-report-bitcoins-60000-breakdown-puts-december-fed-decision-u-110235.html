Tech Report: Bitcoin’s $60,000 breakdown puts December Fed decision under the spotlight

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Bitcoin fell below $60,000 on Friday for the first time since July, with US spot Bitcoin funds recording withdrawals and short-term Treasury bills offering investors more than 4% a year. That creates a problem for the Federal Reserve's December meeting: traders expect a quarter-point rate increase, while Governor Christopher Waller has argued that persistent inflation could justify twice as much.

It has been revealed that there's also another problem with the schedule. Officials will announce their decision and fresh rate projections on Dec. 9, while November's consumer price index arrives the following morning. Investors could spend Wednesday pricing the Fed's view of inflation and Thursday deciding whether the newly introduced data support it, with Bitcoin already under strain before either announcement. Recent findings suggest that the Fed held its target rate at 3.75% to 4.00% in October, with Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack voting for a quarter-point increase and Waller siding with the majority to wait. He explained that decision as a matter of pace: if markets already believed the Fed would tighten, it could afford to hold off for a meeting so long as the promised increases eventually happened.

It has been revealed that by Nov. 10, Waller was warning that the next move might need to be bigger. In a speech delivered before the October CPI release, he said officials should consider raising rates by 50 basis points in December if core consumer prices, which exclude food and energy, kept climbing by roughly 0.3% a month. The figures published hours later met that condition: core CPI rose 0.3% in October, the third consecutive monthly increase of that size. It has been revealed that waller pressed the point again in a Nov. 18 television interview, saying the Fed should either make the larger December move or pair a quarter-point increase with a convincing message that further tightening would follow. The October meeting minutes, also released Nov. 18, show that a couple of participants had already raised the possibility of more than 25 basis points in December if inflation ran hot. The minutes don't identify those participants, so they can't be used to attribute that discussion to Waller.

Industry observers note that however, traders still favored the smaller move. In a Nov. 19 research note, UOB said futures pricing as of Nov. 18 implied a quarter-point increase, with roughly a 7.6% chance of an additional quarter point. These were market-implied odds at a particular moment, rather than a vote count or a promise from the Fed.

A 25-basis-point increase would lift the target to 4.00%–4.25%, while a 50-basis-point move would take it to 4.25%–4.50%. Investors will need to distinguish between getting to that higher range sooner and the Fed deciding that interest rates must eventually go higher than previously expected. A larger December hike that merely brings forward a planned January increase may have a different effect on longer-term yields than a upcoming projection for a higher peak rate.

Sources indicate that officials will have the October personal consumption expenditures data on Nov. 25 and the November employment report on Dec. 4 before they vote. PCE is the inflation measure used for the Fed's 2% target, though CPI also informs its judgment.

The December meeting runs Dec. 8–9, with newly introduced projections due alongside the decision, and November CPI is scheduled for Dec. 10. That leaves room for the bond market to react to the Fed's outlook and then reassess it less than a day later.

It has been revealed that bitcoin was around $61,900 early Friday, but it later broke below $60,000. CryptoSlate reported a price near $59,470 during the US trading session, as technology stocks also fell. The break came with funds that had helped bring institutional demand into Bitcoin recording another heavy day of net withdrawals.

According to recent reports, US spot Bitcoin ETFs registered $582.3 million in net outflows on Nov. 19, according to Farside Investors. BlackRock's IBIT accounted for $541.1 million of that day's total, or roughly 93%.

Across the 14 November trading sessions through Thursday, the funds had lost a net $3.47 billion, with IBIT accounting for about 85% of those withdrawals. CryptoSlate's separate report details how close that puts November to the industry's monthly outflow record.

Those numbers measure net flows into and out of the funds, rather than the number of investors selling Bitcoin directly on exchanges. They also can't establish whether investors were reacting to the Fed, taking profits, or raising cash for other reasons. Still, they show that Bitcoin was losing a source of investment demand well before a December policy surprise became a possibility.

It has been revealed that short-term Treasury debt now pays investors considerably more than it did when rates were near zero. On Nov. 19, three-month Treasury yields stood at 4.34% and two-year yields at 4.76%. UOB reported that the dollar index had reached a 19-month high on Nov. 18. Bitcoin doesn't pay interest, and higher financing costs make leveraged positions harder to maintain. Falling prices can then force traders short of collateral to sell, adding orders to a market already dealing with fund withdrawals.

The Fed's minutes also described financial risks beyond crypto, including debt and financing arrangements tied to the AI buildout that could suffer if expected returns disappoint. Those risks help explain why borrowing costs can affect different speculative assets at once, although they don't prove that AI financing or Fed remarks caused any particular Bitcoin ETF redemption.

It has been revealed that the December decision will give markets a new rate target and a set of projections to price. The next morning's CPI report could make that path look too cautious or too aggressive, leaving Bitcoin investors to trade through both judgments while the fund flows show whether fresh buyers have returned.
