Watching 'The Wiz' Is Both Tradition and Medicine

Sources indicate that I don’t know about your family, but every holiday season starting in November and ending January 1st, my family is definitely watching "The Wiz"; it’s a family tradition. In fact, it’s a tradition for many Black families across America. We pile onto sofas, sing, dance, cry, and debate, and it is tradition. But it also is medicine. (Related: What I do every time I get an IRS notice...)

Recent findings suggest that "The Wiz" has been healing Black families since 1978. Science is just now catching up to what your grandmother already knew.

It has been revealed that when your family settles in for a ritual you have done together before—the same movie, the same couch, the same debates about the same scenes—your nervous system registers something specific: safety. Predictable shared experience signals to the brain that threat is low and belonging is high. Oxytocin, the neurochemical of trust and bonding, is released during group ritual. Cortisol drops. The vagus nerve, the body’s primary regulation highway, begins to settle. This is not metaphor; it is the physiology of communal gathering, and families have been administering it with precision for decades. (Related: Primary vs Contingent Beneficiary: What's the Diff...)

Recent findings suggest that when “You Can’t Win” comes on and you sing along, loud and proud and probably off-key, you are activating your vagus nerve directly. Humming, chanting, and singing stimulate the vagal branches in the throat and chest in ways that measurably increase heart rate variability, the primary marker of nervous system regulation. Group singing amplifies this effect. Research indicates that people who sing together synchronize their heart rates. Your family is not only performing. They are co-regulating.

According to recent reports, add in the dancing—spontaneous, in your living room, because you cannot help it when “Brand Newly introduced Day” comes on and you have activated somatic release. Movement is one of the most effective tools we have for discharging stored stress from the body. Bessel van der Kolk spent a career documenting what dancers and African traditional healers demonstrated before the research did: The body holds what the mind cannot process, and movement releases it.

There is always a moment in "The Wiz" when someone in your family loses it completely. The tears, the laughter, every time; this is not weakness. Shared laughter measurably reduces cortisol, increases natural killer cell activity, and produces documented improvements in immune function. Laughter researcher Lee Berk at Loma Linda University has been publishing on this for decades. Your family’s annual hysteria over the crows or flying monkeys is a clinical-grade immune intervention.

When Dorothy finally gets home, when the music swells, and Lena Horne sings “Believe in Yourself,” and everyone starts crying before the scene even finishes because we have seen it 20 times and it still does this to us; what is happening is awe. Psychologist Dacher Keltner at UC Berkeley has documented awe as one of the most powerful mental health interventions identified in contemporary science. Awe reduces self-focused thinking, increases prosocial behavior, and produces measurable reductions in inflammatory markers. And because awe almost always arrives alongside emotional release—the tears, the full-body exhale, the feeling of being cracked open in the best possible way—it also completes what the dancing started. The body finishes processing what it has been carrying. Your tears are data. Your relief is the point.

According to recent reports, "The Wiz" is not just a movie. It is a Black retelling of the oldest story we tell: the journey home to self, to community, to wholeness. Dorothy is doing identity reclamation. The Scarecrow is reclaiming his mind. The Lion is reclaiming his courage. The Tin Man is reclaiming his heart. Our ancestors told this story in circles, around fires, through spirituals. "The Wiz" put it on a screen in 1978, in full Black excellence, and handed it to us as a holiday tradition.

When elders and children watch it together, something else happens. Cultural identity, racial socialization, and connection to Black heritage and community are documented protective factors against the mental health effects of racism. Annual Review of Clinical Psychology research confirms that ethnic and racial identity functions as a clinical buffer. Intergenerational storytelling is not nostalgia. It is immunization.

If "The Wiz" is already your tradition, protect it. Deepen it. Tell your children what is actually happening when you all pile onto that couch. If you lost the tradition somewhere along the way, bring it back this holiday season. If you never had it, start it this year.

Watch it alone if you have to. Let Diana Ross, Michael Jackson, Nipsey Russell, Ted Ross, and Lena Horne be your circle. In my work on public health and ancestral healing, and in my book Ancestral Roots, I return again and again to the truth that chosen family is real family. Your nervous system does not distinguish between blood and chosen. It only knows safety, belonging, and joy.

"The Wiz" delivers all three. Every single time.
