Sources indicate that lab-grown meat is having an absolute field day on social media, as tons of outlandish claims and conspiracy theories about it are forming: The lab-grown industry is trying to sneak it into our grocery carts by labeling it as natural meat! Here’s why you need to avoid it at all costs! In truth, there’s a lot more to the story, and a ton of those claims are false.

Sources indicate that formally known as cultivated meat, lab-grown meat has been in development for more than a decade, but it’s only recently begun to capture mainstream attention. In 2023, it received regulatory clearance from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). Now, it’s beginning to make its way into the market (albeit in limited quantities), says Steven Surico, RD, a registered dietitian with the Food and Nutrition Department at Good Samaritan University Hospital. As such, lab-grown meat is becoming a grossly misunderstood concept. (Cue: The recent influx of misleading social media videos and AI-generated content.) These posts can blur the line between fact and fiction, leaving consumers unsure of what’s real, scaring them when there’s nothing to be afraid of. “The concern is that cultivated meat is something highly artificial, when in reality, it’s not,” states Surico.

Here’s everything you need to know about lab-grown meat, including what it is, whether it’s being sold in stores, and if you should truly worry about it, according to experts.

Cultivated meat is grown directly from animal cells, as opposed to natural meat that’s produced by raising and slaughtering an animal, indicates Elliot Swartz, PhD, a cultivated meat researcher and the senior principal scientist for cultivated meat at The Good Food Institute.

“A small sample of cells is fed basic nutrients, like amino acids, fats, vitamins, salts, and sugars, in clean, controlled environments similar to those used to make yogurt, cheese, and beer, where the cells are grown and developed into meat or seafood,” he explains. As the cells multiply and develop, they form the muscle and other tissues that make up meat. Once mature, Swartz notes they’re harvested and prepared as a product that should, at least in theory, offer the same taste, smell, texture, and consistency as ordinary meat.

According to recent reports, lab-grown meat vs. plant-based alternatives: “Beyond Meat products are made primarily from plant ingredients and do not contain animal muscle cells, whereas cultivated meat starts with animal cells and grows those cells into animal tissue,” Surico claims.

Recent findings suggest that cultivated meat may address some of the challenges associated with natural meat production, particularly when it comes to food safety, public health, nutrition, and the environmental impact of raising livestock. According to Swartz, one of the biggest advantages is the greater control over the production process, which could offer significant food safety benefits.

In a recent development, research highlights that raising large populations of animals in close quarters can create opportunities for bacteria, other pathogens, and diseases to spread. This can increase the risk of transmission to humans through food (or animal) contact, but cultivated meat could help reduce these risks, Swartz explains.

It has been revealed that cultivated meat is also better for the environment by reducing the land, water, and greenhouse gas emissions needed for meat production. Research from The Good Food Institute even suggests that if cultivated meat can be produced commercially in large quantities using clean energy, it could have a significantly smaller environmental footprint than conventional meat.

Industry observers note that finally, because the cells are grown in a controlled environment, researchers can influence the nutritional composition of the meat, indicates Lauri Wright, RDN, a registered dietitian and associate professor of nutrition and dietetics at the University of South Florida College of Public Health. For example, they can adjust the amount and types of fat in the meat to reduce saturated fat and cholesterol while increasing desirable nutrients like protein, she explains. Theoretically, this could lead to healthier meat that tastes the same, studies suggest.

According to recent reports, while some people are afraid of lab-grown meat because they’re not really sure what’s in it, each product undergoes extensive testing by the FDA and USDA. So, its nutritional content is thoroughly tested to ensure it meets food safety standards, adds Swartz.

In a recent development, yes, but availability is extremely limited. Cultivated meat has received approval from the FDA and USDA for sale in the U.S., as well as in Singapore and Australia, per The Good Food Institute. However, regulatory approval doesn’t necessarily mean that products are widely available. Plus, eight states in the U.S. have enacted bans or temporary restrictions on cultivated meat, including Alabama, Florida, Indiana, Mississippi, Montana, South Dakota, Nebraska, and Texas. These restrictions are largely driven by concerns about protecting traditional agriculture and livestock industries.

According to recent reports, so, contrary to what social media might have you believing, cultivated meat isn’t something you can pick up at your local supermarket just yet. In the U.S., the only product right now being served is Wildtype’s cultivated salmon, which is available at a handful of select restaurants in Washington D.C., Oregon, Arizona, and California.

It has been revealed that while it’s difficult to predict exactly when lab-grown meat will be in grocery stores, Swartz expects a gradual rollout, with smaller, more specialized retailers being the first to carry it. “You could start to see cultivated meat products more widely introduced in higher-end retailers in the early 2030s, but I don’t think you’ll see it in big box grocery stores until the late 2030s,” he claims.

In grocery stores, it’ll be clearly labeled as lab-grown or cultivated meat. “Due to the USDA regulations and labeling, it should be very clear to the consumer if they are buying regular meat or lab-grown meat,” says Surico. In fact, federal law prohibits food from being misbranded or carrying false or misleading labels, and regulators have remarked cultivated meat products must be clearly and accurately labeled.

As for the price, that remains to be seen. Cultivated meat is presently more expensive to produce than most conventional meat and seafood, but Swartz indicates the long-term goal is to bring production costs down to match or fall below those of conventional options.

Sources indicate that not very. “You should not be freaked out,” states Wright. “If anything, you should see it as potential, because the future of lab-grown meat could give consumers more options and alternatives to what they buy and put into their body.”.

Swartz agrees, pointing out that the benefits go beyond offering another way to produce meat. “Whether you care about the environment, animal welfare, food safety, or public health, there’s a lot of potential benefits for cultivated meat that are additive in terms of value for the consumer,” he says.

Of course, that doesn’t mean everyone has to embrace cultivated meat—traditional meat isn’t going away or being phased out of grocery stores or restaurants. The goal is simply to give consumers another option, not replace the foods they already know and enjoy. And if cultivated meat isn’t your jam, that’s perfectly fine, too.
