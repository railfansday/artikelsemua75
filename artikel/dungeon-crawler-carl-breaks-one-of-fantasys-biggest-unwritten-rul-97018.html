Dungeon Crawler Carl is arguably one of the most successful modern science fantasy IPs in recent memory, with author Matt Dinniman's universe growing to eight massive novels since its humble beginnings as a self-published project on Royal Road. Dinniman has always had a relatively unique and unconventional approach to storytelling, but one that has captivated readers more and more with each fresh installment.

Dungeon Crawler Carl's world-building, character backstories, and unexpected array of enemies, allies, and aliens are just the beginning of what makes the story so special. One of the most interesting aspects of how the story has evolved over its lifetime is that many of the most unique and outlandish details aren't even created by Dinniman alone. The author actually breaks a longstanding "unwritten rule" among fantasy/sci-fi writers, and it's safe to say that the creative choice has paid off time and time again. Industry observers note that these aren't just simple polls about favorite characters or potential storylines, and Dinniman gives readers different possibilities for everything from enemies and rewards to locations, names, and other unexpected details. Some of these decisions are relatively minor, while others have ended up playing key roles in the larger narrative. In many cases, the results have even caught Dinniman himself off guard. In a recent development, importantly, this doesn't mean readers are deciding what Carl does or what Donut claims. Dinniman maintains creative control over his characters and their decisions, instead allowing fans to influence the external circumstances they encounter. He then has to figure out how to incorporate those choices into the story, even when readers select something completely unexpected.

It's a fairly unconventional approach to writing a fantasy series, and one that breaks the "unwritten rule" that many fantasy authors follow of keeping the world-building elements of the story very intentional and deliberate. In many ways, Dinniman allows readers to throw him curveballs as some sort of a challenge to himself to make these things work on the page, and so far there are countless examples of how it's paid off.
