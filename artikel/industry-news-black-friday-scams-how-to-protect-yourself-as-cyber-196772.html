With Black Friday almost upon us, it's time to talk about something other than what you should buy or how much it costs.

Recent findings suggest that the biggest shopping day of the year in the United States can also be one of the most precarious, thanks to an increased reliance on e-commerce in the age of rampant cyberattacks and system outages. There's only so much that you, as an individual shopper, can do to protect yourself if an online retailer is compromised or if you're targeted by a scam artist, but it's crucial to understand those measures regardless.

Here are some common-sense things you can do to make sure Black Friday doesn't become a massive headache for you or your family.

One of the main ways that bad actors will try to get you during this holiday shopping season is through phishing, which describes a variety of techniques used by scammers to trick people into giving up money or personal information.

For example, you may get text messages or emails warning of an urgent problem, such as someone using your card to buy something fraudulently, or something more mundane like a delivery notification. While you don't want to outright ignore these warnings because there is always a slim possibility that they are real, you need to double and triple-check things like the sender's email address or a URL or phone number they've given you to solve the "problem." Email phishing requests tend to come from odd-looking, non-standard email addresses, or ones that are misspelled in some way. Generally speaking, companies will not reach out to you over text or email urgently asking for payment or personal information. If you encounter something like this, you can promptly run a Google search of the number, address, or the nature of the request, and you'll potentially find other people warning against the phishing scam. Above all else, exercise as much caution as possible and, if needed, reach out directly to the organization in question if you need to verify a request of some sort.

Retailer websites usually give customers the ability to make an account or otherwise save payment and shipping information to make the checkout process more straightforward. You can do this if you want, but given the number of cyberattacks against retailers, doing so puts your information at risk of being compromised.

There is no perfect solution because no form of online payment is 100 percent free from danger. However, using services like Apple Pay or even PayPal can allay the risk a little bit, especially if you use secure multi-factor authentication for those services. In fact, you should use MFA and strong, unique passwords for every account you need to have. It's even better to use a good password manager, which can come up with strong passwords for you that you don't even need to remember.

Lastly, sometimes around Black Friday, you'll see deals that seem incredibly generous. Maybe an 80-inch OLED TV is going for $150, or a TikTok account is promising the Nintendo Switch 2 for under $100. In these cases, it's best to understand that you might be getting taken for a ride. Black Friday deals aren't that good, unfortunately.

Industry observers note that sadly, there's no hard-and-fast rule to follow here, other than "be wary of anything that seems too good to be true." That's especially true if the deal is coming from a website or service you've never heard of before. It's one thing to see screaming good deals on slightly more trustworthy platforms like Amazon, but another thing entirely if the retailer in question is obscure or sketchy.
