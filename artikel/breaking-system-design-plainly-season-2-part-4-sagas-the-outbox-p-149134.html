📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that part 3 gave you the protocol that makes several machines agree on one outcome — and demonstrated why it blocks. This part is the escape hatch: give up the single agreement, commit step by step, and keep an undo action ready for every step. That is the saga, and the transactional outbox is the small, boring mechanism that makes sagas actually work in production.

It has been revealed that book a trip. One service reserves the seat, a second takes the payment, a third books the room, a fourth sends the confirmation email. The room booking fails after the payment has landed. The customer has now paid for a trip that does not exist. There is no single database transaction spanning four services, so there is no rollback. What you do instead is run the steps in reverse: refund the payment, release the seat, cancel the booking — each undo a compensating transaction, written in advance, one per step.

The benchmark hook for this part is the compensation economics, and it runs in the opposite direction from the intuition that "failure handling is an edge case." With five steps each failing 1% of the time, 4.9% of sagas run a compensation — roughly one saga in twenty. At one million multi-step jobs a day with ten steps each and a 1-in-1,000 step failure rate, that is ~10,000 sagas a day needing their undo path. And the second-order failure is the one that actually hurts: if a compensation itself fails 1% of the time, ~1 saga in 1,040 ends unresolved — sitting in a queue for a human, about 960 a day at that volume. (Part 3 quoted ~1 in 2,040; that assumed a 0.5% compensation-failure rate. The unresolved rate is exquisitely sensitive to that one number, which is exactly the point: your saga is only as reliable as its worst compensation.).

Recent findings suggest that you are assembling flat-pack furniture with a friend, but you live in different houses and can only text. You agree on an order: you build the frame, your friend attaches the legs, you add the shelves, your friend tightens everything. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Halfway through, your friend texts: "the legs don't fit." In a single-database world you would both rewind time and pretend nothing happened. In the real world, the frame is already built — you cannot un-build it by wishing. So each of you agreed, before starting, on the undo for your own step: your friend unscrews the legs, you disassemble the frame, and you both end up back where you started, one undo at a time, in reverse order.

Recent findings suggest that that is a saga. Three rules make it work: every step commits immediately (no locks held across services); every step has a named compensation written before the saga starts, not improvised after a failure; and every compensation is idempotent — running it twice has the same effect as running it once — because the network will redeliver, the orchestrator will restart, and your refund will be attempted twice.

According to recent reports, A saga is a sequence of local transactions, each atomic inside one service's own database. After each step commits, the saga records its progress in a durable log — the saga log — and triggers the next step. If a step fails, the saga walks backward, running each completed step's compensation in reverse order. The saga never blocks: a failed step is undone, locks were already released when each local transaction committed, and no participant waits on any other participant's recovery.

The choice is usually decided by one question: when a saga fails at 3 a.m., who gets paged, and can they see what happened? Production teams that have run both tend to pick orchestration for that reason. One practitioner report — 12 microservices, 8,000 distributed transactions a second — is blunt about the history: they abandoned 2PC after it caused three outages in two months, moved to orchestration-based sagas, and now run at 99.7% saga completion, a 0.3% compensation rate, 1.2 s average duration, 4.8 s p99, and a 0.002% failed-compensation rate. Those are real fleet numbers, not a benchmark — and note what they imply: at 8,200 sagas a second, a 0.3% compensation rate is still ~25 compensations a second, every second, all day. Compensation is a hot path, not an exception handler. (Related: Latest Update: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

The curve is the argument for writing compensations first and testing them like forward steps. Every step you add is another chance to pay the undo tax: at 1% per-step failure, a 10-step saga compensates almost twice as often as a 5-step one (9.6% vs 4.9%). The practical consequences follow directly: keep sagas short, make the expensive steps late (so fewer steps need undoing when they fail), and put the step with no good compensation — "email the customer and apologize" — as far from the start as you can, because its undo is a human.

A saga's failure mode is not the failed step. It is the failed compensation. A step that fails cleanly is routine; a compensation that fails leaves the system in a state no protocol can resolve, and the saga lands in a dead-letter queue for a human.

Industry observers note that two things matter here. First, the unresolved rate is dominated by the late steps: a failure at step 5 undoes four steps, so it has four chances for a compensation to fail, while a failure at step 2 has only one. Second, the compensation-failure rate is the only number in the whole design that you fully control — and the cheapest way to move it is idempotency plus retry. A compensation that fails because the service was briefly down is not a human problem; it is a retry problem. Retry it with backoff, forever, and the only compensations that reach a human are the ones that fail deterministically: the refund API rejecting the charge, the inventory record already deleted, the seat already resold. That is why the field figure (0.002%) is two orders of magnitude below the toy 1% assumption: in production, most compensation failures are transient, and retries absorb them.

The non-obvious corollary: a compensation must be written for the world as it is when it runs, not as it was when the step ran. "Void the authorization" is correct on the day of the purchase and wrong the next day, when the card network requires a refund instead. "Increment the inventory count" is wrong if another reservation took the same units in the meantime — the compensation must release that specific reservation. These are semantic compensations, and they are where most saga bugs live: not in the happy path, and not in the retry logic, but in an undo that no longer matches the state it is undoing.

In a recent development, sagas have a second, quieter failure hiding inside every step: the step itself usually needs to do two things at once — enhancement its own database and tell the next service. Write the row, then publish the event, and a crash between the two loses the event: the order exists but nobody ever hears about it. Publish first, then write, and a crash leaves a ghost event for an order that never existed. Two independent writes with no shared transaction cannot both be guaranteed, and the gap between them is a loss window you can measure:.

In a recent development, the fix is to stop having two writes. The transactional outbox pattern, catalogued by Chris Richardson, folds the event into the step's own local transaction: the service inserts the business row and an outbox row in the same database transaction, and a separate relay publishes the outbox rows to the broker afterward. Either both rows exist or neither does — the database you already trust becomes the single source of truth, and everything after the commit is a retry loop.

According to recent reports, the guarantee that buys is at-least-once delivery, not exactly-once. The relay can publish an event, crash before recording its progress, and publish the same event again after restarting. There is no version of this pattern that delivers exactly once — KIP-939, which would let Kafka participate in two-phase commit, is still under development as of October 2026 — so the consumer side must do the second half of the job: deduplicate on the event ID (generated in application code, so it survives relay restarts) and make every handler idempotent. The outbox plus an idempotent consumer is the whole pattern; either half alone is a bug.

It has been revealed that the decision ladder from Part 3 still holds, with one rung added: if the data can live in one database, keep it there. If it is one logical database spread over nodes, use a distributed SQL database and let it run the commit protocol. If it is two different systems run by one team at low volume, XA/2PC is fine. If it is independent services, use a saga — and the moment a step has to notify another service, write the event into the outbox in the same transaction, or the saga's first failure will be a lost event nobody can explain.

The missing compensation is the failure that ships. Every step has a forward action that gets written, tested, and demoed. The compensation gets written last, tested never, and discovered missing the first time the step fails in production — which, at 4.9% of sagas, is the first week. The operational rule is to test compensations the way you test forward steps: run the saga, kill a step on purpose, and assert the system returns to the state it started in. Chaos testing for sagas is not a nice-to-have; it is the only way the undo path ever gets exercised before a customer does it for you.

The non-idempotent compensation is the failure that double-charges. At-least-once delivery means every compensation will eventually run twice. A refund that is not idempotent refunds twice; an inventory release that is not idempotent releases stock that was never reserved. The signal is a reconciliation report that never quite balances, and the fix is to key every compensation on the same event ID the forward step used, so the second run is a no-op. Idempotency is not a property of the saga framework. It is a property of each compensation, written by hand, one at a time.

It has been revealed that the unreadable in-between state is the failure your readers hit. A saga's intermediate states are visible: the payment is taken, the room is not yet booked, and a reader querying in that window sees a trip that is half-built. If your UI, your reports, or your downstream services assume a saga is either done or not started, they will show a customer a charge with no booking — and the customer will call support before the compensation runs. The fix is to design the read path for in-between states: mark the order PENDING, hide it from the customer-facing list until the saga completes, and treat "saga in progress" as a first-class status, not an error.

In a recent development, the unmonitored outbox is the failure that fills a disk. CDC's replication slot and the polling relay's backlog are both silent until they are not: slot lag grows, the outbox table grows, and the first symptom is either a full disk or events arriving hours late. Alert on outbox age — any row older than a minute is a stuck relay — and on slot lag, the same way Part 3 said to alert on in-doubt transactions. The outbox is infrastructure now; monitor it like infrastructure.
