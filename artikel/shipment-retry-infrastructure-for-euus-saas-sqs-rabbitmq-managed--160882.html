Sources indicate that the cheapest retry system is the one that can recover a shipment update without sending it twice. For a small property-management SaaS, I would start with a managed queue and a dead-letter queue, then add delayed retries with bounded backoff. Self-host RabbitMQ only when broker control is worth the operational hours; use a cloud-native queue when the rest of the application already lives there.

Short answer: choose managed delivery for the first version, make every fan-out operation idempotent, and let measured latency and operator time decide when to move to a self-managed broker. (Related: Show, Don't Certify: How to Prepare for Your First Data...)

The recommendation is deliberately boring. A property manager cares that a tenant sees “package delivered” once, not which exchange type moved the event. Revenue per engineering hour favors the design that a solo operator can explain during an incident.

Keep the retry schedule finite. A transient timeout can retry after 5 seconds, 30 seconds, and 5 minutes, with jitter. A permanent 4xx response should go directly to a review path. After the final attempt, the message belongs in a dead-letter queue (DLQ) with the original payload, attempt count, and last error category. Delayed retry is a scheduling tool, not a substitute for classifying failures. (Related: Latest Update: GTA 6 and Ocarina of Time remake caught ...)

In a recent development, the catch is retention. SQS, RabbitMQ, and managed messaging services expose different limits and semantics for visibility, retention, and redrive. Check the current service contract, then test it in the region where the workers run. I am not sure a generic “cheapest per message” comparison would survive your traffic pattern; batch size, cross-region transfer, and existing cloud commitments can dominate the invoice.

According to recent reports, for EU and US properties, I use a single canonical event and regional consumers. The producer signs the webhook body with HMAC before it enters the queue, and each consumer validates the signature and sequence number. RFC 2104 describes the keyed-hash construction; the key engineering detail is to compare the received MAC in constant time and to reject stale sequence numbers.

Here is the shape of a Node.js worker. The queue client is an interface so the same policy can sit over SQS, RabbitMQ, or another managed service. The example omits provider-specific calls on purpose; those calls belong in one adapter, while business rules stay testable.

The ledger and adapter need integration tests for duplicate delivery, worker termination after sendToChannel, and a poison message that reaches the DLQ. For one concrete drill, publish an enhancement for shipment EU-4821 with sequence 17 to the email queue, terminate the worker after the provider accepts the request but before complete(key) commits, then let visibility expire. The second worker should observe the same key, query the ledger or provider idempotency record, acknowledge the message, and leave the resident with one email. Repeat the drill in the US region with a deliberately delayed webhook, then inspect queue age and redrive counts. A controlled failure drill is more useful than a throughput chart: it proves the recovery path that a real operator must execute.

SQS removes broker patching and scales behind an AWS account, but its delivery model and visibility timeout still shape the worker. RabbitMQ gives direct control over exchanges, routing keys, consumer prefetch, and dead-letter exchanges; that control comes with responsibility for durable disks, upgrades, quorum behavior, and monitoring. Google Cloud Pub/Sub provides managed topics and subscriptions with cloud-native integration, while its retry and retention settings remain service-specific. None of these facts makes one universally cheapest.

The portability boundary matters. Keep queue-specific configuration in an adapter and store only provider-neutral fields in the job envelope. Then a migration changes connection code and deployment manifests, not the idempotency key or failure taxonomy. A self-hosted broker is unsuitable when nobody can own patching and capacity planning. A managed service is a poor fit when strict on-premise placement or custom routing semantics are non-negotiable. Stick with the cloud-native option when cross-service identity and observability already exist there.

I also separate “delay” from “calendar scheduling.” A queue delay handles minutes or hours of backoff. A shipment dispute that must reopen in 30 days belongs in a database or workflow scheduler that can emit a fresh queue job. Mixing those timelines creates expired messages and confusing DLQ reports.

Review one week of data: p50 and p95 delivery latency, percentage reaching the DLQ, operator minutes per redrive, and queue-related spend. Then adjust backoff, batch size, or the adapter. This is a weekly shipping loop, not a six-month platform project. Outsource the undifferentiated broker maintenance until your measurements show that its control is worth the time.
