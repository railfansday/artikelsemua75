Four babies who died in New South Wales – three after free births and one after a home birth – might have survived if they had had routine checks and follow-up , a coronial inquest has heard.

Baby DH, Baby D, Baby R, and Baby B died in the state’s northern rivers region between 2022 and 2024.

The inquest in Lismore has heard women chose free or home births for reasons including traumatic experiences, a lack of trust in clinicians, and a lack of services.

Recent findings suggest that baby DH died about eight hours after he was born in a free birth – with a doula but no medical professionals present. The court earlier heard he turned purple, was struggling to breathe and had became floppy.

His parents gave evidence they were worried but the doula kept reassuring them their baby was OK, then left.

The doula denied many of their claims, and denied that she gave them any clinical advice.

Expert neonatologist and associate professor Nicholas Evans mentioned on Friday that Baby DH’s complex heart abnormalities “would have been readily detectable” in a routine pregnancy scan after which he would have been referred to a paediatric cardiologist.

It was a “missed opportunity”, he told the court.

Paediatric cardiologist Dr Paul Brooks mentioned it was “highly likely” all the abnormalities would have been identified in a routine scan before birth.

Even if the extent of the abnormalities wasn’t clear, he told the court, the scan would have shown the baby needed to be born at a major hospital with cardiac surgery capabilities.

“The site of [Baby DH’s] birth meant that he didn’t have a chance.”.

Baby D’s mother did not know she was having twins, and at first thought she’d delivered Baby D’s placenta, only to realise it was a stillborn second twin.

Experts at the inquest agreed it was impossible to know exactly why Baby D died. They discussed the possibility it was because self-described birth keeper Sita Tara Kali did not clamp the umbilical cord before cutting it, but Evans told the court that it was ultimately most likely from respiratory distress because of his compromised heart function.

Baby D might have survived if someone who could do advanced resuscitation had arrived in under 10 minutes, he said, but the “root cause” was that it was not known the mother was carrying twins before the births. (Related: The Age of Machine Learning As Code Has Arrived...)

“If [twins] had been diagnosed, at 18 weeks, as it would usually in pregnancy, there would have been very close monitoring.”.

Recent findings suggest that baby R likely died of “severe post-maturity” after being born at 43 weeks and one day in a free birth. The standard is to induce birth or perform a caesarean section at 42 weeks, as the ageing placenta puts the baby at risk.

The mother had been for an ultrasound at 12 weeks, which had put the date of birth at 28 July, 2020.

But the mother distrusted hospitals and trusted Kali, her birth keeper, the court previously heard.

Kali told her to calculate her due date from the date of conception, an unreliable measure that put the date of birth in mid-August.

That recalculation, Evans wrote in his report to the court, was the “root cause” of the glitch.

“The standard practice now is that you don’t allow a pregnancy to go beyond 42 completed weeks,” Evans said on Friday. (Related: Few-shot learning in practice: GPT-Neo and the 🤗 Accele...)

Expert midwives earlier told the court they didn’t have statistics for babies born beyond 43 weeks because it was so rare.

A 19 August ultrasound highlighted the pregnancy had become a medical emergency – but Kali advised her to “go home and rest”. It is “more likely than not” that the baby would have survived had she gone to hospital after the ultrasound, Evans mentioned.

Baby B’s mother was warned multiple times she needed to go to hospital to give birth to her twins, but she was determined to have a home birth. Rather than let her have a free birth, four midwives assisted her and the head midwife repeatedly counselled her about the risks.

The mother declined ultrasounds as well as a test for early onset group B streptococcus (GBS) – an organism present in 15 to 20% of women that infects the babies of about 2% of carriers.

Baby B likely died of GBS, pneumonia and sepsis, Evans told the court on Friday, while his twin fell ill but survived. Many infected babies survive in hospital thanks to antibiotics, he added.
