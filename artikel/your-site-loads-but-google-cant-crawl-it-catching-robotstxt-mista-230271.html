Everything worked for human visitors, but the production robots.txt file, generated through an API during the rebuild, was restricting search engine crawlers. The fix itself took minutes. Finding the cause took longer, because nobody thought to look at a file that's easy to forget once a site is live.

This article walks through why this kind of problem happens, how to spot it, and how to add a few automated checks so it can't slip through on your next launch.

Recent findings suggest that during development, it's standard practice to keep a staging site out of search results. Teams often do this with a blanket rule in robots.txt:.

Those two lines tell every compliant crawler not to request any URL on the site. That's exactly what you want on a staging server and exactly what you don't want in production.

Industry observers note that the risk grows when robots.txt isn't a hand-edited file anymore. In a custom build, it might be generated by a framework, a build step, a CMS setting or, as in Usmani Academy's case, an API. If the generator's defaults or environment variables carry the staging configuration into production, the restrictive rules go live silently. Nothing looks broken, because browsers ignore robots.txt completely.

The distinction matters when you fix things. If a page is blocked in robots.txt, Google can't fetch it, which also means it can't see a noindex tag on it. So if your goal is to keep a page out of search results, use noindex and leave the page crawlable. If your goal is to get pages crawled again after a bad launch, check robots.txt first.
