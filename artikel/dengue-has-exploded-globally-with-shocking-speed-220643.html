The debilitating viral disease called dengue has exploded in incidence far more than previously thought, a new analysis suggests.

The number of reported global cases of dengue, which is transmitted by infected mosquitoes, rose from 430,000 in 1990 to 14.7 million in 2024 — a 34-fold increase, the analysis says.

It has been revealed that the data were presented on 22 November at the annual meeting of the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene (ASTMH) in National Harbor, Maryland. Scientists also provided some of the strongest results yet about a counter-intuitive way to curb the disease’s staggering spread: the release of specially modified mosquitoes. But researchers also presented discouraging data on other solutions, and scientists at the meeting emphasized that the campaign to beat back dengue will be complicated and costly. Sources indicate that “We’re in an extraordinary and pivotal moment” in the history of studying mosquito-borne illnesses, noted Albert Ko, president of ASTMH. Trial results presented at the meeting demonstrated, he said, that “we can get rigorous evidence on what works, what doesn’t and what needs to be done to scale these effective interventions to protect the 4 billion people” around the world who are exposed to dengue and other mosquito-borne illnesses. It has been revealed that dengue causes fever, headaches and severe bone pain. It is transmitted mainly by Aedes aegypti, a species of mosquito that thrives in warm, urban environments. Global warming and increased urbanization have expanded the mosquitoes’ range. Existing vaccines provide imperfect protection.

Cases of dengue are often recorded in disparate databases that aren’t easily comparable, notes Ahyoung Lim, an infectious-disease modeler at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, who was involved in the study on dengue incidence. The results have also been published as a preprint1 on Research Square ahead of peer review. This has meant that no single database has captured the total number of reported dengue cases over time, leading Lim and her team to compile the most complete record of reported cases and use statistical modelling tools to fill in gaps in which data were missing.

The staggering increase in reported cases isn’t surprising, claims Eng Eong Ooi, a dengue virologist at Duke–NUS Medical School in Singapore, who was not involved in the research. Populations in urban environments around the world are steadily increasing, he says, which means that “more and more people are living at risk of dengue each year”.

Industry observers note that one promising approach to curb dengue starts with breeding mosquitoes and deliberately infecting them with a bacterium called Wolbachia that reduces the insects’ ability to transmit viruses. When released into the wild, these infected mosquitoes pass the bacterium on to their offspring, eventually lowering the number of highly transmissive mosquitoes.

In a recent development, at the ASTMH meeting, another team of researchers shared results from the first randomized controlled trial in the Americas to test Wolbachia’s effect on dengue transmission. Between January 2021 and May 2022, the researchers released roughly 32 million Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes across designated regions of Brazil and monitored almost 6,000 children living inside and outside these regions. The children provided annual blood samples between 2020 and 2024, and the reseachers found that those in the Wolbachia regions were 68% less likely to be infected with dengue compared with those in non-Wolbachia regions.

These positive results mark an crucial milestone in proving Wolbachia’s potential to drastically reduce dengue cases, says Elisabeth Nelson, an epidemiologist at the Yale School of Public Health in Latest Haven, Connecticut, and a researcher on the trial.
