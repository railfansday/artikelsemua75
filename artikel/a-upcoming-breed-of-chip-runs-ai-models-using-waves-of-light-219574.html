A Upcoming Breed of Chip Runs AI Models Using Waves of Light

As artificial intelligence becomes an unavoidable part of everyday life and models become ever more sophisticated, the cost of the energy required to run the technology is ballooning.

To rein in runaway costs, AI labs and cloud providers are trying to build newly introduced data centers in places with cheap and plentiful renewable energy—like Scandinavia. An expanding field of hardware startups, among them German tech giant Q.ANT, is coming at the problem from a different direction: by developing a fresh breed of chip—photonic processors—meant to minimize the amount of energy that AI models consume. According to recent reports, whereas regular GPUs transmit and process data by shuffling electrons around, photonic processors use waves of light. Because light doesn’t generate heat as it travels and allows more data to be processed simultaneously, photonic processors supposedly consume less energy and require minimal cooling—one of the largest sources of cost for data centers.

According to recent reports, the aim isn’t to replace GPUs with photonic processors, but to run them side-by-side. Whilst GPUs excel at grinding through masses of additions and multiplications—linear math—photonic processors are best suited to complex, nonlinear calculations.

Few photonic processors are running inside data centers today. But Q.ANT recently rolled out its chips to facilities operated by cloud company IONOS as part of a pilot program. It claims that more than 30 teams of engineers are now developing AI models optimized for photonic computing.

Recent findings suggest that earlier this month, WIRED spoke with Michael Förtsch, founder and CEO of Q.ANT. We discussed the energy bottlenecks constraining AI development, how photonic processors differ from regular chips, and the challenges Q.ANT faces in trying to popularize a latest class of hardware.

This conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

WIRED: Michael, could you start by outlining the problem Q.ANT is aiming to solve? Why does the world need photonic processors to run AI models?

Michael Förtsch: The overall problem is the cost generated by the huge amount of energy consumption.

If you compute the way we think light should be used—not as a direct replacement for a GPU, but as a coprocessor—you basically have a new processor that takes care of the heavy math.

Sources indicate that the cost driver today is the access to the memory. The less data you need in order to complete a job, the more efficient and better your system. We execute complicated math in a single step, where [regular chips] need thousands of steps. This saves a bunch of data—or memory access points.

How would you explain to the layperson how photonic computing works? How can light be used to perform mathematical computations?

Industry observers note that light is a wave. What is a wave? A sine function. [The sine function is a mathematical rule that, when plotted to a graph, produces a smooth repeating wave. —Editor] If I want to model something that’s round in shape with only linear functions, I need an infinite amount of data points and lines in order to describe something very simple: a circle. If I have the sine function at hand, because light is a wave, I need two points and I have already described the circle in all its beauty.

Light is very, very gifted to operate on complicated equations.

What distinguishes the type of problem best left to regular chips from problems that should be delegated to photonic processors?

The Nvidia H200 GPU is a masterpiece of technology. It’s art. It’s truly hard to attack it in its homeground. But light has strengths where the GPU is weak—and vice versa.

According to recent reports, linear math? Please route it to the GPU. Non-linear and complex stuff? Please route it to us.

Recent findings suggest that we believe we will be much stronger in everything related to pictures and videos: image recognition, segmentation, and generation. Then, everything that’s related to robotics and physical AI.

What is the estimated gain in energy efficiency tied to using photonic processors alongside GPUs for AI tasks?

According to recent reports, I’m not saying photonic computing will come for free. But I see that photonic computing, in combination with [CPUs and GPUs], can bring you a lot more artificial intelligence at a fraction of the energy.

Under simulation, we are ending up at around four or five times energy reduction. But in real hardware we are not yet demonstrating it.

Can any task run on photonic hardware by default, or do they need to be configured in a certain way?

Recent findings suggest that there’s no need to learn a different language than C++ or Python. And you can already execute [preexisting] code on our machines.

But if you take something that has been optimized for a GPU only, you should not expect that automatically things will run faster. You have to do some adaptations.

It’s a chicken and egg problem, right? Until developers are building models to run on this type of processor, it’s going to be difficult to sell them to cloud providers. Equally, until photonic processors are available for people to use, it’s going to be tough to convince developers to design for them.

Whether it’s photonic or any other processor technology, it will need time to get the credibility and trust that’s needed for a firm like Google to build data center infrastructure around it.

That’s the reason we chose to go into data centers with proof of concept systems, to validate that photonic computing is robust enough to withstand that environment.

We want to invite the world to compute on our systems. We are building a upcoming chicken, so you can start producing eggs. Let’s find out together whether they’re tasty or not.

What else should I understand about why photonic processors are not already widely in use?

If hypothetically we received an order of 100,000 [photonic processors], we at Q.ANT are ready, but I’m not sure about the suppliers.

Hardware is not software. On the assembly side, it will need [time] before we have the same robust supply chain as the semiconductor world.

Do you expect to suffer from the same problem as other hardware challengers in securing capacity with the significant chip manufacturers?

[To produce Q.ANT chips] you only need 90 nanometer node technology, from the 2000s. A lot of foundries already use this capability to produce chips that are super cheap. With this technology, you can use the same line to produce high-performance chips with a much higher street price, without massive upfront investment in your assets.

That resonates well with the industry.

Are Q.ANT processors built to function alongside a particular brand of GPUs—say, Nvidia or AMD—or are they totally agnostic?

Sources indicate that there’s no need to go single-sided with a particular GPU. We want to have the freedom to cooperate with any of them.

Either way, we are not competitive to GPUs, we complete their function set.

Can you imagine a future in which AI models run entirely on photonic processors, or will they always be a sidekick?

If we see an opportunity in five years to attack the GPU as well, we would not be shy about taking it. But at the same time, I do not know what workloads in five years are going to be like.

It depends on the architecture of future AI models, you mean?

Could the chance to perform certain calculations more cheaply with photonic processors change the way that AI engineers design future models?

The core message is that we need to do something to fight the energy cost in the AI stack.

That’s where we see the future of AI: very data-optimized models that allow for tasks of the same quality, but at a fraction of the energy—and therefore a fraction of the operating cost.
