Science advocacy organizations and members of Congress are now rushing to address scientists’ most pressing question: how much money will be allocated to research in the spending bill for the fiscal year 2027?

According to recent reports, when it convenes on 3 January, the 120th Congress should also address two thornier, longer-term questions: who should get to participate in making decisions about science and technology? And how can policy institutions adapt to include more public voices?

According to recent reports, these questions sit at the centre of science’s ‘social contract’, the government-led support system that exchanges taxpayer funding and scientific autonomy for the promise of downstream public benefit. And they speak to a much older problem than the Second World War-era debates that created the modern US research system: the challenges of how to govern and the role of experts in democracies. (Related: Scaling up BERT-like model Inference on modern CPU - P...)

In the main, postwar US science policy has privileged experts over the public. In the 80 years since the publication of science adviser Vannevar Bush’s report Science, The Endless Frontier, the government has tested policy mechanisms that more closely align science with the nation’s social and economic needs. But public engagement remains in the wings.

Entire NSF science advisory board fired by Trump administration.

Entire NSF science advisory board fired by Trump administration.

It has been revealed that the National Science Foundation (NSF), a major funder of basic science at universities, has grown to embody the US social contract. And it has become a battleground for the competing visions of academia and the White House over federal science policy.

With no Senate-confirmed director and its acting director due to leave at the end of the year, the NSF faces a widening leadership gap. The American Institute of Physics and others have reported in detail on the firing, in April, of the National Science Board, which helps to oversee the NSF. Agency observers say that the absence of a Senate-confirmed director has made the NSF subject to greater White House direction.

The agency awarded nearly 50% fewer grants in the 2026 fiscal year than in the years from 2021 to 2024. The NSF has remarked that it has carried over more than $1 billion into the 2027 fiscal year to invest in agency priorities aligned with broader initiatives of the administration of President Donald Trump. It has two years to spend its research appropriations, although it does not typically carry over such a large share of its annual budget.

Over the past two years, the Trump administration has overhauled the nation’s long-standing social contract with science and, with it, the trusted partnership between universities and the federal government. The resulting impact on US science and increasing public attention on science policy has opened a unique window for reform. Congress should seize this opportunity to reimagine the nation’s science-policy institutions, rather than just trying to stitch them back together.
