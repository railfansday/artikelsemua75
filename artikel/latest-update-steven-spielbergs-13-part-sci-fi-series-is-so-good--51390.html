📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Few things are as creepy as children in horror films and TV series. What's scarier? Children who are being manipulated by something — or someone — unknown, that is. It's doubtful that The Exorcist would have been as effective had Regan (Linda Blair) been an adult, or Sinister as seriously messed up as it is if Bughuul did his dirty work through senior citizens. This brings us to The Whispers, a forgotten, 13-part sci-fi horror series that premiered on ABC in 2015 from executive producer Steven Spielberg, where children communicate with an entity known only as Drill, who promises them rewards if they play his "games," resulting in a series of incidents that seemingly have no connection… until they do. Eleven years later, its creepy premise still makes an ordinary childhood friendship feel like a threat.

According to recent reports, the Whispers opens with a playful scene, as children in a yard run through sprinklers, blow bubbles, and do what kids do. That is, except Harper (Abby Ryder Fortson), who's off to the side, talking to someone we can only assume is an imaginary friend she's named Drill. Her mother, Amanda (Autumn Reeser), calls her in, much to Harper's chagrin. She hasn't learned all the rules for the game Drill wants her to play yet.

What the game is, no one knows, but it involves Harper collecting tools and going up into the treehouse, where she's not supposed to be alone. Amanda storms out, but Harper won't come down, claiming that it's part of the game, and Drill says that Amanda is now "it." Amanda climbs up, walks to an "X" drawn on the floor, and promptly crashes through, hitting the ground, seriously injuring her and sending her into a coma.

It's the opening salvo in a battle against an unknown entity, one that may be supernatural or even alien in nature, and one that picks and chooses the children it "whispers" to further its own agenda. It's up to Special Agent Claire Bennigan (Lily Rabe), an FBI child specialist, and Wes Lawrence (Barry Sloane), a high-ranking Defense Department operative, to find out who or what Drill is, why he's targeting children for his games, and what his endgame could be.

Sources indicate that it takes on an increased urgency for each of them, given they all have their own children. Additionally, Claire's husband, U.S. Air Force pilot Captain Sean Bennigan (Milo Ventimiglia), who was thought to have died in a plane crash, is part of Drill's game. And Drill's always one step ahead.

The Whispers seamlessly blends sci-fi and horror with an unsettling eeriness, deepening and building the mystery with an underlying, ever-increasing anxiety. (And yes, it's thanks to those creepy kids.) Each of the young actors in primary roles — Fortson, Kyle Harrison Breitkopf, Kylie Rogers, and Kayden Magnuson — is brilliant, playing innocents unaware (maybe) of the consequences of playing Drill's games (Magnuson is particularly chilling in the finale). The adult cast, too, is strong. Ventimiglia is the strongest of the adult cast, playing what is essentially two different characters in the first and second half of the season. The Whispers also bears an undeniable mark of its executive producer: the exploration of the adult/child dynamic. The children listen to Drill and are willing to do his bidding because he listens to them, and the adults in their lives don't. From Hook to The Fabelmans to E.T.: The Extra-Terrestrial, there's a repeated theme of the disconnect between adults and children in Spielberg's works. Adults are absent, oblivious, and even antagonistic, and that's no different in The Whispers. The children are told that they're not allowed to talk to their imaginary friend. They leave their homes — despite so-called adult supervision — to carry out Drill's instructions in perilous ways.

Industry observers note that while most Spielberg fare has adults learning to pay more attention to their children, The Whispers ends on a life lesson, faced with the consequences of adults not paying attention until it's too late. It begs the question: who is the real danger, Drill or the parents? It's actually Drill who makes the argument for the latter, saying: "If parents paid more attention to them, I wouldn't have been able to [corrupt them]. But they're always alone, watching TV, playing video games. So, who's really corrupting the children, Claire?". (Related: Industry News: The Reformer - Pushing the limits of lan...)

Industry observers note that but it's a question that would go unanswered, along with a host of others, with ABC pulling the plug on The Whispers after one season that ends on a major, unresolved cliffhanger. Don't let that stop you: The Whispers is the perfect weekend binge that keeps the viewer engaged from start to finish, and is a thrilling and eerie ride worth taking. Unless your child starts talking to an imaginary friend.
