Home design’s future depends on how homes support everyday life

🌐 Source: Qz • 📅 24 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (1014 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • The 2026 residential design conversation is placing considerable attention on tactile materials, minimalist interiors, natural stone, and visually distinctive finishes.
  • Industry observers note that that question extends into the growing market for smart-home technology.
  • Those considerations become particularly relevant against America’s demographic and housing challenges.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The 2026 residential design conversation is placing considerable attention on tactile materials, minimalist interiors, natural stone, and visually distinctive finishes. According to Houzz Research’s 2026 U.S. Houzz Emerging Summer Trends Report, searches for sandstone increased by 257%, while interest in Venetian plaster rose by 94%, reflecting growing interest in sensory-rich interiors. These preferences offer insight into evolving tastes, although they also raise a broader question about how aesthetic priorities relate to everyday usability. As homes receive growing focus for their visual presentation, practical considerations such as accessibility, ergonomics, and long-term functionality may deserve an equally prominent place in the design process.

Industry observers note that that question extends into the growing market for smart-home technology. Voice assistants, automated lighting, connected appliances, and app-controlled systems are becoming familiar elements of residential innovation. Pew Research Center’s 2026 report found that 35% of U.S. adults have a smart speaker, while 18% have an AI-enabled smart doorbell. These figures illustrate consumer exposure to connected technology, while also inviting consideration of what innovation means beyond digital convenience. Technology may contribute more meaningfully to residential wellness when it also addresses everyday needs, including water quality, hygiene, accessibility, and household efficiency. Those considerations become particularly relevant against America’s demographic and housing challenges. The AARP 2025–2026 Policy Book estimates that the population aged 65 and older could increase from 57 million in 2022 to 82 million by 2050. Meanwhile, Realtor.com’s March 2026 Housing Supply Gap $GPS Report estimates that the national housing shortfall reached 4.03 million homes in 2025. Together, these developments raise questions about the suitability of existing housing and the priorities guiding fresh construction. Bathrooms with difficult thresholds, inaccessible fixtures, and limited mobility accommodations may create additional challenges for older residents. Designing for different stages of life, from infancy through advanced age, could become an increasingly important consideration.

For Steve Scheer, president of Brondell, a home wellness firm developing products for water filtration, hygiene, bathing, and indoor air quality, the relationship between these challenges points toward a broader discussion about residential planning. “Housing innovation involves more than the introduction of connected devices or visually distinctive interiors,” he says. “It also encompasses the physical environments and everyday products people depend on.” Brondell’s product categories provide examples of how practical functionality, accessibility, and resource awareness can inform decisions about residential living.

According to Scheer, the discussion begins with understanding how people interact with their homes throughout the day. Products used for drinking, bathing, washing, and personal hygiene can influence everyday routines, making their design relevant to broader conversations about preventive living. “Consumers want solutions that fit naturally into their lives,” Scheer claims. That perspective raises an crucial consideration for manufacturers. Usability can depend on details such as the placement of controls, the accessibility of fixtures, and the physical effort required to operate everyday products.

It has been revealed that ergonomics consequently becomes an vital consideration in product development, particularly when everyday fixtures need to accommodate people with different physical abilities. Brondell’s bidets and toilet seats, for example, incorporate side controls and remote operation that may help individuals with limited mobility maintain greater independence in the bathroom. Similar principles inform its kitchen faucets and shower products, where reach, control placement, and ease of operation influence everyday usability.

In a recent development, brian Inami, Brondell’s Vice President of Product Development, emphasizes the importance of translating human needs into practical engineering decisions. “Smart product design begins with understanding how people will actually use something in their daily lives. Every detail, from the placement of a control to the way a fixture operates, can influence accessibility, comfort, and the overall experience,” Inami indicates.

Consumer preferences also raise questions about how functionality and appearance influence purchasing decisions. Brondell’s reverse-osmosis filtration portfolio offers an example. “We have two reverse-osmosis systems that reflect different design priorities. One places greater emphasis on filtration capabilities, while the other gives more attention to aesthetics and integration into the kitchen environment,” Scheer says. “It’s interesting to see how consumers respond to these different features, particularly how visual design influences their preferences.”.

This instance highlights the importance of communicating technical specifications in accessible terms. Scheer emphasizes that helping consumers understand filtration performance may allow them to make more informed decisions about their household needs.

According to recent reports, these considerations also connect to sustainability. Bidets may reduce reliance on toilet paper through water-based hygiene, while household filtration systems can offer an alternative to single-use plastic water bottles. Both examples demonstrate how everyday products can contribute to personal wellness and resource conservation. For Scheer, sustainability forms part of a broader understanding of healthier living, where individual well-being and environmental responsibility can inform the same product decisions.

Beyond individual products, these principles extend to residential development. America’s housing shortage creates an opportunity to reconsider how newly introduced homes and apartment buildings accommodate the people living in them. Alongside housing availability, developers may wish to consider the role of communal gardens, shared indoor facilities, and accessible outdoor spaces. These environments can encourage interaction, help residents build relationships, and contribute to a greater sense of belonging. Residential planning consequently has a social dimension, with community spaces influencing the experience of living in a development.

According to recent reports, scheer identifies three priorities for future home innovation: intentional design, sustainability, and greater consideration of an aging population. “Manufacturers can incorporate ergonomics and impactful design during product development, while architects and developers can account for accessibility and communal experiences during initial planning,” he states. “Designing for people across different life stages, from young children to older adults, can also help create homes that accommodate changing needs.”.

Essentially, residential innovation may increasingly be evaluated through its contribution to everyday life. Technology and aesthetics will continue influencing design preferences, while accessibility, practical performance, and environmental responsibility may become equally notable considerations.

As demographic changes and housing pressures influence residential planning, the opportunity lies in creating environments that support independence, encourage responsible resource use, accommodate different abilities, and foster human connection. These considerations may help define a broader vision of what homes and communities can contribute throughout people's lives.
