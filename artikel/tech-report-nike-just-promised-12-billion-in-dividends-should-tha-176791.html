Nike (NKE +5.08%) began its investor day on Nov. 17 by pledging to spend $12 billion on dividends from fiscal 2027 through fiscal 2031. On the surface, such a commitment might reassure investors who are losing confidence in the stock. (Related: Latest Update: Dividends Aren't What You Think The...)

Recent findings suggest that unfortunately, when it became apparent that this was merely the fiscal 2026 dividend commitment being continued for another five years, investors soured on the stock again. Moreover, they have good reason to question whether the company can meet the $12 billion commitment, and here's why.

Nike unveiled its 24th consecutive payout hike last November. If it unveils another one this month, it will mark 25 years of annual dividend increases. (Related: Latest Update: Towers of Fortune codes (November 2026)...)

Furthermore, the stock is down by 80% from its all-time high in 2021, and what that does to the yield has grabbed investors' attention. With the current yearly payout of $1.64 per share, that lower stock price has raised the yield to about 4.7%.

NYSE · As of 11:38 AM ET 11/24/26.

However, the dividend cost shows why the $12 billion pledge might bode poorly for the stock. Nike spent just over $2.4 billion annually in payouts in fiscal 2026. Since that amounts to $12 billion over five years, it suggests the dividend increases may stop, which may give investors another reason to sell the stock.

Recent findings suggest that and they may question if it can even afford $12 billion. In the first quarter of fiscal 2027 (ended Aug. 31), Nike reported a free cash outflow of $64 million. Also, in fiscal 2026, the free cash flow of $2.2 billion was just under the company's dividend cost.

According to recent reports, furthermore, the prospects for a recovery continue to appear dim. Analysts forecast an 8% revenue decline in fiscal 2027 and a 1% drop the next year, implying Nike will have to dip further into its $8.4 billion in liquidity to fund the payout.

In a recent development, to management's credit, it has worked to reverse its unfortunate decision to rely purely on online sales. Unfortunately, it has struggled to regain the shelf space it surrendered to competitors.

And it continues to rely on retro brands such as Air Jordan, just as aggressive competitors such as Deckers Outdoors HOKA, and footwear designed by On Holdings have offered innovative products and won over customers who might have chosen Nike in the past. Thus, unless Nike can win back some of the business it lost, its dividend remains in danger.

Industry observers note that considering the current state of the dividend, investors should assume little in the way of payout hikes. Nike's $12 billion pledge amounts to matching its fiscal 2026 dividend outlay for five more years. That suggests it will either stop increasing its dividend or, at best, the payout hikes will be very limited.

In a recent development, moreover, this comes at a time when free cash flows have fallen below the cost of the payout. With revenue on track to fall for at least the next two years, the manufacturer lacks an obvious path to reversing this trend.

According to recent reports, the one bit of good news for shareholders is that its $8.4 billion in liquidity gives it time to rebuild its competitive advantage. Nonetheless, unless Nike can regain its edge, the apparel stock's dividend will continue to be in trouble.
