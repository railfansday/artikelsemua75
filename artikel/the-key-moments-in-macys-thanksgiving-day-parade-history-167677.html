Sources indicate that in 1928, the parade's balloons got an upgrade: helium. And once the march was over, organizers did something that sounds wild today. They let the balloons go.

Helium made the whole thing work. It's the second-lightest element after hydrogen and much lighter than air, so a helium balloon rises on its own. That's why the parade needs handlers on the ground holding the lines, and why the characters float high above the crowd instead of bobbing along at street level.

According to recent reports, hydrogen could have done the job, too, but it's highly flammable. Helium doesn't burn, which makes it the far safer bet for a giant balloon moving through packed city streets. The danger of hydrogen became painfully clear in 1937, when the hydrogen-filled Hindenburg airship burst into flames while landing in Lakehurst, Latest Jersey.

For three Thanksgivings, from 1942 to 1944, there was no Macy's $M parade. World War II had created shortages of the two things the giant balloons couldn't do without: rubber and helium.

Those are the only years the parade has ever been called off since it started in 1924. It even kept going through the worst of the Great Depression.

Rubber was the first problem. After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on Dec. 7, 1941, and pulled the U.S. into the war, Japanese forces quickly took over much of Southeast Asia, including Malaya and the Dutch East Indies. That's where most of America's natural rubber came from.

Washington scrambled to hold on to what was left. Tire rationing started in early 1942, and the War Production Board funneled rubber to the military for trucks, planes, jeeps, boots and gas masks. That summer, President Franklin D. Roosevelt asked Americans to turn in their old rubber in a national scrap drive.

According to recent reports, helium was the second problem. At the time, the U.S. produced nearly all of the world's supply, pulled from natural gas fields in the Great Plains. The Navy needed it for blimps that hunted enemy submarines off the coast and escorted convoys.

According to recent reports, with the military claiming both, there was no way to justify a parade. Macy's went a step further than canceling: It deflated its balloons and donated the rubber to the war effort.

The balloons didn't fly over Manhattan again until 1945. Those missing years are also why the parade's numbering lags behind the calendar, and why this year's parade, 102 years after the first one, is the 100th.

In a recent development, the 1945 parade had another first: It was the first one held on a Thanksgiving date set by federal law. In 1939, President Franklin D. Roosevelt had moved the holiday up a week to give stores a longer Christmas shopping season, a push that came straight from retailers. Not every state went along, so for a few years the country had two Thanksgivings, and critics took to calling Roosevelt's version "Franksgiving.".

Congress settled it in December 1941, fixing Thanksgiving on the fourth Thursday of November starting in 1942. With no parades from 1942 through 1944, the 1945 march was the first to run under the latest rule, and it's set the date ever since.

By 1997, Macy's $M had been flying giant character balloons for 70 years, and the system hadn't changed much: Each one is steered by a team of handlers on foot, holding ropes and adjusting the balloon's height and direction as they walk.

That works fine in calm air. But a giant balloon catches wind like a sail, and a sudden gust can shove it sideways or drag it down faster than anyone on the ground can react. Handlers can pull their lines in, but a strong enough gust will still push a balloon off course.

Manhattan makes that especially dangerous. The route is lined with streetlights, traffic signals and trees, and spectators stand just a few feet from where the balloons pass overhead. A balloon blown off its path doesn't have anywhere safe to go.

The accident pushed New York City officials to rethink when the balloons should be allowed to fly at all.

On parade morning, the NYPD and Macy's keep a close eye on conditions. There are weather monitoring stations along the route, and each giant balloon's assistant pilot carries a wind meter that an NYPD representative also tracks. The final call on the balloons comes just before the parade starts, based on real-time data from along the route.

Grounding isn't the only option. When gusts pick up, handlers can fly a balloon lower to keep it in the parade rather than pulling it entirely. Macy's trains its volunteer handlers ahead of time for exactly that.

The limits apply only to the balloons. Floats, bands, performers and Santa's sleigh roll on regardless.

For spectators, that means the balloon lineup is never quite set in stone. Whether your favorite character shows up depends on the forecast as much as on Macy's intends.
