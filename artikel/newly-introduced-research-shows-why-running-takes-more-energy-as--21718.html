Running can get harder as you age. Normal wear and tear takes a toll, and your body needs more energy to cover the same distance.

Sources indicate that “The aging process leads to decreased strength and volume of muscles, including the calf muscles involved in plantarflexion,” Changal T. Nguyen, MD, a sports medicine physiatrist, told Outside.

Dr. Nguyen is also a clinical assistant professor of orthopedic surgery at Stanford and was not involved in the study. “The calf muscles and Achilles tendon also become less stiff with age, so they work less effectively as a spring, she mentioned.

Recent findings suggest that the good news, Dr. Nguyen said, is that adding a few simple exercises to your routine can help keep your calves strong and your Achilles tendon functioning well as you age.

The authors of the fresh study recruited 18 runners with an average age of 26.1 years and 18 runners with an average age of 63.4 years. The study referred to each group as “young athletes” and “master athletes,” respectively. (Related: Industry News: Average Retirement Savings by Age: Are Y...)

The researchers measured the athletes’ Achilles tendon stiffness and plantar flexor movements. (The plantar flexor muscles—located in the feet, ankles, and calves—propel you forward and upward as you run.) Then, both groups completed two six-minute treadmill sessions at two different speeds—roughly 5.6 miles per hour (mph) and 6.7 mph.

Sources indicate that they found that master athletes’ hips worked harder than those of younger athletes. Their ankles, on the other hand, did less work compared to those of younger athletes. Master athletes also exhibited weaker plantar flexors.

Some of these findings could be explained by decreased calf and Achilles stiffness, Dr. Nguyen remarked: “When the calf can no longer generate as much power at push-off, the body compensates by relying more on other muscles, such as those around the hip.”.

The researchers found that master athletes also spent more energy running, and their energy cost increased with speed.

“One notable finding is that the gap in energy cost widened at the faster speed, from about five percent to about 13 percent,” Dr. Nguyen stated. “This hints that the age-related deficit may grow as running demands increase, although the study tested only two moderate speeds, so whether the gap keeps widening at tempo or race pace is unknown.”.

It has been revealed that there are a couple of limitations worth noting. The study had a small sample size, and the runners were tested at only two speeds on a treadmill, Dr. Nguyen said. Because of these limitations, it’s uncertain whether the findings would hold in different settings—for instance, on a trail—or at different speeds.

Strengthening your calf muscles and improving Achilles tendon function promotes gait efficiency (the amount of energy needed to walk or run; a high gait efficiency means less energy is needed to complete the motions involved in walking or running), which can help you keep running as you age, Dr. Nguyen noted.
