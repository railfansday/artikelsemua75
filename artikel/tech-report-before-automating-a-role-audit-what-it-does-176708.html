Dan Haiem is the founder and CEO of AppMakers USA, helping business leaders design, build and scale apps that deliver real-world impact.

Every time someone claims, “We can automate that,” I think of “the doorman fallacy” that marketer Rory Sutherland shared in his book Alchemy. (Related: Latest Update: Dividends Aren't What You Think The...)

It goes like this: A manager looks at the doorman and sees one job: opening the door. An automatic door does that for less money, so the doorman is let go. Then the hotel slowly learns the other tasks the doorman did: greeting guests, noticing who walked in and finding a taxi when it rained. (Related: Compound Interest Calculator: See Your Money Grow...)

In May, AI was cited as the leading cause of U.S. job cuts for the third month in a row, with 38,579 cuts, according to Challenger, Gray & Christmas. I’m not here to debate those decisions, but it’s important that someone at least understands what a role actually does before deciding which ones are necessary.

According to recent reports, in evaluating roles, many leaders only look at their most visible task. A technical support answers tickets, a QA finds bugs and a project manager sends updates.

Recent findings suggest that those verbs are often easy to automate, but they also miss the judgment, relationships, small catches and in-betweens. In my experience, a good QA engineer knows which bug a client will actually notice. A good support rep knows which customer is about to leave.​.

Sources indicate that trust me, I’m not against automation. My team automates plenty, but I’ve learned that the most vital question is: Which part of a role is the door, and which part is the doorman?

We’ve automated a few things on my team, and client onboarding is where we learned this lesson the hard way. In our onboarding process, a prospect would fill out the contact form on our site and get an email with an NDA and a link to a questionnaire. Once they submitted it, an automation tool read their answers and decided whether we were a fit.

According to recent reports, it looked efficient but we had a hard time closing clients. It took us a while to connect that to the form. You see, people often fill out a questionnaire just to finish it. I know because I do the same sometimes, especially when I’m busy. You pick whatever gets you to the next screen.

In a recent development, A real project, though, with its half-formed ideas and open questions, rarely fits in a dropdown. Short answers and ticked boxes told us very little about what they actually needed. I now believe that was one of the reasons. We had automated a judgment call, not a door.

Now, we get on a call first and have a real conversation, with follow-up questions about what they’re trying to solve. A form can only ask the questions you thought of in advance. A conversation can follow the answer that surprises you.

According to recent reports, only once we agree it’s a good fit does the NDA, contract and other paperwork run on automation. Based on what I’ve seen, prospects appreciate that conversation more than any form.

Industry observers note that if you’re under pressure to show AI savings, here’s what I’d do before touching a role. Write down everything the role does, including things that go beyond its stated description. Ask the person doing it, because they know what they catch.

Industry observers note that based on those insights, sort each item as mechanical, judgment or relationship. Mechanical work is repeatable and rule-based. Judgment is deciding when the rules don’t fit. Relationship is anywhere the other person cares that you’re human.

Automate the mechanical bucket first, and only that one. In our case, the paperwork that follows a yes was the right thing to automate. Our onboarding form went wrong because we let a tool make a judgment call that looked like a form field.

According to recent reports, if a step needs follow-up questions to get to the truth, it belongs in the judgment bucket. In that case, keep at least one human touchpoint to build that trust. For us, that’s the discovery call.

In my experience, none of this takes long. It’s an afternoon with the person who does the job and a whiteboard.​.

AI is going to automate a lot of doors, and plenty of them deserve it. Before you do, find out who’s standing there and what they actually do all day. Creating savings on the spreadsheet is easy. The value you can’t see is the part you only notice after it’s gone.​​.
