Industry observers note that openAI just did something no frontier lab had done before: it published the pretraining loss of every model it has trained since GPT-3.5 — thirteen models, measured on one fixed held-out dataset drawn from its own codebase. And the curve it released does not look like the slow, grinding progress scaling laws promised. It looks like a bend in the road.

METR ran the numbers, and the result is startling.

From 2022 through early 2026, OpenAI's base-model log-perplexity fell slowly, about 0.022 per year. Then, at a breakpoint in February 2026, the slope changed. Since then it has been falling at about 0.156 per year — 7.1 times steeper. (Related: How to train a latest language model from scratch using...)

Put differently: at the current pace, OpenAI would cover the entire log-perplexity drop from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4.1 — a journey that took three years — in about six and a half months. (Related: My Journey to a serverless transformers pipeline on Goo...)

Recent findings suggest that that is the opposite of what the textbooks predict. Text can never be perfectly predicted, so loss has a floor it cannot cross. Under standard scaling laws, each tenfold increase in compute buys a smaller drop in loss than the last. Progress in loss was supposed to get harder over time, not easier. The 2026 data points fall below every curve the old model would have drawn.

Sources indicate that here is where it gets genuinely interesting. If models are getting dramatically better at predicting text, you would expect their benchmark scores to jump too. They haven't — at least not at anything like the same rate. METR points to Epoch AI's ECI, a broad capability index, which highlights no comparable acceleration in OpenAI's models.

So we have a genuine fork in the road, and METR lays out the three ways to read it:.

In a recent development, 1. The data overstates the progress. A single held-out dataset, drawn from OpenAI's own codebase, is a narrow window. Recent training recipes may help disproportionately on code-like text. Small parts of the codebase may have leaked into training data. Overfitting, in one form or another, could be inflating the apparent gain.

Industry observers note that 2. Benchmarks are the real measure, and perplexity is just an input. On this reading, the acceleration in loss barely changes our forecasts of real-world capability. Models are better at predicting tokens; that doesn't automatically mean they are better at doing useful work. But even this reading has a sting in its tail: if AI itself contributed to the acceleration, it previews AI's coming contribution to every part of the training pipeline.

According to recent reports, 3. Perplexity is the deeper measure, and the benchmarks are lagging. Many researchers treat pretraining perplexity as a more fundamental capability signal than any benchmark suite. On this reading, the rapid acceleration in pretraining will be followed — with a lag — by an acceleration in capabilities measured by real-world impact.

METR is explicit that its own staff disagree. There is no consensus view. But it is equally explicit about the stakes: some interpretations imply we should expect a broad-based acceleration in real-world AI capabilities over the next six months.

It has been revealed that the loss curve did not arrive alone. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published it in an essay, "Perplexity and Intelligence," and used it to propose something unusual: that frontier labs adopt perplexity as a shared yardstick and agree on common development limits they would not cross until the ability to understand and monitor AI behavior catches up.

The next window, around 0.68–0.70, is where Pachocki expects further transformative capabilities — possibly ones nobody presently tests for. His proposal is not a treaty and names no enforcement mechanism; it is an invitation to set shared evaluation norms, red-team safety cases before first use, and fix a first limit measured by a metric tracked across labs.

Microsoft has already joined the transparency push: with OpenAI's permission, it disclosed MAI-2 Base at 0.819 log-perplexity on OpenAI's evaluation dataset — close to GPT-5.6 Sol on that measure, because both models share a tokenizer.

If reading 2 is right, not much changes: keep shipping, keep benchmarking, and treat the loss curve as a curiosity. If reading 3 is right, the next six months could bring a capability jump that benchmarks haven't priced in — and the models you build on today may be replaced faster than your roadmap assumes.

The honest position, and METR's, is that we don't yet know which reading is right. But we now have, for the first time, a public number to argue about instead of vibes. That alone is progress — and it is why METR is urging every frontier developer to publish the same curve.

According to recent reports, the loss is falling seven times faster than it used to. The question is whether capability is about to follow.
