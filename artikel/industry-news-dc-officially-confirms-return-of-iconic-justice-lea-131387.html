Sources indicate that A major Justice League icon returns with a villainous twist that may soon become a huge threat to DC's top superheroes. Several notorious DC villains have genuinely turned their lives around to earn a coveted seat inside the Justice League. For instance, Plastic Man left his origins as a small-time safecracker to become one of a Justice League mainstay, while Catwoman fought alongside the Justice League of America during the Fresh 52 era and Killer Frost redeemed herself as a core member of Batman’s JLA squad. Most recently, the Justice League's villain amnesty blueprint has given foes like Lex Luthor, Starro, and Major Force a second chance.

Sources indicate that on the other side of the spectrum, even the purest champions of the Justice League have succumbed to external corruption or moral compromises that pushed them into villainy either temporarily or permanently. Hal Jordan’s grief-fueled descent into Parallax during Emerald Twilight is still the most infamous villain turn in Justice League history to this day, alongside Maxwell Lord’s cold-blooded betrayal of the Justice League International in Countdown to Infinite Crisis and Triumph’s bitter fall after being erased from the timeline. Superman became an authoritarian tyrant in Injustice and The Justice Lords, and Mister Terrific recently ascended toward Darkseid-like godhood after absorbing the League's powers. DC's fresh focus on the Atom in morally complex storylines across multiple flagship titles is a refreshing treat for the hero. Despite possessing one of the coolest power sets in the DC Universe, Ray Palmer has often been relegated to brief supporting roles when his scientific knowledge and tools are needed, or to background crowd shots during Justice League crossovers. Now, between his disillusioned alliance with Deathstroke here and his ominous appearance perched right on Mister Terrific’s shoulder in Justice League Unlimited #27, DC is finally treating Palmer as an unpredictable major player whose brilliance and quiet frustrations could have serious consequences for the entire superhero community.

According to recent reports, eight decades after his comic book debut, the Atom should be one of pop culture’s most recognizable superheroes by now, especially since DC beat Marvel to the shrinking-hero concept by over two decades. Golden Age Atom Al Pratt first debuted all the way back in All-American Comics #19 in 1940, twenty-two years before Hank Pym. Silver Age physicist Ray Palmer launched in Showcase #34 in 1961, months ahead of Pym’s first Tales to Astonish appearance and over a year before Ant-Man donned his costume. Despite pioneering the subatomic superhero trope and joining the Justice League early in the Silver Age, DC's Atoms are still virtually unknown to casual audiences.

It has been revealed that the wide popularity gap between Marvel and DC's shrinking heroes comes down almost entirely to missed big-screen opportunities rather than any flaw in the Atom as a character. While Marvel Studios turned Ant-Man into a billion-dollar theatrical trilogy and a pivotal hero in Avengers: Endgame, DC relegated Ray Palmer to supporting roles on television’s Arrow and DC’s Legends of Tomorrow and cut Ryan Choi’s subplot down to a brief cameo in Zack Snyder’s Justice League. In the comics, DC is only beginning to put Atom at the forefront, not only in titles like Justice League Unlimited, but also in Absolute Universe's Absolute Flash.
