Another commented, "This was so real it almost gave me a stroke from rage." According to recent reports, “Sister” is one more place where women’s unpaid labor goes unnoticed. SNL may have found the topic funny, but the numbers are hard to laugh off:.

61 percent of family caregivers of adults are women, compared to 38 percent who are men.

Nearly 25 percent of women caregivers provide around-the-clock care, compared with 15 percent of men (AARP).

Family caregivers lose an average of $120,500 over a lifetime in wages, retirement income, and Social Security benefits (AARP).

In a recent development, the value of that care? $1.01 trillion a year, more than the United States spent on Medicare benefits in 2025 (AARP).

In a recent development, medicare generally doesn’t cover ongoing help with bathing, dressing, or getting to the bathroom. A home health aide costs roughly $6,688 a month in 2025. Most families can’t swing that, so the work falls to relatives and, as the sketch suggests, to daughters.

But the brothers in the ad never call it work. One cheerfully says Sister isn’t confused by the medications, or if she is, “she hasn’t brought it up to me, because I haven’t asked.” When labor happens quietly, at home and without a paycheck, the people who benefit can go years without noticing it.

In 2017, Stanford researchers writing in JAMA Neurology called out this inequity. They noted that 83 percent of caregiving comes from unpaid sources, meaning family, and that the average person with dementia needs 171 hours of care per month. Their verdict: “The best long-term care insurance in our country is a conscientious daughter.”.

No wonder the SNL ad resonated. Sisters saddled with caregiving resent the lopsided split, and the data backs them up. In November of 2026, Bay Alarm Medical, a provider of medical alert systems and senior safety solutions, surveyed 1,008 U.S. adults who share or once shared care for a parent over 65 and found that 47 percent resent a sibling because caregiving wasn’t divided fairly.

A study from Purdue University and the University of Missouri found that sibling tension climbs when a parent has cognitive impairment, especially among the siblings doing the caring. Non-caregivers often seemed unaware of the situation and stayed out of it to dodge conflict. Earlier research tells the same story from both sides: The sibling doing the most feels frustration and anger toward those doing less, while the less-involved feel guilty.

This helps explain why the last stage of a parent’s life is one of the most perilous for siblings. One of the most significant findings in a June 2026 survey by Bay Alarm was that 18 percent reported that they had lost a relationship with a sibling because of disagreements over caring for an aging parent.

Sources indicate that an Aging Life Care Manager can help families navigate disagreements and develop a initiative that respects the parent's wishes. Lina Supnet-Zapata, the incoming president of the Aging Life Care Association, knows personally the caregiving challenges for siblings. "After my father passed away," she indicates, "my siblings and I disagreed so much about what our mother's care should look like that we didn't speak for almost six months. Those arguments nearly destroyed our relationships.".

Now Supnet-Zapata helps families address these thorny problems. She describes a “default daughter trap,” where one capable daughter quietly becomes the family’s entire care blueprint. It has three moving parts:.

Sources indicate that invisible labor. She answers the calls, tracks medications, spots behavioral changes, coordinates providers, and translates medical updates for everyone else.

According to recent reports, the competence penalty. She’s great at logistics, so relatives assume her capacity is unlimited and step back.

Personal erosion. Carrying the load silently disturbs her career momentum, her physical and mental health, her marriage, and her ability to enjoy being a daughter instead of a case manager.

Supnet-Zapata offers three practical moves for caregivers:.

Replace vague offers with concrete tasks. “Let me know if you need anything” isn’t enough. Ask siblings to name the specific responsibilities they will do.

According to recent reports, build a backup proposal before a crisis. Organize legal, financial, and medical records early, and line up backup caregivers so everything doesn’t collapse when the primary daughter is unavailable.

Practice self-compassion. Drop the perfectionism and have realistic expectations. "The biggest thing I want families to understand is that being a good caregiver doesn't mean doing everything yourself," she says. "Getting help can be one of the best things you do for both your parent and yourself.".
