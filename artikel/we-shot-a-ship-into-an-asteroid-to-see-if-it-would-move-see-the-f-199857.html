We Shot a Ship Into an Asteroid to See if It Would Move. See the First Images of the Impact

Five years ago, NASA introduced its DART spacecraft with the express intent of ramming it into Dimorphos, an asteroid orbiting the larger Didymos asteroid, to see if its trajectory could be changed. The mission was a success, making Dimorphos the first celestial object whose orbit was moved by humans. And now, the European Space Agency has published images showing the damage the mission caused to the asteroid.

Industry observers note that the ESA livestreamed the presentation on Tuesday to the delight of cosmic rubberneckers everywhere. The images shown during the livestream were captured by the ESA’s Hera, which was rolled out in October 2024 and spent two years in deep space with its two CubeSats to measure, map, probe and eventually land on the asteroid. At the time of the mission, the only instruments that could see the impact and the massive cloud of debris flying off Dimorphos were the Hubble Space Telescope and multiple telescopes on Earth. But they were unable to get a good enough view to see how much damage it did to the asteroid or any other potential changes that occurred. This means that researchers had to estimate the damage until Hera highlighted scientists exactly what happened. Spoiler: All of those estimates were incorrect, and by a wide margin.

Sources indicate that hera indicated the damage was vastly more significant than originally thought. Scientists believed the impact would generate a 20-meter crater; Hera found that almost the entire end of the asteroid had been blown off, resulting in a crater the size of a football stadium and as wide as Dimorphos itself. This is visible in the above image — as you can see, Dimorphos used to be roughly the shape of an egg. Now, it’s the shape of a deviled egg or a kidney bean, depending on which angle you view it from.

The ESA’s Hera team discovered another neat effect of the impact: Before DART crashed into the asteroid, it was tidally locked to Didymos, much like the moon is to Earth. Scientists expected it to stay that way after the impact, but the images from Hera confirmed that Dimorphos now tumbles randomly, which none of the researchers had on their bingo card.

Recent findings suggest that “Before the DART impact, Dimorphos has always shown its same face to Didymos,” mentioned ESA Hera Mission Specialist Michael Kueppers during the presentation. “And this is what we see now. You can see it looks for a few seconds in the video like regular rotation, but it’s spinning around completely crazy.”.

ESA mission manager Ian Carnelli mentioned this surprise was great news because it’ll allow Hera to view Dimorphos on all of its sides, which would have been challenging if it were still tidally locked to Didymos.

It has been revealed that the ESA concluded the stream with one final surprise. According to Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel, the Hera team told engineers that it was entirely safe to go to Dimorphos because all the debris would be ejected from the DART impact, with the remaining bits reabsorbed by Didymos and Dimorphos before Hera arrived.

According to recent reports, but there is actually quite a lot of debris around the two asteroids. Kueppers indicates they’ve identified over 100 boulders so far, marked as white dots in the image above. Despite being completely wrong about the debris field, the Hera team was in good spirits since this means there are more things for the team to study.

It has been revealed that kueppers indicates scientists are “always happy” either way because when they predict something correctly, they get awarded, and when they predict something wrong, they have a challenge to solve. “The problem is for the engineers,” he remarked.

The three discoveries were made just 20 days into Hera’s mission, which is slated to last six months. The Hera team said more images and discoveries are coming in the next half year.

The point of the DART mission was to see whether humans could deal with a potentially dangerous asteroid by ramming into it or destroying it, as is often seen in Hollywood films like Deep Impact and Armageddon. NASA even frames it as a “planetary defense test.”.

The mission was considered an overwhelming success. NASA was hoping to shorten the orbit of Dimorphos by at least 73 seconds, with the most optimal guess being approximately 10 minutes. The orbit was shortened by 33 minutes and 15 seconds. It was such a large discrepancy that scientists studied it and determined that the large amount of debris created extra recoil that amplified the push.

Recent findings suggest that the change in orbit around Didymos also changed the orbit of both asteroids around the sun by about 11.7 microns per second. This doesn’t seem significant, but it adds up to about 1 kilometer every 2.7 years. This is enough to steer potential city-killer asteroids away from Earth’s orbit, given enough time and a major enough battering ram.

Hera’s findings also show the impact was much more devastating than scientists first thought. This provides even more data for NASA and the ESA to work through to proposal for such an event if it ever happens.

“As latest data come in each day, astronomers will be able to better assess whether, and how, a mission like DART could be used in the future to help protect Earth from a collision with an asteroid if we ever discover one headed our way,” noted Lori Glaze, director of NASA’s Planetary Science Division, in a statement from 2022.
