Industry News: Banks Have Had It Up to Here With Your AI Agent

🌐 Source: WIRED • 📅 22 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (922 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Wells Fargo is set to enact an updated agreement with millions of customers, specifically about AI agents, starting on Tuesday. The bank’s warning to early adopters could not be clearer. “Your use of any AI Agent is AT YOUR OWN RISK,” read Wells Fargo’s latest terms.

AI agents, which automate online tasks and click on your behalf, have stumbled through 2026. They remain nascent technology, but a growing subset of power people latched onto the personal bots this year. Sometimes these early adopters enjoyed the efficient help of automation, and other times they publicly embarrassed themselves when the AI agents made major mistakes, like posting bank details for everyone at work to see. Builders of these AI tools often market agents as the ultimate tool for shopping decisions, whether that’s putting together a grocery order, finding accommodations for a vacation, or discovering specific holiday gifts for your parents. I’ve used an agent to help shop for a new couch, comparing options within a specific budget and tracking price drops. (Related: Jimmy John’s reveals Christmas Vacation meal with exclu...)

Some consumers value these agents so much that they are oblivious to the overarching security concerns, pairing account credentials and granting permissions for the agents to take sweeping actions without forethought. Although the segment of online transactions completed by these autonomous tools remains minuscule, US-based banking institutions are already sick of third-party AI agents.

It’s not just banks; AI agents are igniting turf wars around the web. Amazon, one of the largest online retailers, blocked Meta’s Muse, one of the most widely used AI agents, from making purchases on its platform. Travel companies are also pushing back—United recently announced that personal bots would be blocked from purchasing flights on your behalf.

According to recent reports, the automation, which remains a central selling point of always-on agents, is exactly what may get users in trouble. “Consumers authorize goals, not every click,” says Kacy Kim, an associate professor of marketing at Bryant University who studies consumer behavior and emerging tech. While Kim expects some consumers to feel like it's unfair to be held accountable for their agent’s mistakes, she doesn’t expect these policy moves by financial institutions to scare off the early-adopter crowd already in deep and using the tools as part of everyday life.

Banks appear to be trying to get ahead of major concerns, in case agentic tools stick around and don’t just disappear as another tech fad. “All of our data indicates that most people don't even know what agents are, much less use them,” notes Sucharita Kodali, a Forrester analyst who focuses on ecommerce and consumer behavior. She sees banks as fundamentally risk-averse, so taking proactive measures now makes sense, even if most agentic issues remain edge cases.

In a recent development, “The banks see risks with everything,” she says. “From the security of transactions to automated withdrawals that could happen to the detriment of account holders.”.

Industry observers note that even though outliers exist, many customers are aware of the risks and are only comfortable handing over so much control to an AI agent, and only for certain shopping tasks. “People are very comfortable with AI buying toilet paper,” says Luca Cian, a professor at the University of Virginia who studies consumer psychology and AI.

In a recent development, he sees consumers as increasingly comfortable turning to AI tools for shopping advice but often only automating boring buys, like household supplies. Shoppers are less comfortable automating away dynamic, big-ticket purchases that require additional care and discretion.

Recent findings suggest that cian gives a hypothetical scenario where a customer’s AI agent accidentally buys 10 roasted chickens from the supermarket. Who’s to blame? The retailer would likely argue that they completed a valid order that you placed. The AI builder may claim that they just provided the software, and even included disclaimers about potential mistakes. And now your bank is making it clear they don’t want to be held responsible either. Hope you need the protein, because it looks like you just bought 10 hot chickens.

Certain credit cards, though, are looking into limited protections for shoppers. Mainly, American Express unveiled that some agentic mistakes, like ordering too many of a certain item, may be eligible for reimbursement in the future through the card provider if you can't get your money back from the original retailer.

It has been revealed that banks are pumping out reasonable warnings about third-party agents, but don’t get it twisted. These banks are all-in on first-party tools. “Banks are doing two things at once,” Cian claims. “They're locking the door to outside agents and building their own.” Consumers have interacted with Wells Fargo’s tool that answers questions using generative AI over a billion times. Bank of America revamped its fresh EricaAssist agent this year to help employees. Citigroup even launched an internal development platform specifically centered on agents.

Despite all the hype, Kodali remains skeptical about whether all of this hoopla is even necessary. “The problem with agents and shopping is that, first of all, it is not difficult to buy on Amazon or Walmart or anywhere else,” she says. “In most of those transactions, you don't need an agent.” Agentic shopping often seems to be a solution in search of a problem. Or at least an attempt by AI builders to find a way to earn affiliate revenue, eventually, from their software tools.

Whether AI agents grow in popularity for shopping automation or slowly fade into the digital graveyard, banks are already resolute on the matter. It’s your bot, and your problem.
