According to recent reports, cathie Wood's investment thesis for buying Bitcoin (BTC -1.54%) instead of gold is an outlier, but it could prove prescient. On Nov. 9, the Ark Invest chief executive officer remarked "gold is going down" and continued to argue that Bitcoin's correlation with gold has been minuscule since 2019.

She expects Bitcoin to keep outperforming gold, and she also expects that what's good for one asset may not have much effect on the other. That's a bet on the ratio between their prices, so Bitcoin could still fall in dollar terms -- but is Wood's idea fundamentally sound?

The correlation coefficient of two assets measures how closely their prices move together, from -1 (moving in opposite directions) to 1 (moving in lockstep). Bitcoin's correlation with SPDR Gold Shares, a popular gold exchange-traded fund (ETF), has averaged 0.13 during the past 10 years. Thus, the core of Wood's argument is grounded in fact.

But take a look at this chart, which shows the fluctuation of the correlation over time:.

Fundamental Chart data by YCharts.

In a recent development, as you can see, there are many brief periods when gold and Bitcoin are highly correlated, and many when they're strongly negatively correlated. While it's true that the average of all these fluctuations is about 0.13, a very weak correlation, there actually aren't ever any periods longer than a few months within the last 10 years where that value is in force.

Industry observers note that in September, Wood said that Bitcoin was "both a risk-off and a risk-on asset," meaning that (in theory) it can rally when investors are seeking safety and when they're seeking growth. In practice, Bitcoin has mostly traded like a risk-on asset, often falling in market sell-offs, sometimes right alongside gold. Gold, she told Fox Business in December 2025, is "more of a risk-off asset," which is consistent with what most investors believe about it and with its historical performance. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

Wood also argues that Bitcoin's supply makes it a superior store of value to gold over the long run. Bitcoin's circulating supply expands by about 0.8% annually, versus about 1.8% for gold, whose miners can dig more when prices rise. Plus, Bitcoin's supply is limited to 21 million bitcoins of which more than 20 million are already in circulation. Gold has no fixed cap, and its output rises with price.

During the past 10 years, Bitcoin's annualized volatility (how widely its price swings in a year) was 56.7%, whereas the gold ETF's volatility was just 16%. Bitcoin's worst drops have topped 80% several times, while the gold ETF's worst-ever loss was 45.6%.

It has been revealed that an asset that can lose most of its value can't reliably guard your purchasing power. Gold's adoption is also mature after centuries in central bank vaults and institutional portfolios, while Bitcoin's 17-year-old adoption story is still relatively latest, leaving more room for new buyers.

Industry observers note that wood's supply argument is the compelling part of her case, since Bitcoin's issuance is fixed and slowing while gold's rises with its price. Even so, I'm not convinced Bitcoin will keep outrunning gold the way Wood expects, and I don't find her correlation argument very persuasive. Still, most portfolios can stand to hold both some gold and some Bitcoin, and there isn't much point in committing to buy only one or the other.
