It has been revealed that ambarella (AMBA -19.96%) shares plummeted 38% to $27 in after-hours trading Tuesday after the chipmaker warned that a worldwide memory shortage is hurting demand for its products. Nasdaq halted trading in the stock at 4:01 p.m. ET ahead of the news.

Ambarella makes low-power chips that run artificial intelligence directly on devices such as security cameras, car safety systems, drones and dashcams. Revenue for the third quarter, which ended Oct. 31, rose 6% to $115.2 million, while adjusted earnings rose to $0.28 per share from $0.27 a year earlier.

But what spooked investors were the projections: For the fourth quarter, which ends Jan. 31, Ambarella expects revenue of $70 million to $75 million. That's a drop of 35% to 39% from the quarter it just reported.

According to recent reports, "The unprecedented rise in memory prices and the scarcity of memory supply we previously highlighted has intensified and is causing many customers to reduce their outlooks," CEO Fermi Wang noted in the release. He added that third-quarter revenue landed at the low end of the company's guidance range because consumer device sales came in below expectations. Ambarella doesn't make memory, but almost every device that uses its chips also needs memory chips. When memory becomes scarce and expensive, the camera and car electronics makers that buy from Ambarella build fewer products or delay them, and they order fewer Ambarella chips.

Recent findings suggest that ambarella isn't the only firm feeling it this week. Hewlett Packard Enterprise CEO Antonio Neri mentioned on Monday's earnings call that he expects memory supply to stay constrained until at least the second half of 2028. On Tuesday, HP Inc. said in its earnings release that it is "taking steps to address memory and storage cost pressures.".

Recent findings suggest that ambarella guided for adjusted operating expenses of $57.5 million to $59.5 million in the fourth quarter, with an adjusted gross margin of 59% to 60.25%. At the midpoints, that works out to about $43 million in gross profit against roughly $58.5 million in expenses, resulting in an adjusted operating loss of about $15 million. Using the company's third-quarter figures, which leave out stock-based compensation and acquisition costs the same way its guidance does, Ambarella earned an adjusted operating profit of about $11 million. Sources indicate that the company expects to keep its overall spending roughly level even as revenue drops, and it has the cash to do so. It ended the quarter with $282.8 million in cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities, up from $272.3 million three months earlier.

In a recent development, ambarella had been flagging the problem for months. Its March annual report listed memory shortages as a possibility. By its June quarterly report, it said there "have been tightening supply conditions in the memory market." In September's quarterly report, it added that tighter memory supply "could have an impact on" customers' demand for its products.

According to recent reports, the earnings release 8-K that came out a day earlier didn't mention memory specifically at all. It forecast third-quarter revenue of $115 million to $124 million. Ambarella came in at $115.2 million, the bottom of that range.

According to recent reports, wang tried to separate the shortage from the long-term story. Memory problems "do not have an impact on our very positive secular growth outlook for edge AI," he stated, and he expects the organization's fiscal 2028, which begins in February, "to be a growth year.".

Sources indicate that that's a claim worth assessing. Ambarella says many of its customers are lowering their outlooks while memory stays scarce and expensive, and if HPE's view holds, that may not ease until at least the second half of 2028. Wang also noted edge AI revenue hit a record in the third quarter, so the demand for AI on devices is real. The question is how long the memory squeeze keeps it from turning into sales.

Recent findings suggest that for investors in semiconductor stocks, Ambarella's warning is a reminder that the AI boom comes at a cost to companies outside the data center, with the same memory demand that's filling orders for AI servers draining supply from smaller device makers. Until Ambarella shows its revenue can recover, its stock is likely to trade on headlines about memory prices (as HPE and Dell did on Tuesday), and buyers looking for a turnaround in AI may want to wait for next quarter's guidance.
