Mice start moving indoors when fall nights turn cold, and a 1/4-inch gap in your house is enough to let them in

House mice often enter homes as temperatures drop. Learn how to seal entry points, set traps safely and clean up droppings without risk.

In places where house mice live outdoors for most of the year, the animals often enter homes in autumn and winter as the nights grow colder.

That pattern is described in a house mouse guide updated in July 2025. Its authors are Niamh Quinn of UC Cooperative Extension and Matt Frye of the Cornell IPM Program.

Industry observers note that “House mice can squeeze under gaps ¼ inch tall, and through openings 3/8 inch wide,” the two wrote. Those measurements equal about 6 and 10 millimeters. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

The house mouse (Mus musculus) is native to Central Asia. It crossed to North America aboard ships carrying settlers from Europe and elsewhere.

The species lives close to people because they store cereals and grains, the foods it prefers.

Industry observers note that an adult weighs about 0.5 ounce (14 grams) and is 5 to 7 inches (13 to 18 centimeters) long, tail included. It can eat up to 20 percent of its body weight a day, taken in roughly 200 small meals each night. (Related: Industry News: Welcome spaCy to the Hugging Face Hub...)

It has been revealed that A house mouse rarely strays more than 30 feet (9 meters) from its nest and its food. It builds that nest in a sheltered spot, using finely shredded paper or other fibrous material.

According to recent reports, where resources stay plentiful and stable, house mice can breed all year. A female may produce five to ten litters in one year, each with about five or six pups.

The young arrive 18 to 21 days after conception and can breed at five to six weeks of age. The usual life span is nine to 12 months.

“If a pen fits under a door, a mouse can enlarge the gap to enter,” Quinn wrote in November 2025. For rats in cities, Quinn put the smallest usable opening at the size of a quarter.

Mice climb well and can scale any rough vertical surface. They also run along wire cables and ropes, and can leap as high as 12 inches (30 centimeters) from the floor onto a flat surface.

Entry points listed by Quinn and Frye include cracks in building foundations and the openings around water pipes, vents and utility cables.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) advises checking inside and outside the home for gaps and holes.

“Exclusion is the most successful and permanent form of house mouse control,” the guide states.

It calls for metal or concrete to seal those cracks and openings. Doors, windows and screens need a tight fit, and door and window edges may need a metal cover to stop gnawing.

Plastic screening, rubber, vinyl, expanding foam and wood are classed as gnawable, and as ineffective plugs for mouse holes.

“Steel wool scouring pads make good temporary plugs but may rust over time,” Quinn and Frye cautioned. The CDC advises steel wool ringed with caulk for small holes, and lath screen or metal, cement, hardware cloth or metal sheeting for larger ones.

Quinn’s article adds door sweeps built to exclude pests, rather than weather strips, and metal mesh over vents.

Ultrasonic devices are of very limited use, because the sound is directional and does not pass behind objects. Some cats and dogs catch mice, but pets seldom keep a rodent population under control.

It has been revealed that for mice already indoors, the California guide favors setting enough traps to keep the trapping period short. It puts the maximum spacing at about 10 feet (3 meters) in areas with mouse activity.

It has been revealed that setting traps in pairs, 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 centimeters) apart, can raise the catch of mice that jump over obstacles. Good spots are behind objects, in dark corners and wherever there is evidence of mice.

Peanut butter is a popular bait because mice find it very attractive, though it can pose allergy risks to some people.

It has been revealed that every rodenticide is toxic to people, pets and wildlife. Poisoned mice often die inside the building being treated, and in that case trapping may be the better approach.

Droppings and gnaw marks can reveal where mice are active. One house mouse leaves approximately 50 to 75 droppings a day, according to University of Nebraska–Lincoln Extension.

House mice can carry leptospirosis, salmonella and lymphocytic choriomeningitis (LCM), a viral disease. Quinn and Frye cite an estimate that five percent of house mice across the United States carry the LCM virus and can transmit it.

People can catch LCM through exposure to fresh urine, droppings, saliva or nesting material from infected rodents. The most common hantavirus that causes lung disease in the United States comes from another species, the deer mouse.

Sources indicate that “Don’t vacuum or sweep rodent urine, droppings, or nesting materials,” the agency’s cleanup instructions warn. That can put tiny droplets carrying viruses into the air.

Sources indicate that the steps start with rubber or plastic gloves and a household disinfectant or freshly made bleach solution, left to soak for five minutes. The bleach mix is 1.5 cups (355 milliliters) in 1 gallon (3.8 liters) of water.

The information was obtained from the University of California Statewide Integrated Pest Management Program (IPM).
