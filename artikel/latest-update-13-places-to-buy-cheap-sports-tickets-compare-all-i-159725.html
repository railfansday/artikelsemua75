Recent findings suggest that before you buy sports tickets, price the same section at the team’s box office, on its official resale site, and on two or three ticket marketplaces, comparing the totals each indicates with fees included. Then buy only where a guarantee covers a fake ticket or a canceled game.

Industry observers note that comparing those totals got easier on May 12, 2025, when the Federal Trade Commission’s Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees took effect. It requires any business that advertises or lists live-event tickets, resale sites included, to show the total price up front, including mandatory fees. Only taxes, delivery charges, and extras you choose to add can wait until checkout, so the first price you see is close to what you’ll pay. The totals still differ for seats in the same section, though. Each seller charges its own fees, and resale tickets are priced by whoever lists them. That price can be above face value, the base price the team sets, or below it. (Related: Show, Don't Certify: How to Prepare for Your First Data...)

Industry observers note that the guarantee is the other half of the comparison. The major marketplaces all promise valid tickets that arrive in time, but they differ on postponed games, on refunds versus credit, and on whether you get replacement seats.

Start with the team, since a ticket it sells you is genuine.

Recent findings suggest that buying straight from another fan skips the fees and, usually, any guarantee. With a mobile ticket, have the seller transfer it to your account and accept it before you pay. On Ticketmaster, accepting a transfer challenges you a new barcode only you can use, and the seller’s copy stops working.

U.S. PIRG notes that the federal Fair Credit Billing Act gives credit card buyers far stronger protection than debit card buyers against fake tickets. So buy the lowest total that comes with a guarantee, and pay for it with a credit card.
