Recent findings suggest that automation Anywhere announced on November 24, 2026 that it had completed its acquisition of Boost.ai, adding conversational and voice AI for regulated industries to an agentic automation platform used by more than 4,000 customers. The combined offering is intended to carry customer requests from conversations to completed work across enterprise systems.

The tech giant reports nearly half a billion AI agent and automation executions annually. Boost.ai adds a customer-facing interface to that execution base, extending Automation Anywhere’s agentic automation and orchestration platform into customer service.

The completion follows the definitive agreement announced on October 7, 2026, under which Automation Anywhere agreed to acquire Boost.ai from Nordic Capital. At the time, it expected the transaction to close in the fourth quarter of 2026, subject to customary regulatory approvals and closing conditions.

It has been revealed that automation Anywhere calls the distance between what AI can understand and what an enterprise can execute the Autonomy Gap. Its customer-service sequence is engage, understand, reason, orchestrate and act, with governance at every step and people handling exceptions. Boost.ai supplies the voice and conversational entry point, while Automation Anywhere coordinates the work across systems and departments. It has been revealed that boost.ai supports more than 36 languages and has a organization-reported resolution rate above 90% in production deployments. Its hybrid architecture combines natural language understanding with generative and agentic AI. Its security and privacy measures are aligned with GDPR data-protection and HIPAA health-information requirements and supported by an independent SOC 2 report, reflecting its focus on regulated industries. Recent findings suggest that in the release’s lost-bank-card example, Boost.ai answers the call and Automation Anywhere applies bank policy, blocks and reissues the card across core banking and customer relationship management (CRM) systems, texts a reference and writes an audit trail without a human agent. The request touches four to five systems and two to three departments, illustrating why resolving a conversation and completing the underlying service operation are different tasks.

Industry observers note that the broader design uses reusable execution: identity verification, payments, account updates and compliance checks are built once, governed centrally and shared by customer, employee and business-process agents. The approach is intended to prevent each application’s AI agent from rebuilding the same capabilities inside a separate software silo.

Jack Henry already uses both companies’ products. It routes about 50,000 calls per month through Boost.ai for nearly 100 financial institutions and separately saves more than 4,000 hours monthly through Automation Anywhere, according to the release.

Jack Henry Chief Operating Officer Shanon McLachlan described its AI approach as balancing productivity gains with appropriate governance and human oversight. “Automation Anywhere and Boost.ai are helping us streamline work so our teams can focus more of their time on serving our clients,” she mentioned.

The late-2025 acquisition of Aisera added enterprise knowledge and automated employee service to Automation Anywhere’s platform. The firm now groups its portfolio into Autonomous Customer Service Operations for requests arriving through voice, chat or AI agents; Autonomous Employee Operations for IT, finance and HR requests; and Autonomous Business Operations for end-to-end processes across enterprise resource planning (ERP), CRM and other enterprise systems.

Recent findings suggest that for enterprises, the practical significance is a shared execution layer beneath customer, employee and business-process agents. The central test is whether that layer can complete requests across fragmented systems while keeping policy, audit trails and human exceptions under the same governance, making finished work rather than conversation alone the measure of the acquisition’s customer-service ambitions.
