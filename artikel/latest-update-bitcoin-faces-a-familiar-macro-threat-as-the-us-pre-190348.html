The Wall Street Journal reported on Nov. 24 that the US was preparing higher tariffs on China, the European Union, and other trading partners, which could impact Bitcoin.

Citing people familiar with the plans, the Journal reported that US Trade Representative (USTR) Jamieson Greer could release the findings of a months-long investigation into manufacturing overcapacity as soon as this week. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

On Nov. 24, the USTR docket still showed the investigation opened in March and four days of hearings in May, listing no determination or tariff schedule for the case. A check of published USTR notices in the Federal Register found none dated Nov. 19 through Nov. 24.

Industry observers note that the available White House, Treasury, and Commerce trade agency listings carried no announcement of the action described in the report. Importers still have no published list of the goods that would face these duties, the rates that would apply or the date they would start. On Nov. 20, Greer told Bloomberg Television he expected the work to be finished by year-end. He declined to specify which economies might face additional tariffs.

USTR opened the investigations on March 11 under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 and is examining whether policies such as subsidies sustain more manufacturing capacity than demand supports and whether those practices burden or restrict US commerce.

In a recent development, the agency’s fact sheet described the sectors it wanted to examine. An investigation of how a product is made or subsidized leaves open which products, if any, may face duties.

According to recent reports, the inquiry covers 16 economies, including the EU. The March 17 initiation notice also lists Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, South Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan and India. Hearings ran from May 5 through May 8.

According to recent reports, under Section 304 of the Trade Act, USTR must determine whether the practices are actionable. If it makes an affirmative finding, it must decide what action, if any, to take. The law requires the trade representative to publish the determination and the facts it rests on in the Federal Register.

Public hearings ended in May. The USTR docket still highlighted no determination on Nov. 24.

A USTR determination would establish whether the practices are actionable and, if so, what action USTR should take.

On July 23, USTR announced final duties on 60 economies after separate investigations into their failures to impose and enforce bans on imports made with forced labor.

On April 9, 2025, President Donald Trump posted that he had authorized a 90-day pause in some higher tariffs while raising the rate for China.

In a recent development, the executive order dated the same day amended the tariff schedule and applied the changes to covered goods entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, at or after 12:01 a.m. Eastern daylight time on April 10.

The reciprocal tariff changes came under different authority from the current investigation.

The written terms tell the tech giant whether its goods fall under the amended tariff schedule and whether its shipment enters before or after the cutoff. The applicable rate can depend on those details and on whether an exemption applies.

In a recent development, after Trump signaled a possible tariff increase on China in October 2025, Bitcoin fell during a broader selloff. That episode highlights why a tariff headline can impact crypto investors before importers have any upcoming duties to enter on customs paperwork.

If the USTR determination leads to tariffs, the product coverage, rates, and entry dates will show where costs change and when. Until USTR publishes its findings, the docket remains the place to look for the public decision.
