Some FHA borrowers pay mortgage insurance for a decade or more. There's an industry push to change the rules

Homeowners with conventional loans who pay mortgage insurance typically can shed that expense — meant to protect a lender against loss if the borrower defaults — once they've built sufficient equity in their home. But that's not how it works for those with a Federal Housing Administration loan.

A fresh push to change the rules governing mortgage insurance for FHA loans came this week, after the Department of Housing and Urban Development released a report to Congress on Monday showing that the FHA's mortgage insurance fund remains financially strong. Its capital ratio — a measure of its financial cushion against potential losses — was 10.29% as of Sept. 30, down from 11.47% a year earlier, but still more than five times the 2% minimum required by Congress. Sources indicate that "The program's financial strength provides an opportunity for targeted changes that improve affordability for borrowers while preserving the long-term stability" of FHA's insurance fund, said Bob Broeksmit, president and CEO of the Mortgage Bankers Association, in a statement released after the report was issued. The push, which revives a longstanding mortgage industry request, also comes several weeks after President Donald Trump issued an executive order that included calling for the launch of a federal pilot program that would cancel mortgage insurance on certain conventional loans once borrowers meet equity requirements.

The order would apply generally to future loans backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac — two government-sponsored enterprises that purchase qualifying mortgages from lenders and resell them as bundled securities. It wouldn't apply to FHA loans.

Recent findings suggest that FHA mortgages are issued by private lenders but insured by the federal government, which protects lenders against losses if a borrower defaults. The loans are designed to make homeownership more accessible, particularly for borrowers with limited savings for a down payment or with lower credit scores.

Affordability challenges persist in the housing market, with elevated home prices and mortgage rates putting homeownership out of reach for many prospective buyers. Existing homeowners are also facing rising costs alongside their mortgages, including property taxes and homeowners insurance amid overall high inflation.

About 83% of new FHA purchase loans went to first-time homebuyers in the most recent fiscal year, according to the FHA report to Congress. Altogether, the FHA insured more than 8.4 million mortgages as of Sept. 30.

The median age of first-time buyers is 40, according to a new report from the National Association of Realtors.

FHA loans also require borrowers to pay mortgage insurance regardless of the size of their down payment. Under current rules established in 2013, FHA borrowers with a down payment that's less than 10% of the loan must pay mortgage insurance premiums for the duration of the loan. Those who put 10% or more down pay that insurance for 11 years.

Industry observers note that borrowers generally pay both an upfront mortgage insurance premium and an annual premium, which is typically divided into monthly payments. The upfront premium is usually 1.75% of the base loan amount and can be financed into the mortgage. For many borrowers with 30-year FHA loans, the annual premium is 0.55% of the outstanding loan balance.

Conventional mortgages generally don't require mortgage insurance unless the loan is for more than 80% of the home's value. In 2022, the premium typically ranged from 0.58% to 1.86% annually, according to Fannie Mae.

If those borrowers have to pay mortgage insurance, they can typically request its removal once they've built 20% equity in the home, or it is automatically canceled when their loan drops to 78% of the original value.

The mortgage industry has floated two different proposals.

It has been revealed that one would reduce the rate that applies to FHA mortgage insurance. For example, the Mortgage Bankers Association recently suggested a rate reduction of 20 basis points, which would lower the standard 0.55% rate to 0.35% for upcoming borrowers.

For example: On a $350,000 mortgage, a 0.55% rate generally translates into $1,925 annually, or about $160 monthly, at the start of the loan. As the principal owed slowly goes down over time through a borrower's monthly payments, so does that mortgage insurance amount.

At 0.35%, the annual premium would be $1,225 at the start of the loan, or $102 monthly — a difference of $58 per month.

In a recent development, the FHA last cut that rate in 2023, when it was reduced from 0.85%. At the time, HUD estimated that affected borrowers would save an average of $800 annually. However, in the FHA's new report to Congress, the agency noted that the 2023 cut has reduced the fund's revenue, contributing to a decline in its capital ratio.

The other industry proposal would implement auto-cancellation of premiums once the loan balance fell to 78% of the home's original value at purchase — in other words, the same as for Fannie- and Freddie-backed mortgages.

"Cancellation prevents the squeeze for borrowers who have paid down their loans and shouldn't be paying for coverage they have outgrown," said Michael Farrell, president of the National Association of Mortgage Brokers, a trade group that generally has supported the cancellation proposal.

"NAMB believes that cancellation at 78% protects the [FHA insurance fund], because premiums can restart if the [capital] ratio ever falls to 2%," Farrell noted.

FHA mortgages received that treatment from 2001 through 2013, but the agency eliminated it to shore up its insurance fund after financial reserves deteriorated over time following the housing market crash, which led to the 2008 financial crisis.

Sources indicate that whether the proposals will go anywhere this time is uncertain. HUD, which oversees the FHA, did not respond to a CNBC email seeking more information.
