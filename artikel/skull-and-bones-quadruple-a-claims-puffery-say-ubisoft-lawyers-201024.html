Skull And Bones Quadruple-A Claims “Puffery,” Say Ubisoft Lawyers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that remember Skull and Bones? It spent 11 years in development and was forgotten the second it launched, as Ubisoft's foray into quadruple-A gaming almost immediately found itself lost at sea. It's hard to be charitable about a game with a $200 million budget that was worse than the then-decade-old Black Flag, and not a notch on its contemporary, Sea of Thieves, but it didn't just face any old ordinary backlash.

In 2025, Jacob Himberger sued Ubisoft in Kansas over allegations of deceptive marketing, arguing that the manufacturer violated the state's consumer protection act.

Sources indicate that the lawsuit is two-pronged. First, it suggests that Ubisoft misled consumers into believing they were buying ownership of the game, rather than a revocable license, highlighting The Crew as an example of server-dependent games becoming unplayable. Secondly, it points to the quadruple-A claim, suggesting that Ubisoft went beyond hype and misled consumers into buying Skull and Bones. However, as reported by Game File, the company's lawyers argue this was "classic puffery.".

According to recent reports, in lawyer-speak, "puffery" means that a claim was not objective advertising and instead exaggerated promotion. Ubisoft argues that when CEO Yves Guillemot described Skull and Bones as "a really full, triple... quadruple-A game, that will deliver in the long run," he was just being a salesman. Though it's also worth noting that the game-maker's misguided pirate RPG was almost its first $70 game, making it distinct from other 'triple-A' titles in its portfolio. Though, these days, you can buy Skull and Bones for $29.99.

It has been revealed that there isn't really a standard idea of what quadruple-A means, but a few studios have thrown the label around to try and signal that their game is truly next-gen and above what other triple-A titles can offer. Xbox, for example, claimed its canceled Perfect Dark reboot was 'AAAA' in job listings, while Dead Space creator Glen Schofield called his spiritual successor The Callisto Protocol an 'AAAA' experience ahead of launch. Not exactly the most esteemed firm, and if anything, a signal that you should probably be a bit cautious when a game is marketed as the next big 'AAAA' hit.
