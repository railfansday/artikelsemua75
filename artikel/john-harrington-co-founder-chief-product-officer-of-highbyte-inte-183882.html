Its Intelligence Hub provides an Industrial DataOps layer for connecting, modeling, contextualizing, and delivering operational data to enterprise applications, cloud platforms, and AI systems. In a recent development, before co-founding HighByte in 2018, you held leadership roles at Kepware and PTC, and had worked alongside your fellow founders since 2012. What specific interoperability or security problem convinced you that manufacturers needed an entirely new industrial data platform, and how has that original vision evolved with the rise of AI?

It has been revealed that I’ve worked in manufacturing and industrial environments for nearly my entire career. In these roles, I’ve had the opportunity to view industrial data from both sides of the coin, working at a manufacturer that made data-driven decisions and a number of software providers that enable digitalization and smart manufacturing.

This is a significant challenge. For one thing, users often need data that is curated differently than how most execution systems—MES solutions, ERPs, data historians, or otherwise—choose to store it. If they are lucky, they might be able to copy data from these systems and paste it into Excel to be analyzed and compared. Unfortunately, this method takes a lot of time, delays their decision-making, and requires access to domain experts who can explain the nuances of the data being reviewed.

Luckily, today’s data storage and analysis systems are tremendous. Cloud platforms like AWS and Azure and data warehouse providers like Snowflake and Databricks have simplified the scale challenge, creating unified repositories for industrial data across plants and offering easy access for business customers through applications and LLMs. Still, there’s a larger need to link these capable operational systems with emerging cloud and AI solutions, which itself requires the automated delivery of usable, contextualized, and compliant data. Like any other business, AI has had an impact on the way HighByte operates. On the one hand, we’ve incorporated AI into our platform, creating a Pipeline AI Agent and a Modeling Agent for LLM-assisted pipeline, model, and instance generation and management. But these solutions have also changed how customers use and interact with industrial data in their day-to-day operations. By establishing a foundation of connected, contextualized, high-quality data for our customers, we provide the critical information that LLMs and agents need to operate effectively and efficiently in manufacturing environments.

Industry observers note that manufacturers are often eager to deploy AI before addressing the condition of their underlying data. What does a robust Industrial DataOps strategy look like in practice, and what warning signs indicate that a company’s data foundation is not ready for AI?

It’s vital to note that any Industrial DataOps strategy should be part of an organization’s more comprehensive Industrial Data Strategy, which encompasses a broad view of organizational scope, applications, data platforms, integrations, networks, governance, and regulatory requirements. Within this larger strategy, a more targeted Industrial DataOps approach can address integration requirements and focus on delivering usable data to required applications based on established contracts and cadences. This involves creating a robust data foundation and defining standard data products and pipelines for each application or use case.

According to recent reports, any firm that lacks a strong data foundation will not be capable of successful AI deployment at scale. It’s as simple as that. An effective Industrial DataOps strategy is one that ensures data is contextualized, connected, governed, and trusted before it’s consumed by applications or AI models.

Manufacturers should be careful not to rush AI deployments if their data is not yet up to the task. The warning signs are easy to spot: siloed systems, inconsistent naming conventions, duplicate data pipelines, and teams spending more time and effort finding data than actually using it. If you’re still debating which version of your data is accurate, you’re not ready to implement and scale AI solutions.

Industry observers note that AI agents work best when they have very focused objectives and a limited number of capable tools at their disposal to access data. Overly broad agents with too many potential tools and unclear data resources will inevitably result in hallucinations and incorrect decisions, ultimately doing more harm than good.

Industrial data is frequently fragmented across programmable logic controllers, manufacturing execution systems, historians, enterprise applications, and cloud platforms. What context must be added to this raw data before an AI model or agent can reliably interpret it and make useful decisions?

Industry observers note that imagine if I dropped you in the middle of a supermarket and asked you to buy me the items that I need. No detail, no list, no direction. Even worse, the aisle markers and signage are nowhere to be found. What do you think the odds are that you’d get me exactly what I’m looking for?

In a recent development, this is the type of scenario that AI models and agents are likely to find themselves in without proper context. Even with access to raw manufacturing data, this information alone cannot provide sufficient guidance for AI models to take accurate and impactful action. They need as much context as possible in order to effectively carry out their assigned tasks and provide reliable output.

Going back to the supermarket example, imagine if I asked you to specifically buy the supplies I need to create a PB&J sandwich. You could walk into the store, find the proper aisles based on the clearly marked signage, and get me the bread, peanut butter, jelly, and a knife for spreading. Maybe even some potato chips on the side.

The agent can cite clearly marked temperature, pressure, and vibration data and compare it against historical performance, allowing it to recognize any relevant fluctuations and take preventative action as needed.

You have predicted that agentic AI could produce a hundredfold increase in edge-based data consumers, with specialized agents supporting maintenance, quality, scheduling, and supply chain operations. How must traditional factory data architecture change to support this scale without creating thousands of brittle integrations?

Recent findings suggest that traditional industrial data architectures were designed to support a handful of applications consuming data in each factory. Now, agentic AI is set to completely change that equation. Instead of managing dozens of data consumers, manufacturers will need to support thousands of automated, specialized agents moving forward.

It has been revealed that the answer here isn’t thousands of new point-to-point integrations. Instead, it’s a centralized data layer that can contextualize, govern, and securely deliver the right data product to the right consumer at the right time—regardless of whether it’s a human user or an automated agent. Accessibility, context, and governance will all be key for maximizing the full potential of AI solutions on the factory floor.

HighByte has introduced Model Context Protocol services that allow industrial data pipelines to be exposed as tools for AI agents. How does Model Context Protocol complement existing standards such as Open Platform Communications Unified Architecture, Message Queuing Telemetry Transport, and application programming interfaces, and what governance is required to prevent agents from accessing the wrong tools or data?

Sources indicate that OPC UA and MQTT were designed for reading and writing device-level data, while APIs were designed for interacting directly with applications. MCP was designed specifically for interacting with LLMs. The HighByte Intelligence Hub is a middleware layer that unites these protocols, interacting with each device, system, and agent using their preferred protocol and curating data products using data from one or more sources.

Just as importantly, Intelligence Hub creates a layer of control and governance between agents and operational systems. Rather than giving agents unrestricted access to data, manufacturers can govern what tools the agents can access, which systems they can interact with, and what kinds of decisions they can influence.

Traditionally, governance was applied in the application layer through user-role-based access controls. As manufacturers deploy more agents across maintenance, quality, scheduling, and supply chain operations, traditional applications go away and agent-specific governance becomes crucial. In an agentic ecosystem, the governance of data flows effectively becomes the mechanism for governing the overall actions of AI agents.

In a recent development, the Pipeline AI Agent in HighByte Intelligence Hub allows consumers to create, explain, and modify industrial data pipelines through natural language while retaining human review. Which DataOps responsibilities are well suited to AI automation, and where should human approval remain mandatory?

Deploying complex data pipelines is often iterative and, as a result, relatively challenging. With the HighByte Intelligence Hub, we have provided multiple modes for easily creating, modifying, and managing both modeling and pipeline configuration tasks. A user can manually create and modify their own configurations, leverage the Pipeline and Modeling Agents for AI-driven assistance, and use MCP configuration tools to fully configure the Intelligence Hub externally with an agent. We expect that the user will select the most appropriate method based on their experience level with the Intelligence Hub and AI and the size and scope of the changes.

According to recent reports, given all these methods of configuration, the system will need to be tested and validated. This is done in real time by agents that observe and approve any changes being applied. The agents are also great at explaining what the configuration does for troubleshooting and documentation purposes.

In a recent development, global manufacturers need common data standards across facilities, but every plant has different equipment, processes, and operational requirements. How can companies create reusable industrial data products while preserving the local context that makes the data meaningful?

It has been revealed that this is one of the core benefits of a robust Industrial DataOps strategy and platform. When different facilities are unified under a single platform, teams can align and standardize data collection and naming practices across different sites, while still maintaining the flexibility needed to account for site-specific context.

The key is to separate standardization from uniformity. Manufacturers should establish common enterprise models, naming conventions, and governance policies, but at the same time should still allow sites to capture the operational context unique to each facility’s processes and outputs. For example, a bottling line in one facility may operate differently than an equivalent line elsewhere, but both can still map to a shared enterprise data model.

HighByte recently partnered with Siemens to combine industrial edge connectivity, contextualized data, and tools for building AI models and agents. Does this type of collaboration signal that the future industrial AI stack will be built through interoperable ecosystems rather than a single end-to-end platform?

Yes, the future industrial AI stack won’t be owned by any one organization; it will be built on interoperable technologies that each solve a specific problem well. HighByte partnered with Siemens because we both recognized that successful industrial AI requires both operational connectivity and trusted, contextualized data. Together, Siemens Industrial Edge and HighByte Intelligence Hub help manufacturers bridge the gap between IT and OT systems; connecting data from machinery, SCADA, historians, MES, ERP and all the rest of the systems in and around the factory into a unified data infrastructure that AI can actually use.

AI agents are only as effective as the data they consume, and each operational system provides a piece of the puzzle for the agent, but not the entire puzzle. Open, interoperable ecosystems give manufacturers the flexibility to innovate with best-in-breed solutions without creating fresh silos or vendor lock-in. By combining Siemens’ industrial connectivity capacity with HighByte’s DataOps capabilities, manufacturers can more easily connect, contextualize, and consume industrial data to scale AI models and agents across the enterprise.

Predictive maintenance, quality inspection, energy optimization, and production planning are all frequently presented as promising industrial AI applications. How should manufacturers select their first use case, establish measurable business value, and avoid becoming trapped in a cycle of pilots that never scale?

It has been revealed that it’s essential that manufacturers start with a problem they need to solve, not a technology they want to adopt. The best first use case is one that is causing frustration today, has actual measurable business value, ties to accessible data, and has a clear path to operational adoption. Once that has been achieved, you can take your learnings and apply AI to tackle the next challenge.

Today, too many teams are focusing on deploying isolated pilots, when they should really be prioritizing the underlying data architecture that’s required to scale AI across systems, sites, and enterprise infrastructure. Success will come not from a single use case, but instead, from building a reusable foundation that supports multiple AI deployments over time and expands in tandem with increasing AI integration.

Looking ahead, how do you expect AI agents to change the roles of plant operators, engineers, and data teams, and what technical or organizational barrier must be solved before manufacturers can safely give these systems greater autonomy?

I don’t foresee a world where AI agents fully replace manufacturing professionals, but I do see one where AI changes how they work. With the support of effective agents, operators will be able to spend less time searching for information and more time making impactful, informed decisions. Engineers will be tasked with overseeing and optimizing fleets of highly specialized agents, and data teams will shift from report building to data product and AI system governance.

The real differentiator in this AI-driven future will be how effectively manufacturers can deploy and govern specialized agents that can take autonomous action on trusted industrial data for real business challenges. Those who solve the data and governance challenge early will be the ones who realize the greatest value from AI. The ultimate value of AI for manufacturers is to reduce downtime, increase throughput, reduce defects and scrap, improve safety, reduce costs, and improve the agility of the business to react to supply and demand changes.
