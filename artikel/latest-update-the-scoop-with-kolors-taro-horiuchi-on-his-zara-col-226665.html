It has been revealed that welcome to the Scoop: a weekly email series in which I quiz fashion insiders on the stories of the week. This will be a way for the Vogue Business community to synthesize and reflect on the latest headlines and get a little inside scoop every Friday.

In a recent development, this week’s guest is Taro Horiuchi, who took over the creative direction of Japanese brand Kolor from its founder Junichi Abe, a couple of years ago. Before that, Taro was running his namesake womenswear label, which he released after moving to France in 2010, as well as a menswear line called TH Product, founded in 2018 (the two merged when he took over Kolor in 2025). Sources indicate that before all that, Taro studied photography at London’s Kingston University and fashion design at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Antwerp, alongside Demna and Glenn Martens, who were in the year above and below, respectively. The thread that seems to bind Taro’s work together is turning basic garments on their heads, which is also what he sought to do in his most recent collaboration with a little conglomerate called Zara. I called him up to discuss that and more. (Related: Latest Update: F1 star Max Verstappen played 40 hours o...)

On December 3, we will be launching a collaboration with Zara. We are hosting a pop-up in Tokyo on December 2, and the global launch will be the following day. It’s the first global collaboration we’ve announced since I took over as creative director in 2025, so I feel honored to be in this position. The collection will span womenswear, menswear, and childrenswear. It’s the first time Kolor is making kids’ clothing, so that was an interesting challenge for us.

Shots from the Kolor x Zara campaign. It’s the brand’s first global collaboration since Horiuchi took over as creative director from founder Junichi Abe in 2025.

What was it like designing children’s clothes then?

Sources indicate that the core challenge for us in this project was expressing the brand without the high pricing and intricate details. We wanted to make good, interesting clothes regardless of whether it was for children or adults. However, there are constraints when it comes to making clothes for children, both in terms of the textiles you can use and because the accessories and the parts on the clothing are very small. That was definitely a learning experience for us, which we took on happily.

It has been revealed that can you tell me a little bit about the collection? What was the inspiration behind it?

So the theme for this project is “World Uniform”. It’s something we do at Kolor: take something basic and turn it into something a little different. Fans of Kolor are always on the cutting edge of fashion, and they are always seeking something new. But those who shop at Zara are looking for clothes for their daily lives. So our challenge was to find the perfect balance between familiar and inspirational.

Behind the scenes of the Kolor x Zara campaign shoot. The collection’s theme, “World Uniform”, reflects Kolor’s habit of taking basic garments and making them “a little different”.

For sure, there are those who go to Zara for basics, but I’d argue that’s changing because of all the luxury brand collaborations Zara orchestrates, including the one with Kolor.

One thing that really stood out to me through this experience was the very high quality of fabrics and operations that Zara offers. My family and friends wear a lot of Zara, though I personally haven’t, so I wasn’t aware of the way they work. But the whole process was very inspirational to me. Both the product quality and the speed of responding were quite remarkable.

Industry observers note that I remember when Zara launched in Greece; I was about 8 or 9. My mother was delighted because she could buy cheap but nice clothes for us. I still have some of those sweaters from 30 years ago, and my son wears them now. Has it been fun to work with Zara’s manufacturing resources?

For sure. When it comes to any collaboration, it doesn’t make sense for me to make anything you can make alone. I am very happy to say we managed to realize something that Kolor couldn’t have made on its own, and I believe Zara feels the same way about us.

A look from Kolor x Zara, which rolls out globally on December 3 after a pop-up in Tokyo on December 2.

Sources indicate that you’ve taken over Kolor from its founder. It feels like a tough gig to follow a founder who is still involved and alive. But if we look at people like Julian Klausner at Dries Van Noten and Haider Ackermann at Tom Ford, maybe that’s part of the success recipe? How do you work with Junichi Abe, and is there a lot of pressure to match him?

I’ll answer your second question about pressure first. Of course, I feel the pressure, and I also respect Abe-san incredibly. Whether it’s a recipe for success, I’m not sure yet. What I can say is that the core value and DNA of a fashion brand aren’t something you can build in one day. It takes time. All I can do is what Abe-san told me to do at the very beginning of my time at Kolor: “Don’t repeat what I could. Just make Kolor unique to you,” he noted.

There are quite a few open positions at the big luxury houses at the moment, and we expect them to be filled any day now. Does the constant change-up in creative directors affect the way you and other designers work or view the industry?

According to recent reports, things change much faster than when I started in fashion around maybe 2005. As I was saying earlier, building a brand takes time because it needs to have a culture and a philosophy. The compatibility of the brand and the designer is very vital too in all that. In that sense, speedy changes are not good because they don’t allow the two to get to know each other.

Kolor x Zara. Horiuchi says the collaboration let him make something Kolor “couldn’t have made on its own”, thanks to Zara’s fabrics and production speed.

Sources indicate that applications for the 2027 LVMH prize are now open. You won the Diesel Award at ITS 20 years ago. How necessary are those awards and prizes to young designers?

I graduated from the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Antwerp in 2007, and I am still so thankful to Barbara (Franchin, founder and director of ITS) and Renzo (Rosso, founder of Diesel and founder and chairman of OTB) for the opportunity. Looking back now, this was a big turning point in my career. It wasn’t so much about winning the award, but more about the people I met. Demna was in the year above me at Antwerp, and together we organized an exhibition about Japan. At the Diesel award, I met Matthieu Blazy, who is now at Chanel. So, for me, those prizes and competitions are meaningful because of the communities they draw together. If you want to tell what the future of the industry will be in 20 years, you should look at who is entering those competitions now.

Sources indicate that but I have a concern about the future of the industry. When I was a student, the tuition fees for Antwerp were around €1,000 per year. Now, for non-EEA students, it’s between €13,000 and €25,000. The people who can’t afford such high tuition will not be entering the fashion industry. Demna wouldn’t have been at Antwerp if the tuition was any more expensive. So maybe, one suggestion for the organizations that support those awards would be to focus more on scholarships and grants.
