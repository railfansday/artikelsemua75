Conservation Groups Warn Senate Bill Could Put Bears Ears, Boundary Waters and More at Risk

A permitting reform bill presently before the US Senate would “effectively lock in project approvals that irrevocably open up recreation landscapes to development, including places like Bears Ears or the Boundary Waters,” states outdoor recreation advocacy group Outdoor Alliance.

Industry observers note that the Senate is currently voting on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA), which is co-sponsored by Senators Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV), Mike Lee (R-UT), Martin Heinrich (D-NM), and Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI). The bill’s aim is to speed up energy and infrastructure projects by streamlining the permitting process. (Related: Industry News: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Fa...)

Proponents argue the bill would lower energy costs, provide more regulatory certainty for developers, and facilitate future clean energy projects, but critics say it would weaken environmental protections and curtail public input. (Related: Showcase Your Projects in Spaces using Gradio...)

Bears Ears National Monument. Photo via Bureau of Land Management.

Sources indicate that the bill would narrow or change several existing environmental review and permitting requirements under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), Clean Water Act, and Endangered Species Act. Crucially, outdoor recreation groups like Outdoor Alliance also warn that provisions designed to create “project certainty” for developers would make it difficult or impossible for future administrations to reconsider or modify permits.

The Center for Western Priorities, in partnership with Earthworks, has identified dozens of public-land landscapes that could be affected by the legislation, including Bears Ears National Monument, the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness, and Tongass National Forest. The map highlights areas where existing or proposed mining, drilling, logging, and infrastructure projects could benefit from the bill’s provisions protecting federal development approvals from future reversal.

More than 110 mining claims have been filed in the Bears Ears area since the monument’s boundaries were shrunk, for instance. “Any mining looks approved by this administration would be nearly impossible to revoke, even if the monuments are restored,” the Center for Western Priorities writes.

Industry observers note that if BAAJA passes in the Senate, it will go back to the House of Representatives. Conservation groups are urging the public to express their support for public-lands integrity to lawmakers. Outdoor Alliance created an Action Alert to make it easy to contact members of Congress.
