Tech Report: Watermarks can be stripped. Signatures can't. Know which you're buying.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that two technologies answer the question was this content AI-generated, and they are constantly conflated in procurement conversations. Statistical watermarks embed a signal in the content itself. Cryptographic provenance attaches a signed manifest about the content, the C2PA pattern: what made it, with what model, when, edited how, signed by a key the verifier can check.

Sources indicate that watermarks are clever and fragile. Token-level statistical marking survives light paraphrase but degrades under translation, heavy editing, and re-encoding. Image watermarks fall to cropping and regeneration. Detection generally requires access to the marking scheme's statistics, which means watermarks work best as an operator checking your own output, not as a public verification layer. And a determined actor can strip most marks with effort that keeps falling every quarter. (Related: Jujutsu Kaisen’s Gojo voice actor had no idea he was re...)

Signatures have the opposite failure profile. A signed creation record cannot be forged without the key, and verification is public and cheap. The weakness is detachment: platforms re-encode media and strip metadata, so a valid signature often fails to survive the journey from your system to a social feed. Signed provenance proves a lot at the point of creation and less after ten hops through infrastructure that discards metadata. (Related: IShowSpeed breaks down in tears after finishing GTA 6 i...)

Sources indicate that the practical conclusion for anyone building or buying: neither technology alone answers the compliance question, which is can you show, to a skeptical third party, which of your systems produced which content, when. That answer lives in your own records, in a signed, append-only creation log binding content hash to model version, operator, and timestamp. Watermarks and C2PA manifests then become corroboration layered on top of evidence you already hold, instead of a hope that the mark survives the wild.

Sources indicate that disclosure laws are pushing this into budgets. When a regulation requires you to label AI-generated content, the labeling obligation is trivial and the provability obligation is the actual work: you will need to demonstrate that the label was applied, by which system, at publication time, and your watermark vendor's dashboard is not that demonstration.

If a regulator asked you to prove a specific piece of content came from your pipeline, what artifact would you hand over, and could a stranger verify it without calling your vendor?
