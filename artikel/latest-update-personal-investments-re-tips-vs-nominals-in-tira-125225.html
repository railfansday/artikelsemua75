It has been revealed that bottom line if you think inflation will be greater than ~2.5% tips are better, otherwise nominals are better.Again, I think you may be overlooking how your planned spending reacts to inflation as well.10 years at 2.5% is about 28% cumulative. So, if you were planning to spend $50K this year on stuff subject to consumer inflation, and you are expecting annualized inflation of 2.5% over the next 10 years, your central estimate is buying equivalent stuff in 2036 will cost you about $64K.OK, but suppose inflation is 3.5% annualized instead. That's about 41% cumulative, so now it actually costs $70.5K.OK, but suppose inflation is 1.5% annualized instead. That's about 16% cumulative, so now it actually costs $58K.So now scenarios:You put enough in TIPS to expect it to be $64K in 10 years at 2.5% inflation. If inflation is 3.5% annualized instead, you have $70.5K to spend. If it is 16%, you have $58K to spend. Cool, you always have enough to spend, no more but also no less.OK, then instead you put it half in TIPS, and half in nominals. If inflation is 3.5% annualized instead, you have $67.25K to spend. Oops, not enough, since it actually costs $70.5K. If inflation is 1.5% annualized, you have $61K to spend. Yay, more than you need, since it actually costs $58K.So which is preferable?Well, assuming the risks are symmetric (which is not necessarily always a great assumption), the probability-weighted expected value should be the same. However, the variability of the NET outcome (money available to spend versus actual cost of planned spending) is different. In one case, there is no variability. In the other case, there is sometimes less than you need for the planned spending, and sometimes more. And that is a form of risk.This is what I mean by uncompensated risk. You have increased the uncertainty of the outcome, but haven't added any probability weighted expected value. And normally, that is considered a bad deal in finance.All this is true because you are not just betting on how much money you will get. You are betting on how much you will get versus how much you are going to spend. And because how you much you are going to spend is also a function of consumer inflation, it creates this situation where one bet is in fact riskier than the other, despite the fact they have the same probability-weighted expected value.Finally, I note none of this analysis depends on guessing whether inflation will be higher or lower than the financial markets are implicitly expecting over any given period. It instead assumes you can't guess any better about that then is already represented in market expectations.we are in the favorable position of not needing to spend any of the tira due to our high balances in brokerage and rira, obviously we will have to deal with RMD's in 2029 but spending down of assets is difficult at times. our philosphy in retirement is don't deny yourself and enjoy your money but don't burn money. we could buy a sailboat or 2nd home just to say we have one of each and then wonder where $2mill went.
