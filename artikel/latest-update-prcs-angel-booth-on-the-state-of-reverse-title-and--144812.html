There’s no substitute for experience, as the time-worn cliché goes, and Angel Booth of Premier Reverse Closings (PRC) has spent nearly a quarter century building her knowledge base across the intersection of reverse mortgages and title and settlement services.

“I’ve been doing this going on 24 years,” Booth told HousingWire‘s Reverse Mortgage Daily during the recent National Reverse Mortgage Lenders Association (NRMLA)’s Annual Meeting & Expo in San Antonio. “I’ve done every role you can think of in an operation, from filing paperwork to answering phones, running a desk, managing an office and now being in sales. I recently got a promotion into a role where I’m the vice president of operations and the national sales manager as well. It’s basically things I’ve been doing already.”.

In this edition of RMD’s Monday Morning Q&A, Booth offers her thoughts on the state of reverse mortgage business amid higher interest rates and slower home sales, the key risk assessments that her organization provides to lenders and servicers, and the need to train the next generation of title and settlement professionals. Pierson: Business is slow across the mortgage industry and that’s evident in Home Equity Conversion Mortgage numbers. As title and settlement providers, you’re also reliant on closing more loans. Has it been discouraging to see HECM business running so low or has much of that shifted over to proprietary products? (Related: Industry News: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Fa...)

Booth: Well, it’s two parts, because we are a national division, but we primarily do reverse mortgages. We don’t have a shop with our title and settlement licenses where we do forward business with any regularity. We’re part of a bigger conglomerate and we have sister companies where, if it’s a forward home purchase, we’ll ask one of our sister companies to take that on.

As far as the numbers go, it’s a little slower than normal. Maybe the past three years have been not so great for us on the reverse side as well, but it’s not as slow as it is for some of our sister companies on the forward side. There’s some concern just because it’s that time of the year where we ebb and flow. But we are every bit as busy closing loans. We have difficult files and they’re pushing through those every day.

Pierson: Benutech recently published a report about mismatches between reverse mortgage servicing records and title records. How do you avoid these situations? Do servicers need to be more proactive or have good business partners to catch these errors?

Booth: With a reverse mortgage, not all the time, but many times, it could be the last loan [on the property]. So whether there’s a trust, whether a power of attorney was used, whatever that vesting looks like, that would have all been approved when that person got the loan. If something’s going to change, that borrower should be consulting or going to their servicer before they’re making any changes. But that doesn’t happen.

Industry observers note that we’ll be midway through a transaction. We put a date down on a file to get ready for closing and find that the borrower has recorded another deed or did something they weren’t supposed to do. If they’re early on in the process, maybe they’re changing their vesting and they’re taking it out of the trust or they’re putting it in a trust. They’ve added someone to the title, not realizing that, no, you can’t do that.

We run into problems with that when we’re trying to clear a file, because now there’s deceased parties, or this borrower and their family member don’t talk anymore, they’re not going to sign off, or they want money to sign off. I think it’s just a matter of borrowers sometimes doing their own thing. They might get misguided information from an attorney — not that their attorney is trying to intentionally give them the wrong information, but they’re not looking at it through a title lens.

I’m not going to bash title companies; there’s a reality there. Some companies have more resources and have more of a footprint in the space. Some of them are using AI tools — they’re not actually using as many humans to do the job. Yes, AI is helpful and it’s effective, but you still need a person to make sure these records are being checked.

According to recent reports, we’ve caught quite a few things within our searches. We’re in 45 states, so sometimes we’re dealing with an abstract, or sometimes we’re dealing with our in-house title department, and there’s discrepancies we have to address. Our lenders don’t always like it. They’re like, “Why are we dealing this from 20 years ago?” We’re like, “There’s something missing and we’re not finding it. We need more information to lock that in.” We are a very conservative title company, so we catch those things and we address them.

Sources indicate that there are some title companies who will write past that. And that’s how you end up down the line where there’s a foreclosure, the heirs are trying to take the property back or refinance it, and there are clouds because the title manufacturer may have taken something on as a risk that the lenders love in the moment. But they don’t love it when they’re trying to get that property off their books and they can’t because there’s some type of a vesting issue or ownership issue that they can’t get past, outside of taking it to court.

Pierson: Panelists who spoke at NRMLA Annual indicated lingering reputational concerns with the reverse mortgage product. Past celebrity marketing campaigns were referenced as something to possibly try again. From your perspective, what needs to be done to put the right messaging out there?

Booth: The biggest conversations I’ve been having are around information. It might be the loan officer who’s not fully trained and doesn’t fully understand the product to sell it, or it’s someone who fully understands the product and can sell it, but they don’t have an audience to connect with and get that message to.

I won’t say we should dumb it down, but we should break down that information so people can absorb it, retain it and actually use it moving forward. We just have to continue to inform people and make sure they know the right things — and correct them when it’s the wrong thing.

It has been revealed that I don’t know if the reputation will ever go away. But I do know what won’t make it go away is if people are not doing these loans for the right reasons, and we have more borrowers that maybe have a bad experience — even if it’s nowhere near what happened in the late 1990s, early 2000s. I think that should be the goal: The lender, the broker, the title firm, even the notary should be trying to make sure that the reverse mortgage experience is a good one.

It has been revealed that having the Reverse Mastermind Summit being a presence and growing is great. I think that people outside of NRMLA are doing things, taking reverse knowledge to other conventions, boards and groups to just get those conversations going. Start talking about reverse mortgages in a positive light. Educate people who are curious. I just did a conference with the National Association of Default Professionals. I’d never heard of it before. It’s REO. They’re interested in finding more information on reverse mortgages: “How can we figure out what to do when we run into a property where someone has passed away?”.

Industry observers note that pierson: Let’s discuss reverse for purchase deals, which were a focus at NRMLA Annual. They could be poised to take off as more real estate agents gain awareness. From a title and settlement services perspective, are you able to do them on the timelines that lenders want?

According to recent reports, booth: With lenders, I say we are family. They’re like our cousins and we don’t always use the same lingo within the family. A lot of what lenders are saying when they use that 10- to 15-day closing number, they’re not factoring in the onset — the contract being done and everything that goes into people looking and buying. That’s not a 10- to 15-day thing. But once a file is actually clear to close, that time frame is absolutely attainable.

Recent findings suggest that we actually don’t take on many purchase transactions, again because we focus primarily on the first-mortgage refinance. We do proprietary loans as well, but those sister companies where we have people who are doing purchases regularly, we would much rather them take on those deals than us do something, because we don’t ever want to cause an issue. Maybe there’s a upcoming form or a new procedure that we’re not privy to because we don’t do them every day. I do think there’s always going to be a reasonable time to get those done as long as everyone’s involved, everyone’s informed, everyone’s communicating.

Pierson: Training and education are ongoing topics of interest across housing. Most companies have employees who are aging out and need to identify fresh blood to replace them. What is the title and settlement industry doing on this front?

Industry observers note that booth: A lot of it comes from who you know. On the title and settlement positions that we put out there, it’s kind of like, ‘Hey, I don’t necessarily know what this thing is, but I’m going to try it.’ And we gauge whether you’re going to pick up this or you’re not, because it’s hard to explain.

People can go to school to get a real estate license. You can go get trained to be a Realtor or a loan originator. But there’s really no training for title and settlement work, so you’re looking at skill sets. You’re looking at character. You’re looking at people who are able to have retention and you just give them a try. That’s what happened to me — someone took a chance on me.

In a recent development, in the role I’m in now, and looking toward the future, we’re looking at college campuses, college fairs, or finding interns at the high school level. Not every kid is going to college, and if you can help them out while they’re figuring it out, they might pick up something that can turn into a career for them. We’re thinking about younger blood, younger people, who maybe aren’t tainted and haven’t learned things the wrong way. We can get them early before they’ve got all these different ideas and teach them the right way, at least in our operation.

We do cross-training quite a bit in our operation, to make sure everyone is learning the pieces of the puzzle. Sometimes you might have a group or a team who only do this one thing, and they know nothing about this other part and how it comes together. We’re trying to help people understand how the bigger process works. But that takes time.
