Every young person with a dream for Hollywood has certainly pretended what it might feel like to win an Academy Award. They’ve held a hairbrush as a microphone, a shampoo bottle in hand, practicing their speech in front of the bathroom mirror. Many of those speeches come from those they have watched win on their TV. Whether it was an expected win or a massive shock, many inspiring victories have shaped generations that followed. We’re here to celebrate ten.

From groundbreaking firsts to sweeps that reshaped the landscape of the awards and movies at large, these ten inspiring wins have stayed with us long beyond their speeches. While many other groundbreaking Academy Award wins changed Hollywood, these ten represent a well-rounded mix of categories, eras, and legacies. Whose Oscar win made the greatest impact on you? The history and legacy of songs in films leave a very different impression than the movies they came from. Not only can we enjoy them on their own, but they also bring us back to that moment when we heard them on the silver screen. For a long time, the Best Original Song winners maintained a specific essence and style. Then the 21st Century came and the times had changed. Once Eminem won for “Lose Yourself,” the door was open for Three 6 Mafia to win for “It’s Hard Out Here for a Pimp.” Winning for the film Hustle & Flow, the song emerged victorious as it captured the grit, struggle, and ambition of the film's main character, DJay (Terrence Howard), in a way that resonated emotionally. According to recent reports, the hip-hop group’s victory didn’t come as a shock for defeating Dolly Parton, but because of how it proved that independent, underground Southern hip-hop artists could reach mainstream Hollywood success without compromising their roots. Coming out of Memphis, Three 6 Mafia was an unlikely contender in a traditional award show setting, making their victory feel like a win for "outsiders.” They became the first hip-hop group to win an Academy Award and perform at the ceremony, making the night’s milestone even sweeter. Members Juicy J and DJ Paul didn't expect to win and had no prepared acceptance speech, but their enthusiasm reverberated through the room.

Recent findings suggest that if there’s one thing that Bob Fosse is, it’s specific. Though he had been in the director’s seat in the past, his greatest cinematic triumph came through in his semi-autobiographical film All That Jazz. The legendary choreographer had a vision, and editor Alan Heim delivered. Though the Oscar went solely to Heim, Fosse collaborated quite closely with him in the cutting room. Heim and Fosse shaped the film with a driving, musical pace, blending theatrical choreography, rehearsal footage, and surreal sequences into a cohesive pulse. As Roy Scheider’s Joe Gideon nears physical collapse, the editing shifts fluidly between gritty backstage reality, hospital scenes, and elaborate musical fantasies.

It has been revealed that building on techniques discovered while editing Lenny, Heim moved fluidly across timelines and wove fantasy and dream logic into a straight narrative. This mirrors a mind under intense physical and creative strain. Techniques like cutting slightly off the beat or overlapping dialogue keep momentum high without feeling mechanical. Heim described moving time around and collaborating with Fosse as a creative adventure. Heim’s victory was a creative win. His win came against tough competition, beating heavy hitters including Apocalypse Now and Kramer vs. Kramer. The wildcard win highlighted how complex, long-gestating post-production can reward visual and rhythmic craftsmanship. Heim’s artistry is a benchmark of ambitious editing.

Industry observers note that sometimes playing yourself, or at least a version of it, can lead to massive success. For Diane Keaton, playing Annie Hall came as old hat, as Woody Allen modeled the titular character around her, name and all. Incorporating her natural mannerisms, humor, and tomboyish style was all it took as the Academy voters praised her fresh, self-deprecating comedic timing and nuanced portrayal of an awkward yet intelligent woman. Rather than playing a one-note "ditzy" character, Keaton balanced nervous tics and awkwardness with genuine intelligence and vulnerability. Her vintage menswear wardrobe of vests, ties, baggy trousers, and hats helped turn her into a defining late-1970s style icon.

Keaton’s victory for Annie Hall broke traditional conventions, celebrating individuality over traditional Hollywood glamour. Her candid, low-key acceptance moment stood out against overly scripted awards-show polish, making the win feel genuine and approachable. By redefining the leading lady through her achingly real presentation, Keaton influenced generations of performers and viewers alike. Sharp-witted, neurotic, and self-deprecating personas can be as equally charming as the formally polished presentation of a romantic damsel.

Before Get Out, most audiences knew Jordan Peele from his sketch comedy days. Then his directorial debut rattled cinema as a genuine Hollywood player. The film was a massive success, largely because of Peele’s groundbreaking original screenplay. Peele was awarded for writing an original horror-satire script that blended suspense with sharp social commentary on modern Black experiences. He combined psychological horror and satire into what he called a "social thriller," addressing themes underrepresented in mainstream horror cinema. Peele’s victory was monumental because he became the first Black writer to win the category. Only three other creators of color had been nominated in the category before him: Suzanne de Passé, Spike Lee, and John Singleton.

According to recent reports, shot in 23 days on a $4.5 million budget, Get Out proved that tight constraints and unconventional premises could yield major critical and commercial success. Peele framed the moment as part of a wider renaissance in Black filmmaking, encouraging young creators to claim their voices and tell stories that challenge industry norms. Peele shared in his acceptance speech that he stopped writing the script about 20 times because he doubted it would ever get made, yet kept returning to it because he believed people needed to hear and see the story. That part alone reminds anyone watching, no matter their craft, of the power of resilience. Coming from a low-budget debut by a writer-director best known for sketch comedy, Peele broke long-standing Oscar barriers for horror films and Black screenwriters. Underdogs rise up!​​​​​​​.

It has been revealed that when we examine the top-tier actors in cinema history, that list almost certainly includes Marlon Brando. His style of acting was on full display in On the Waterfront, and he was rewarded for it. He won the Academy Award because of his groundbreaking, naturalistic method-acting performance as dockworker Terry Malloy. His approach stood out as both intense and lifelike compared to traditional theatrical screen acting. The win marked Brando's fourth consecutive Best Actor nomination, signaling that Academy voters were ready to reward his sustained impact.

Recent findings suggest that brando’s performance paired working-class grit with quiet pain and moral awakening, most famously in the backseat cab scene in which he uttered, “I could have been a contender.” Brando was in quite a tough category, besting Humphrey Bogart, Bing Crosby, James Mason, and Dan O'Herlihy. Brando’s inspirational win validated emotional vulnerability beneath a tough exterior, influencing future elite actors including Daniel Day-Lewis and Joaquin Phoenix. Though Brando’s victory did not come as a shock, it was part of the film’s eight-award sweep that night. ​​​​​​​.

Sources indicate that stanley Kubrick’s imprint on cinema is quite profound. Though his career includes many monumental moments, his victory alongside John Alcott for Best Cinematography is the most inspiring. Winning for the film Barry Lyndon, the victory came from its groundbreaking low-light filming techniques and a painterly visual style that recreated an 18th-century world without electric light. Capturing enough light in near darkness, Alcott and Kubrick used ultra-fast Zeiss Planar 50mm f/0.7 lenses originally created for NASA low-light photography. Exterior scenes relied on natural daylight and careful light control rather than heavy artificial setups.

The victory inspired future filmmakers because it proved authentic, period-accurate visuals could be achieved through daring technical problem-solving rather than standard Hollywood shortcuts. Principal photography took roughly 300 days across multiple countries, highlighting a close working relationship between a demanding director and a cinematographer willing to go the distance to achieve greatness. The win wasn’t undeserved, but rather shocking due to the overall sentiment regarding the slower, 3-hour period piece. While it competed against stiff competition like Jaws and One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest in other categories, there was no denying the inspiring influence of artistic creativity and ingenuity on the film’s cinematography.​​​​​​​.

Firsts always have a massive impact, but Halle Berry’s monumental win for Best Actress was huge. For her role as Leticia Musgrove in Monster’s Ball, Berry was the first Black woman to win in that category. The win came to some as a shock, as the Golden Globes tend to be a predictor for the Oscars. She lost to Sissy Spacek for In the Bedroom. She won against a stacked lineup that included Dame Judi Dench for Iris, Nicole Kidman for Moulin Rouge!, Spacek for In the Bedroom, and Renée Zellweger for Bridget Jones's Diary.

Recent findings suggest that in her acceptance speech, she dedicated the moment to "every nameless, faceless woman of color" and honored trailblazers and peers who shaped her path. She took on a demanding, low-paying dramatic part despite warnings about its difficult and explicit material. The role itself drew intense discussion around portrayals of Black women and female sexuality, but even with her win, Hollywood's casting habits did not fundamentally shift overnight. That same ceremony saw Denzel Washington win Best Actor, marking the only time both top acting Oscars went to performers of color in the same year. ​​​​​​​.

Foreign language films rarely earned mainstream representation in the Best Picture category. When they do, it’s a formality and almost like a participation trophy. But then, one profound film walked away with the top honor: Parasite. Directed by Bong Joon Ho, the film earned the award because of its universal themes of class inequality, genre-blending craftsmanship, strong word-of-mouth among voters, and late-season awards momentum. It became the first non-English-language film to win Best Picture. Voters praised its tight structure, foreshadowing, ensemble acting, and seamless shifts between comedy, thriller, and drama.

Recent findings suggest that bong's win, echoing the idea that the most personal is the most creative, highlighted the power of distinct, auteur-driven storytelling. The win revealed that non-English films and international creators can reach the highest level of mainstream awards recognition rather than be confined to international categories. It broadened global representation and encouraged viewers to explore cinema beyond Hollywood. While Sam Mendes’ 1917 entered the season as the clear frontrunner, the director’s charismatic campaigning, strong backing, and enthusiastic recommendations from voters helped overcome the traditional subtitle barrier. Now we celebrate foreign-language films as genuine contenders each year. ​​​​​​​.

It has been revealed that sequels rarely get a chance for Oscar glory, let alone the third chapter of a franchise. Then The Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King proved the naysayers completely wrong. Not only did the Peter Jackson adaptation have a good night at the 2004 Academy Awards, but it also swept all 11 of its Oscar nominations! Because The Fellowship of the Ring and The Two Towers had earned major nominations and technical wins without capturing Best Picture or Best Director, voters treated the finale as a cumulative reward for the entire trilogy, backed by a massive studio campaign that overcame longtime Academy bias against fantasy films. The Return of the King tied the all-time Oscar record held by Ben-Hur and Titanic.

Recent findings suggest that shooting an entire trilogy back-to-back over years was an unprecedented risk that reaped a beneficial reward. Winning celebrated a story rooted in courage, hope, friendship, and enduring human effort during a turbulent era. Fantasy had historically struggled to win top honors at the Oscars, making the clean sweep a watershed moment that helped break that barrier. What may come as a shock is that the massive achievement came even though none of the cast members received an acting nomination. ​​​​​​​.

Horror rarely received a seat at the table. Then The Silence of the Lambs not only received an invitation, it was the true guest of honor. The film earned the rare honor of winning the “Big Five”—Best Picture, Best Director, Best Actor, Best Actress, and Best Adapted Screenplay. It became the third film in Oscar history to win all five major categories, joining It Happened One Night and One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest. Jonathan Demme's direction emphasized psychological tension and character empathy over constant gore, working from Ted Tally's adaptation of Thomas Harris' novel. Jodie Foster as FBI trainee Clarice Starling and Anthony Hopkins, with roughly 16 minutes of screen time, as Hannibal Lecter, anchored the story with intense, memorable acting.

Defying the odds, its dark themes, cannibalism, and graphic violence felt far removed from traditional prestige Oscar fare. The Silence of the Lambs proved a dark thriller film could earn top-tier prestige rather than being dismissed as mere genre entertainment. Another shocker in its victory was its time of release. Coming out in February 1991, it won 13 months later—showing a film that did not need a late-year release to stay in the Oscar conversation. Though it faced off against massive contenders like Beauty and the Beast and faced protests outside the ceremony over its portrayal of killer Jame Gumb, controversy and competition were nothing for the massive hit.
