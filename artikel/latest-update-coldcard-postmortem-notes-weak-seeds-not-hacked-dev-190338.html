Coinkite on Tuesday published its full postmortem on the Coldcard seed-generation flaw that let thieves drain bitcoin from customer wallets starting in July, saying no device was hacked and that the stolen keys were rebuilt offline from weak seeds.

Industry observers note that the 18-page report, dated Nov. 23 and modeled on a National Transportation Safety Board accident report, lays out how the defect shipped in Coldcard firmware from March 29, 2021, through July 30, 2026, and why years of outside reviews missed it. “The losses are real and, for some customers, life-changing. No report or firmware enhancement can undo that. We are sorry,” Coinkite wrote. (Related: How we sped up transformer inference 100x for 🤗 API cus...)

Industry observers note that A seed is the list of recovery words that controls a bitcoin wallet, and it is only as safe as the randomness used to create it. Coldcard devices were supposed to draw that randomness from the hardware random number generator built into their STM32 chip.

Instead, a firmware setting switched that hardware source off, and a safeguard in Coinkite’s libNgU code library failed to catch it. Seed generation quietly fell back to Yasmarang, a predictable software generator built into MicroPython, the programming environment the firmware runs on.

Industry observers note that nothing crashed, and the devices produced normal-looking seed words. Coinkite estimates affected seeds carried roughly 40 bits of effective security on the older Mk2 and Mk3 models and about 72 bits on the Mk4, Mk5 and Q, against an intended 128 bits. That shrinks the pool of possible seeds enough for attackers to search it offline.

Recent findings suggest that the report indicates Coinkite’s release checks confirmed the hardware code was present, but never confirmed that seed generation actually reached it. At least 15 independent review efforts since 2021 also missed the flaw, including two paid third-party reviews and an AI-assisted firmware review this year that produced 85 candidate findings.

It has been revealed that unexpected transactions from affected seeds began July 29. Coinkite stated it identified the defect the next day, after people discovered thefts, and shipped fixed firmware for every supported release track on July 31.

As of Nov. 18, Coinkite had received about 300 loss-related support tickets tied to 242 customer accounts, or about 0.21% of its estimated Coldcard user base. The firm’s blog post cites 236 accounts. Because Coldcard is self-custodial, Coinkite can only count losses that customers report, and it notes the true number is likely higher.

In a recent development, the report cites Galaxy Research figures showing 1,778.84 BTC stolen from more than 8,600 addresses, worth about $107 million at today’s price. Galaxy pegged losses above $115 million in August, based on prices when the coins were taken, and white hats later moved some exposed coins into a recovery trust.

Who found and exploited the flaw is still unknown, and law enforcement is investigating. The report calls AI-assisted discovery “a plausible hypothesis, not an attribution,” noting that the first thefts came two days after the full model weights of the AI model Kimi K3 were released. Bitcoin Core developers ended long embargoes last month for bugs that AI tools can easily rediscover.

According to recent reports, coinkite remarked an independent review by Keylabs, published Oct. 15, found the defect “fully closed” in Mk5 firmware 5.6.2 and 5.6.3 and Q firmware 1.5.2Q and 1.5.3Q. That review did not cover Coldcard’s Edge releases, a preview track for developers and early adopters.

According to recent reports, newer Coldcard firmware now requires users to add their own randomness, through key presses, dice rolls or coin flips, and mixes it with three hardware sources. The device also checks at startup that seed generation reaches the hardware generator, and stops if it does not.

But a firmware update does not repair a seed that was already created on affected firmware. Coinkite told owners whose seeds were made on affected firmware to install verified fixed firmware, create a fresh seed and move their coins. Seeds made with at least 50 private dice rolls were not weakened.

Recent findings suggest that three of the report’s 15 recommendations to Coinkite remain open, including whether to compensate affected customers. The company said it has “no decision” on a blueprint yet and asked whoever holds the stolen bitcoin to return it through [email protected].
