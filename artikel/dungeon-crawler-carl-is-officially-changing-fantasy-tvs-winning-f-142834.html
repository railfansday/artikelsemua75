Industry observers note that fantasy is a major staple for television, whether it's Xena: Warrior Princess leading a wave of syndicated action/adventure reveals in the 90s or Game of Thrones spearheading the "Peak TV" era. Most streaming services also have one big fantasy series in their libraries—see Percy Jackson and the Olympians on Disney+ or The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power on Prime Video. Peacock is about to get in on the fantasy genre with its upcoming adaptation of a beloved book series, and it might revolutionize the genre by bringing a different flavor of fantasy to the table.

Industry observers note that that series is Dungeon Crawler Carl. Peacock is turning Matt Dimmiman's highly popular novels into a television series, with Seth MacFarlane (Family Guy, Ted) serving as an executive producer and a writing staff that includes Christopher Yost (The Avengers: Earth's Mightiest Heroes) and Eric Heisserer (Arrival). Dungeon Crawler Carl is also the first television adaptation of a 'LitRPG' book, meaning that if the show is as successful as the novels that inspired it, it'll pave the path for a upcoming wave of fantasy indicates. The 'LitRPG' subgenre of fiction usually takes a character into a world that has similar mechanics to a roleplaying game, like Dungeons & Dragons, or a video game like Final Fantasy. A LitRPG protagonist often finds themselves "leveling up" as the story progresses, gaining powerful weaponry or skills that help them on their journey. Dungeon Crawler Carl takes this approach, as former Coast Guard member Carl and his ex-girlfriend's cat, Princess Donut, find themselves trapped in a World Dungeon that has enveloped Earth. To survive, Carl and Donut fight their way through the dungeon's levels while their exploits are televised across the galaxy. (Related: My Journey to a serverless transformers pipeline on Goo...)

In a recent development, dungeon Crawler Carl might not be the first LitRPG book, but it arguably set a high standard for the genre. Most LitRPG's, up to that point, fell into the same trap as isekai anime: they were less a coherent narrative and more of a power fantasy for the author. Matt Dimmiman, on the other hand, has struck a strange balance of dark humor and meta commentary on the lengths some people will go to for entertainment— a mix he's admitted is tough to keep up.

"I never intended for this to be a funny story, but the concept along with some of the characters—specifically Princess Donut (who is given sapience and the ability to talk in an early chapter)—allows for some really bizarre and ultimately funny scenarios...I try to rely on the characters themselves to get from point A to B, and humor is oftentimes a major coping mechanism. Plus, they are on a game show.".

By sticking to LitRPG mechanics, Dungeon Crawler Carl has a perfect way to hook audiences. The gaming mechanics will appeal to video game players, while the dark humor and action will keep everyone else along for the ride. Each book in the Dungeon Crawler Carl series could also map to a season of television, and with eight books published to date, that means Peacock has a series on its hands that could last a long time.

Four worlds and four completely different ideas of what a good life looks like. This is where you belong, what it would ask of you, and what you would get back in return.

It has been revealed that you belong somewhere old enough that the mountains have names and somebody can still tell you who gave them. Middle-earth is for people who think the right thing is worth doing even when it will not be finished in their lifetime.

Recent findings suggest that you want the sea, a crew, and absolutely nobody telling you where you are allowed to go. The Grand Line is the world for people whose main ambition in life is finding out what is over there.

In a recent development, you would rather be feared and useful than liked and useless. The Continent is a world where the monsters are the straightforward part of the job and the people are where it all gets complicated.

You want the myths to be real and loose in the actual world, and you want to find out at about thirteen that you were never anywhere near as ordinary as everybody kept telling you.

The success of Dungeon Crawler Carl's led to an outpouring of LitRPG books. He Who Fights with Monsters thrusts office worker Jason Asano into a realm full of monsters, forcing him to fight for his life while dealing with the sinister drawback of his abilities. Defiance of the Fall chronicles the aftermath of Earth being pulled into the middle of the multiverse, as a teenager must survive with only a hatchet and his wits. These books feature world-building as rich as Dungeon Crawler Carl, but with unique takes on the LitRPG genre that could help them stand out if they ever get a television adaptation.

Recent findings suggest that while Dungeon Crawler Carl has become a pop culture phenomenon in its own right, its success has unlocked the next big wave of fantasy stories. How that impacts the television landscape is yet to be seen.
