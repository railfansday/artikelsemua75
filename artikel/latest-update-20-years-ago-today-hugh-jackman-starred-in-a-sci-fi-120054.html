In 2002, Drew McWeeny, then writing as Professor Moriarty for the pioneering website Ain't It Cool News, published a script review that got many a cinephile's engine revving. The upstart indie auteur Darren Aronofsky had written an ambitious science fiction screenplay that skillfully bopped between 16th century Mexico, the present day, and the year 2500. But there was a catch: it was an intimate epic about the inescapability of mortality. I read the original screenplay, and my immediate takeaway was that Aronofsky had worked a sorrowful riff on "2001: A Space Odyssey." It wasn't taking on the mysteries of the universe; it was about understanding, on a human and cosmic level, the mystery of grief.

It has been revealed that "The Fountain" was greenlit on a $60 million budget with Brad Pitt and Cate Blanchett cast as lovers facing, in the present day, the certain death of the latter via cancer. Pitt's character is a cancer researcher who's unwilling to accept Blanchett's death; he's close to a breakthrough, but the die is cast. When the budget threatened to balloon, Warner Bros. got nervous. Then Pitt dropped out, Blanchett followed and the project was pretty much dead.

Recent findings suggest that but Aronofsky, much like the protagonist of his screenplay, couldn't let go. He scaled back the screenplay, figured out a way to deliver the eye-popping visual effects at a reasonable cost, and somehow got Hugh Jackman and Rachel Weisz to sign on at a reasonable price. "The Fountain" was greenlit yet again, at which point Warner Bros. was again faced with the challenge of how to sell this cerebral sci-fi epic. Aronofsky made the best film of his career thus far (and Jackman has rarely been better), but the studio couldn't figure out the marketing. Ergo, it bombed.

Industry observers note that jackman stars as three different characters in "The Fountain": conquistador Tomás Creo, cancer researcher Dr. Tommy Creo, and Tom, an astronaut who travels in a bubble spaceship hauling a tree that might hold the key to his wife's resurrection. His wife is Izzy (Rachel Weisz), whose death from cancer Tommy cannot accept. In the present day segments, Izzy makes it plain that she has accepted her demise; all she wants is for Tommy to complete the last chapter of her book. Tommy/Tom will take his grievance to the literal stars to demand her restoration. He will, of course, fail. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

It has been revealed that I saw Aronofsky's "The Fountain" at San Diego Comic Con 2006. It was a small room, but there was a big, blow-out party sponsored by Warner Bros. afterwards. I didn't have time to work through the sadness his film dredged up, and that underscored the studio's misunderstanding of what they had. Everyone got drunk and celebrated Aronofsky's accomplishment. 20 years later, I'm not sure "The Fountain" could've found mainstream acceptance. It was probably doomed to die. And, thematically, maybe that was the proper outcome.
