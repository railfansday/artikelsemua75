An unseen planet past Neptune may be in Earths weight class

Math points to our solar system having another planet in Earth's weight class far beyond Neptune, tugging distant icy bodies onto strange orbits, a newly introduced study proposes.

That would make it far smaller than Planet Nine, a hypothesized giant world five to 10 times Earth's mass that has drawn most of the attention in the hunt for an undiscovered planet. No one has spotted this suspected world, nicknamed Planet X2, but these researchers argue it would explain the strangest orbits in the outer solar system better. Sources indicate that every search for a missing planet has started the same way, with something in space that doesn't add up. In the 1800s, astronomers noticed Uranus wasn't staying on its predicted path, and the culprit turned out to be Neptune. Percival Lowell thought Uranus was still misbehaving and went looking for a "Planet X," a search that led to Pluto in 1930. But Pluto proved far too small to explain the trouble. Today, clues come from farther out, in a growing collection of frozen worlds beyond Neptune that move in ways the known planets can't explain. To many astronomers, that hints that something is still out there — maybe one planet, maybe more.

Beyond Neptune, a vast spill of icy leftovers from the solar system's birth circles the sun. Most of these objects travel in the Kuiper Belt, a broad band 38 to 50 times as far from the sun as Earth. Over the past 20 years, astronomers have found more and more of these objects on steeply tilted or stretched-out orbits.

In the latest study, the researchers built a computer model of the young solar system. It included the four giant planets, thousands of small icy bodies, and 1,500 Pluto-size worlds. Then, the computer worked out how each body tugged on the others, step by step, across 4.5 billion years. When the team added Planet X2, up to twice Earth's mass, its gravity pulled some icy objects out of Neptune's reach and tipped others onto steep angles. Together, those effects reproduced the orbits of every known oddball, including Sedna, a distant world that swings far beyond the planets on a long, narrow loop. Models without the planet fell far short, according to the paper, published in the journal Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.

Every hidden-planet study relies on computer models, but this one seems to replay more of the solar system's history. Early on, Neptune slowly drifted outward from the sun. As it moved, its gravity flung some icy objects far out and trapped others in orbits that march in step with Neptune.

Lykawka's own 2023 model skipped that drift and didn't include the main Kuiper Belt, where most known icy objects travel. It showed that Planet X2 could create the odd distant orbits, but not whether the planet would also wreck the parts of the belt that Neptune built. In the new model, Planet X2 creates the strange distant orbits and leaves the rest of the belt much like what astronomers see today.

According to recent reports, A competing idea — that a few extra Mars- to Earth-size worlds formed among the giant planets and got flung around early on — fared worse in the team's model. The giant planets would have thrown those worlds out of the solar system within tens of millions of years — too fast to explain the strange orbits seen today.

According to recent reports, planet Nine faces problems of its own. Its backers proposed it after noticing that the orbits of a few distant icy objects seemed to point in roughly the same direction, as if something major had herded them into line. Other researchers have argued that the pattern may be a fluke, caused by having too few known objects and by telescopes watching only certain patches of sky. Several recently discovered distant objects follow orbits that don't fit what Planet Nine predicts, the authors say.

Industry observers note that in the researchers' best-fitting models, Planet X2 circles the sun at an average distance of about 33 billion to 46 billion miles. That's roughly 12 to 17 times as far out as Neptune, and nine to 13 times as far as Pluto. Even at its closest, the planet would stay nearly 19 billion miles from the sun — more than six times Neptune's distance. Its orbit would likely tilt only slightly from the path the other planets follow.

"If this planet exists, it could change how we understand the history of our solar system and how its most distant objects came to follow such unusual orbits," Lykawka said.

The researchers admit the model doesn't fix everything. For example, compared with what a major sky survey found, the simulations predicted too few objects in the larger orbits the team studied.

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory, which photographs the night sky again and again, could settle the debate, catching the planet as a faint, slowly moving dot. That's a tall order for something so far from the sun's light. Another possibility is that Rubin could find enough distant icy objects to show whether their orbits carry Planet X2's gravitational fingerprints.

According to recent reports, "We now have an opportunity to put this idea to the test," stated study co-author Jonti Horner, a professor at the University of Southern Queensland, in a statement. "As the Rubin Observatory surveys the sky, we will find new clues that help us understand the distant reaches of the Solar System — and whether another planet is still waiting to be discovered.".
