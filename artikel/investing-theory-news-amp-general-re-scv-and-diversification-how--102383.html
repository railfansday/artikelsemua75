They would not have described SCV as an asset class, but SCV stocks would have been among their individual stock picks. The concept of diversifiable risk wasn’t well understood then, and investors focused on individual stocks and their return potential, not classes of stocks with specific properties.Probably the classes that were considered then were defined using dividend yield.I am old enough to remember the booklets that S&P put out that had information on a lot of stocks but the information on each was limited. My broker at the full service brokerage gave me one once or twice. That was sometime in the 1990's probably earlier in the decade. I remember when the Red Chip Review came out in the 1990's, a newsletter for Small Cap stocks. It was a major deal as there was limited analyst coverage for Small Cap stocks back then.The point I am trying to make is that information on individual Small Cap stocks was limited in the 1990's, imagine what it would have been like in 1929. My guess is that back then there was very little research available for Small Cap Value stocks, that trading was limited, and that for institutions that most of these stocks would have been not investible. If it would have been difficult for institutions to invest in Small Cap Value stocks just imagine the challenges facing retail investors.Yes, the thinking on dividend yield was opposite that it is now, yields on stocks were higher than with bonds because stocks were riskier and needed the higher yields to compensate investors for the risk.
