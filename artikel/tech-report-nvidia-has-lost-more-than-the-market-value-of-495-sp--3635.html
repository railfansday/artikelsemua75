On Oct. 6, Nvidia (NVDA -3.30%) peaked at an intraday high of $243.37 per share, reaching a valuation of $5.78 trillion. The face of the artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure build-out holds a virtual monopoly in enterprise data centers with its several generations of graphics processing units (GPUs).

But it's been a bumpy ride over the last six weeks. Between its Oct. 6 intraday peak and the Nov. 17 close, Nvidia lost $2.03 trillion in valuation. In just six weeks, Nvidia lost more in market value than all but five S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.02%) companies... one of which is itself.

While Nvidia's historically inexpensive valuation and its jaw-dropping Data Center segment growth suggest its stock is a bargain, it's also possible that its recent downtrend is just beginning.

Let's not beat around the bush: the resignation of now-former CEO Jensen Huang following an admission of an affair with an alleged Chinese spy and allegations of extramarital relations with up to two employees has left Nvidia without an identity.

Although Nvidia has an extensive order backlog, superior GPUs, and its pricing power continues to be aided by GPU supply shortages, the firm's innovation schedule (bringing an advanced AI chip to market annually) and its circular financing deals are very much in question.

BREAKING: The US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has opened an investigation into Nvidia, $NVDA, amid the Jensen Huang situation, per WSJ. The focus of the investigation includes whether the tech giant’s disclosures misled investors. The Justice Department is also now reportedly leading multiple investigations involving Alice Huang.

Furthermore, Huang's actions and departure may have breached the trust of clients, consumers, and/or investors. Earlier this week, the Securities and Exchange Commission opened an investigation over whether the manufacturer misled investors in its disclosures.

Without trust, Nvidia becomes practically uninvestable.

Although long-term trends strongly suggest that AI will be a game changer and that the benchmark S&P 500 will head higher, historical precedent isn't Nvidia's friend over the short run.

Every next-substantial-thing technology since (and including) the advent and proliferation of the internet in the mid-1990s has navigated an early-innings bubble. These bubbles form because investors consistently overestimate the pace of adoption and optimization of hyped innovations. (Related: Deep Learning over the Internet: Training Language Mode...)

The good news for AI is that there isn't an adoption issue. Nvidia's quarterly results make it clear that businesses are freely spending to build out their data centers.

In a recent development, but it's an entirely different story when it comes to optimizing this technology. It took until well after the dot-com bubble burst before businesses figured out how to optimize internet-driven solutions to maximize sales and profits. As with the internet, AI will take time to mature, leaving Nvidia and its peers exposed to a possible bubble-bursting event. (Related: Breaking: How to generate text: using different decodin...)

Lastly, Nvidia's fiscal fourth-quarter gross margin is forecast to dip to 71%-72% from a reported 74.8% (based on generally accepted accounting principles, or GAAP) in the fiscal third quarter.

Although Nvidia's GAAP gross margin has pretty consistently hovered in the low-to-mid 70% range over the trailing three years, the company's fiscal fourth-quarter forecast must have some investors wondering if this is as good as it gets.

NVDA Gross Profit Margin (Quarterly) data by YCharts.

Recent findings suggest that this two- to three-percentage-point dip in GAAP gross margin reflects higher memory prices and the near-term ramp-up associated with Vera Rubin shipments. But if GPU supply picks up from its competition, as is expected, Nvidia's pricing power and GAAP gross margin would be expected to wane over time.

In other words, Nvidia may not be the slam-dunk bargain it appears to be.
