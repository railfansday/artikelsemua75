A mysterious twist in the universe’s oldest light could reveal fresh physics

It has been revealed that A mysterious twist in the universe's oldest light could offer clues to physics that scientists have yet to discover. But before researchers can determine whether this subtle signal reveals something extraordinary about the cosmos, they must answer a crucial question: Is the effect actually coming from the universe, or could it be an illusion created by the telescopes observing it?

The mystery centers on the cosmic microwave background (CMB), a faint glow of radiation left over from approximately 380,000 years after the Major Bang. This ancient light provides a remarkable snapshot of the "baby universe" as it existed nearly 13.8 billion years ago, allowing scientists to investigate conditions from the earliest stages of cosmic history. In a recent development, when the universe was about 380,000 years old, it had cooled enough to become transparent, allowing light to travel freely through space. As radiation scattered off free electrons during this transition, it acquired a small amount of linear polarization. Polarization describes the direction in which light's electric field oscillates, and a similar effect occurs when sunlight scatters through Earth's atmosphere.

Industry observers note that now, observations suggest that the polarization of this ancient radiation may have shifted slightly as the light traveled across the universe. This possible rotation, called cosmic birefringence, is especially intriguing because it could point to physical processes not explained by the Standard Model, the leading theory describing fundamental particles and their interactions. Confirming the effect might even provide latest insights into dark matter and dark energy (see Sidebar). (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

A Mysterious Cosmic Signal or a Telescope Error?

Recent findings suggest that there is a major obstacle to interpreting this potential discovery. An extremely small error in how a telescope's polarization detectors are aligned can produce a signal that looks almost identical to cosmic birefringence.

According to recent reports, to help address this problem, physicists at the University of California San Diego developed a new technique for checking how accurately different groups of detectors measure polarization angles. They tested their approach using existing observations collected by the European Space Agency's Planck satellite, which mapped the universe's ancient radiation in extraordinary detail.

According to recent reports, the research was led by Postdoctoral Fellow Anto I. Lonappan, Chancellor's Distinguished Professor of Physics Brian Keating, and Associate Professor of Physics Kam Arnold. Their findings were published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Separating Real Cosmic Effects From Instrument Errors.

It has been revealed that previous studies have suggested that the polarization of the CMB may have rotated by just a fraction of a degree over its nearly 14 billion years of travel. Although that sounds insignificant, such a tiny change could have enormous implications for fundamental physics.

Unfortunately, a telescope whose polarization detectors are misaligned by a similarly small angle could produce precisely the same apparent rotation. If every detector shares the same alignment error, the resulting measurements cannot distinguish that error from a genuine rotation of the cosmic signal.

According to recent reports, the UC San Diego researchers found a way to investigate the problem from another angle. Rather than relying on a single combined measurement, they compared separate polarization maps generated by different groups of detectors.

Industry observers note that if cosmic birefringence is real, its effect should appear consistently across all these maps. By comparing the maps with one another, the researchers can effectively eliminate any rotation shared by all of them. What remains reveals differences in the calibration of the individual detector groups, making it possible to identify inconsistencies that might otherwise complicate the search for fresh physics.

"The signal we are looking for is incredibly small, so we have to be certain that we are seeing the universe and not our instrument," said Lonappan. "Our method gives us a complementary way to check that distinction. Before interpreting a tiny rotation as upcoming physics, we want to know that the calibration itself can be trusted.".

Testing the Mystery With Planck Satellite Data.

According to recent reports, to evaluate their technique, the researchers analyzed eight polarization maps produced using Planck observations. They then compared their findings with results from an established approach called the Minami-Komatsu analysis, which is used to distinguish instrumental rotation from possible cosmic birefringence.

Industry observers note that although the two methods rely on different assumptions, they produced consistent results. This agreement provides an notable independent check on how relative detector calibration errors are being assessed.

Industry observers note that the new approach also has a deliberate limitation. Because it removes any rotation common to all the maps, it cannot independently measure the overall polarization angle or determine how much the universe itself may have rotated the light. Establishing that absolute rotation still requires an independent reference for calibrating the overall angle.

Recent findings suggest that to demonstrate how their findings fit with existing measurements, the researchers combined their reconstruction of relative calibration differences with the common calibration value inferred from the established analysis. This produced an estimated cosmic birefringence angle of 0.37 ± 0.12 degrees, matching the Minami-Komatsu result.

Importantly, this agreement does not independently confirm that the ancient light has actually rotated. Instead, it indicates that the new method can reproduce results from the existing framework while providing a separate way to examine potential calibration problems.

Searching for Gravitational Waves From the Early Universe.

The technique could also play an notable role in another major pursuit in cosmology: the search for gravitational waves generated during the universe's earliest moments.

To understand why, scientists distinguish between two patterns of polarization in the cosmic microwave background, known as E modes and B modes.

Industry observers note that E modes were first detected in 2002. They arise from density variations in the early universe and form symmetric polarization patterns. B modes have a different appearance, featuring patterns that resemble curls or swirls.

One known source of B modes is gravitational lensing, which occurs when the gravity of matter between Earth and the early universe bends and distorts the path of ancient light. Scientists have already observed B modes created through this process.

However, researchers are also searching for a different kind of B mode that could have been produced by primordial gravitational waves. These ripples in spacetime may have originated during an extremely early period of rapid cosmic expansion, known as inflation. The distinctive B mode signal associated with those primordial gravitational waves has not yet been detected.

Finding it would provide powerful evidence about gravitational waves in the early universe and could offer strong support for particular models of cosmic inflation, potentially transforming scientists' understanding of how the universe began.

Why Extremely Precise Measurements Matter.

The challenge is that even a slight error in a telescope's polarization calibration can cause some of the much stronger E-mode signal to appear incorrectly as B modes. This contamination could make an instrumental artifact look like an exciting cosmological discovery.

Industry observers note that the UC San Diego method could help prevent such mistakes by identifying differences in polarization angle calibration among detector groups. That makes it a potentially valuable tool for future experiments searching for the extremely faint signatures of primordial gravitational waves.

This kind of independent verification will become increasingly important as observatories develop the ability to measure cosmic polarization with greater precision. One example is the Simons Observatory, where Keating serves as principal investigator.

As these instruments become more sensitive, researchers will need equally rigorous methods for confirming that the faint signals they detect are genuine features of the universe rather than imperfections in their equipment.

"The detection of primordial B modes would transform our understanding of the early universe, so the measurement must survive rigorous calibration checks," said Keating. "Anto has developed a useful fresh framework for testing relative polarization-angle calibration. This is ultimately about knowing when we can trust a measurement.".

Beyond the Standard Model: Dark Matter and Dark Energy.

The possibility of cosmic birefringence is particularly fascinating because it could reveal physics that lies beyond the Standard Model.

Although this framework has been remarkably successful at describing fundamental particles and their interactions, it cannot explain everything in the universe. Among its most important unanswered questions are the identity of dark matter and the origin and observed magnitude of dark energy. Together, these mysterious components account for approximately 95% of the universe's total energy density.

Industry observers note that one possible explanation for cosmic birefringence involves a hypothetical field associated with particles resembling axions. Such a field could interact with light and gradually alter its polarization as it crosses the universe. However, these particles have not been confirmed to exist, and they would not necessarily be responsible for dark matter.

Other theoretical explanations connect the possible rotation to fields associated with dark energy, the mysterious phenomenon linked to the accelerating expansion of the universe.

If cosmic birefringence is eventually confirmed, it could provide an entirely new way to investigate these cosmic mysteries and reveal evidence of physical laws beyond those right now understood.

For now, the first challenge is ensuring that the subtle signal can be trusted. By giving researchers another way to identify potential measurement errors, the newly introduced technique strengthens the foundation for future discoveries that could reshape our picture of the universe.
