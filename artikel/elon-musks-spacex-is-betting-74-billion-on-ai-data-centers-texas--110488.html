Elon Musk’s SpaceX is betting $74 billion on AI data centers. Texas and Arizona want to know who pays for the power

🌐 Source: Fortune • 📅 22 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1168 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

SpaceX wants to build a data center in the Arizona desert that would draw 6,000 megawatts of power at full build-out. That is nearly two-thirds of the all-time peak demand that Arizona Public Service, which serves about 1.5 million homes and businesses, hit across its entire system this summer, according to KJZZ.

The Cochise County campus is the larger half of a plan SpaceX confirmed on Nov. 12 to spend more than $74 billion on two AI supercomputing sites: roughly $50 billion in southeastern Arizona and more than $24 billion at Starbase, Texas, Reuters reported. The organization is pitching both as projects that pay for themselves, with on-site power in Texas, dry cooling in Arizona, and promises of lower bills and taxes for neighbors. In a recent development, residents, local officials, and now Arizona’s attorney general want proof first. Who covers the cost of the power plants, water, and grid upgrades behind AI, the tech giant or the people who live next door, is rapidly becoming the question that decides whether these campuses get built. (Related: Show, Don't Certify: How to Prepare for Your First Data...)

The stakes for SpaceX are large. Its AI segment produced more than half of its third-quarter revenue, ahead of rockets and Starlink, according to Reuters, and Fortune has reported on how renting out computing capacity to Anthropic and Google has become central to the company’s valuation. The political ground is shifting under the industry, too: voters sided against data center development in 37 of 38 local ballot contests with complete counts this month, as Fortune reported after the midterms.

The Texas campus is a gas-fired power plant and “super-compute facility” on 444.45 acres near the Port of Brownsville, The Texas Tribune reported. Brownsville de-annexed the land earlier this year at SpaceX’s request, after the company offered $220 million for city water and wastewater projects. The neighboring city of Starbase, which incorporated around SpaceX’s launch site in 2025, then annexed it.

That sequence leaves the project outside the reach of Brownsville’s 90-day data center moratorium, which took effect in early November. The city says its decision was based on the “information and circumstances presented at the time.”.

According to recent reports, because the plant would generate its own electricity, SpaceX claims the facility won’t draw from the public grid. It will still burn a lot of gas. The Texas Commission on Environmental Quality issued an air permit on Nov. 16 that allows 35 natural gas turbines producing 750 megawatts and up to 248 tons of nitrogen oxides a year, Newsweek reported, citing Politico.

The South Texas Environmental Justice Network has revealed a protest, arguing the facilities would pollute the air, use up water, and raise residents’ energy bills. Other residents see opportunity. “There’s a lot of people that need jobs in our community and SpaceX has contributed to those jobs,” Janie Melendez, a Brownsville school teacher who previously served on the Texas Republican Party’s executive committee, told the Tribune.

According to recent reports, melendez has felt SpaceX’s pull in another way. “Since SpaceX moved in, my home has tripled in value,” she remarked. Rising values are a win for owners, but they tend to lift property tax bills and rents, which can squeeze newer residents and renters.

Industry observers note that the tax question is now live. On Nov. 17, Cameron County commissioners voted 3-2 against moving ahead with talks on a 10-year, 100% property tax abatement for the project. Starbase’s own city commission is scheduled to consider a tax abatement on Dec. 9. And in January, SpaceX will face a new governor, Gina Hinojosa, who has vowed to freeze data center approvals until lawmakers pass new protections.

The Arizona campus would sit on 747 acres near the Willcox Playa, about 80 miles east of Tucson, according to the Arizona Daily Star. It would take power from the grid through the Sulphur Springs Valley Electric Cooperative and the Arizona Electric Power Cooperative, and SpaceX indicates the nearby Apache Generating Station, which burns coal and natural gas, would grow from about 1 gigawatt to more than 6 gigawatts. The permit application lists $3.6 billion in planned grid upgrades.

According to recent reports, spaceX’s pitch to the county is aggressive. At a community meeting, Riley Trettle, who heads SpaceX’s energy and supercomputer department, said the project would reduce residents’ electricity bills, lower property taxes by 20%, and create about 1,000 permanent jobs, according to the Star. The application projects a one-time $250 million sales tax benefit for the county and $35 million to $103 million in sales tax revenue, and the tech giant expects about 5,500 construction jobs.

It has been revealed that water is the sharper worry. The campus would be dry-cooled and would not pump groundwater for industrial use, though an existing well could supply up to 10 acre-feet a year for drinking water and maintenance. Residents point to an aquifer already drawn down by large dairy farms, and experts told the Star that the water used to generate the campus’s power could raise annual data center water use by 60% to 90%. The permit doesn’t estimate that figure.

In a recent development, on Nov. 19, Attorney General Kris Mayes asked the Cochise County Planning and Zoning Commission to postpone its Dec. 9 vote until state and federal agencies can review the project. Her letter questions how SpaceX would cover power needs beyond what the regional cooperative can generate, and raises concerns about water, fire safety, traffic, wildlife, and nearby Fort Huachuca. “The people of Cochise County deserve answers before county officials approve a project of this magnitude,” Mayes said in a statement. She plans her own town hall at Cochise Elementary School on Dec. 3.

According to recent reports, arizona’s larger utilities are already wrestling with the same cost split. APS describes its approach as “growth pays for growth”: large customers sign long-term contracts so they, rather than residential and small commercial customers, cover the infrastructure they require, Scripps News reported. Data centers accounted for about 7% of APS’s peak load this year, up from about 5% last year, according to KJZZ.

Regulators draw a finer line. Arizona Corporation Commissioner Kevin Thompson indicates data centers will pay for any upcoming generation they require, but all customers should share reliability investments that benefit the whole grid. “If it’s for the betterment of the grid as a whole, then that peanut butter should be spread across the bread, right?” he told KJZZ.

Sources indicate that meanwhile, APS is asking the commission to approve a 14% to 16% rate increase for residential customers. In that case, an APS witness, Jamie Moe, acknowledged that residential demand isn’t causing high growth-related costs, KJZZ reported.

SpaceX’s Arizona campus would be served by cooperatives rather than APS, but any rate increase would still need the Corporation Commission’s approval. The Dec. 9 votes in Starbase and Cochise County will be the first public tests of whether SpaceX’s promise that its AI campuses will pay their own way holds up once the bills, and the gigawatts, come due.
