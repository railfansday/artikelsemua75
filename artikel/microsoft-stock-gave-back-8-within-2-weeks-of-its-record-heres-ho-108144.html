Microsoft (MSFT -2.77%) hit a record close of $555.80 on Nov. 4. Nine trading days later, on Nov. 17, it ended at $506.24 -- around 9% under the high. Shares trade near $508 as of this writing.

Part of that drop comes from the broader market. The Nasdaq-100 fell around 5% in the same stretch. But Microsoft fell more. (Related: Breaking: Illinois appeals Pacold injunction blocking i...)

Industry observers note that A fast drop from a new peak can feel like a warning. For Microsoft, it usually hasn't been one. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

By my count of its daily closes, the stock has fallen this fast from a record on 18 past occasions since 1995. Shares were higher 12 months later in 15 of the cases, and the typical (median) gain was around 30%. That's about twice the median one-year gain of about 15% for all trading days in the same stretch.

Sources indicate that every case begins on the first day Microsoft closed at least 8% under a record close set within the past 15 trading days. I used split-adjusted closing prices without dividends and counted just one case per record, so a stock that kept falling couldn't count twice until it hit a newly introduced high.

"A year later" means the close 12 months after that first 8% day.

The recent record is especially strong. Since 2018, Microsoft has done this nine times, and the stock was higher a year later in all nine. The middle result was a rise of around 29%.

The best one came in September 2020, when shares rose about 48% over the next year.

But the ride was rarely smooth. Ten of the 18 saw shares fall at least another 10% before the year ended.

Industry observers note that the latest case highlights how rough that can be. Once the stock slid 8% under its Oct. 28, 2025, record, it eventually closed around 29% below where it stood on that 8% day. It didn't hit a fresh record until Oct. 13, 2026. And a year after the pullback started, it was up only about 7%.

In a recent development, the three cases that finished lower fell within around nine months of each other, in April 1999, July 1999, and January 2000. A year later, shares were down each time, by up to about 50%.

According to recent reports, the last one was by far the worst. Microsoft set a record close on Dec. 27, 1999, and fell 8% under it in January 2000. It didn't close above its 1999 high again until October 2016 -- almost 17 years later.

Industry observers note that one substantial difference between then and now is valuation. In all three cases, the stock cost more than 60 times the earnings Microsoft had reported in the previous four quarters.

In a recent development, today, Microsoft's non-GAAP (adjusted) earnings for its past four quarters are $18.04 per share, a number that leaves out the impact of its investments in OpenAI. The stock trades at around 28 times those earnings.

True, the stock arguably still has a premium price for a tech business this substantial. But it's nowhere near the multiples investors paid then.

Of course, earnings growth has cooled a little. Adjusted earnings per share climbed 18% year over year in Microsoft's fiscal first quarter of 2027 (the period ended Sept. 30, 2026). That was its slowest rate in at least four quarters, after growth between 21% and 24% in the previous three quarters.

Industry observers note that what makes this case unusual is that Microsoft's Nov. 4 record came on a one-day jump of almost 7%. None of the 18 past records in this study came on a one-day gain larger than about 5%.

Recent findings suggest that so a lot of the latest drop has just given that jump back. Measured from its Nov. 3 close of around $521, the stock is down only about 2%. In other words, the drop from the record likely overstates how much has changed for Microsoft since early November.

NASDAQ · As of 4:00 PM ET 11/20/26.

All in all, the record here looks promising for long-term shareholders. A quick 8% slide from a high has usually been followed by gains, and the exceptions were when the stock was priced well above where it is now.

Industry observers note that but I don't see a reason to rush. More than half the time, buyers had a chance to pay at least 10% less at some point during the year.

And with earnings growth slowing and the stock still about 28 times adjusted earnings, I'd rather wait for a lower price than the one offered now.

If this pullback follows most of the others, that chance might come -- but history isn't a guarantee.
