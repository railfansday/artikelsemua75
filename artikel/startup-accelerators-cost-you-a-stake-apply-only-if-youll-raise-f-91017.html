Startup Accelerators Cost You a Stake – Apply Only if You’ll Raise From Investors

🌐 Source: Moneycrashers • 📅 22 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 2 min read (490 words, 📖 Standard)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, apply to a startup accelerator only if your business could grow fast enough to raise money from investors. In exchange for a slice of your company, it usually gives you seed money, mentors, and introductions to investors. If you’ll never sell a stake, get free advice from SCORE or a Small Business Development Center instead.

At the best-known programs, the seed money is no grant. Every dollar of it buys a piece of your company.

Y Combinator’s standard deal puts $500,000 into each startup it accepts. Of that, $125,000 buys a fixed 7% of your manufacturer, and Y Combinator invests the other $375,000 through a SAFE, a contract that turns into shares when investors later buy your stock at a set price. Techstars takes a fixed 5% for $20,000 and, in most programs, puts another $200,000 into the same kind of SAFE. Either way, the program’s stake grows past its fixed 7% or 5% once its SAFE turns into shares. (Related: Meta’s ‘secret’ Black Friday sale just started, and it ...)

Recent findings suggest that along with the cash, that stake gets you mentors and introductions to investors. Many programs end with a demo day, where you pitch your company to investors. That trade is worth making only if you’ll go on to raise money from angel investors or venture capital firms. A two-founder software startup that gives up a fixed 7%, then raises its first round from investors it met at demo day, gets what it paid for.

A business that won’t raise from investors should look for help that costs no stake. Universities, cities, and companies run incubators that can offer workspace and advice. Some are free, while others charge a fee or take a stake, so ask first. A bakery that rents low-cost kitchen space from a city incubator and gets coaching there keeps the whole business. SCORE’s mentors advise U.S. business owners for free, and Small Business Development Centers offer no-cost consulting. (Related: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons in a year whil...)

Industry observers note that for a startup that will raise money, judge a program by its people and its terms. Find out which investors come to its demo day and whether its mentors have built companies like yours. Read the terms of both the fixed stake and the SAFE, and check whether you must move, since Y Combinator’s three-month program meets in person in San Francisco.

Before you apply, ask each program what share of the companies in its last two classes raised money within a year of demo day.
