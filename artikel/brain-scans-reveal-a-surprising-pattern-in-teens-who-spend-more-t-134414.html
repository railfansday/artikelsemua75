Brain scans reveal a surprising pattern in teens who spend more time on screens

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A study of 44 Finnish teens linked more screen time to smaller gray matter volume in a memory region and larger volumes in some other areas.

Earlier studies on teenagers and screens have generally associated longer device use with reduced gray matter in the brain.

Gray matter is the brain tissue in which the bodies of nerve cells are concentrated.

That familiar pattern was also observed in a memory region by a team studying Finnish teens aged 16 to 19.

In regions involved in attention and planning, however, more gray matter was found among the heavier screen owners.

These results are difficult to interpret because gray matter volume typically decreases as the teenage brain matures.

The research was led by Hannamari Skog, a doctoral researcher at the University of Eastern Finland‘s Institute of Biomedicine. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

Her team scanned the brains of 44 teens on a single occasion each.

It has been revealed that the participants were recruited from the Physical Activity and Nutrition in Children study in Kuopio in Finland. They had taken part in that project for eight years.

A total of 277 teens attended the project’s eight-year checkup and 60 of them were invited to the brain study.

Those invitations were extended so that the full range of performance on a sprint test would be represented.

Sources indicate that forty-six teens agreed to participate. Two of them were later excluded when structural brain abnormalities were detected on their scans.

The final sample consisted of 25 girls and 19 boys who each underwent magnetic resonance imaging of the brain.

Specialized software estimated gray matter volume in 102 regions of the outer brain and in 10 deeper structures.

It has been revealed that screen use was assessed by questionnaire. Daily hours on phones, tablets, computers, TV and videos were reported by the teens themselves.

The reported average was 9.2 hours per day and about 5.2 of those hours involved phones and tablets.

Individual totals ranged between 2.5 and 19 hours and each figure rested on the teen’s own estimate.

The content viewed on the screens was not assessed by the questionnaire. Only the number of hours was recorded.

Fitness was measured with a standing long jump and a shuttle run that covered 164 feet (50 meters) in total.

Sources indicate that teens who reported more screen time had less gray matter in the medial temporal lobe. This inner part of the temporal lobe is involved in memory and learning.

The same link was found for time on phones and tablets alone. It appeared on both the left and right sides of the brain.

Age, sex, head size and body fat were taken into account. The links still held.

Recent findings suggest that in all, the team ran 72 statistical tests. With that many tests, a few links can appear by pure chance.

So a correction was applied to the results. The memory region links survived it.

Industry observers note that skog’s team had predicted this result. The temporal cortex matures late, they wrote, and is particularly sensitive to environmental influences.

For the authors, the finding raises concerns, because the region is implicated in memory disorders.

“Excessive screen time and smart device usage may be harmful to the areas of the brain that are involved in memory and learning,” Skog said.

The researchers had also expected teens with more screen time to have smaller frontal regions.

According to recent reports, teens with more screen time had larger volumes in two frontal regions. One helps the brain plan movements.

According to recent reports, the other is part of the prefrontal cortex. It supports working memory, planning and decision-making.

Sources indicate that total screen time was also tied to a larger area in the occipital lobe. That lobe processes vision.

Time on phones and tablets was linked to larger volumes in two more frontal regions.

“The larger volume in these areas may be particularly related to applications and games that require attention, motor planning and visual information processing,” Skog said.

Some researchers split screen time into active and passive kinds. Active use includes problem-solving or interactive play. Passive use means simply watching.

A 2019 study cited by the team made this kind of split. Gaming was linked to a larger prefrontal cortex in that work.

In a recent development, social media use was linked to a smaller temporal lobe. The Finnish questionnaire did not distinguish between time spent gaming and time spent on social media.

An earlier analysis from the same Finnish project had a related result. Some working memory scores were higher among teens with more screen time.

A larger region should not be assumed to be a better one. The prefrontal cortex is also one of the last regions to fully mature.

“During normal brain development in adolescence, there is a reduction in grey matter volume and an increase in brain efficiency,” Skog stated.

“The positive link between screen time and the volume of grey matter in these areas may thus also reflect a delay in brain development.”.

That idea was not tested in this study. Only one scan exists for each teen.

How each teen’s brain changed over time was therefore not recorded.

Larger temporal and frontal volumes had been expected in active and fit teens, but this pattern did not appear.

Sources indicate that the teens reported 92 minutes of physical activity a day on average. More activity was tied to a larger region in the right occipital lobe.

But that link was no longer statistically significant after the correction.

Fitter teens had a larger left parietal lobe. This region helps process sensation and attention.

Better fitness was also linked to smaller volumes in the right hippocampus and one other region.

Neither of those two links remained after the correction, and the authors wrote that both may be chance findings.

Part of the weak result may be explained by exercise intensity, which was not captured by the questionnaire.

The authors wrote that teenagers’ everyday activity may be too light to influence brain volume.

It has been revealed that each teen was scanned just once. So the study can’t show whether screen habits came before the brain differences or after.

The group of 44 was small and not typical of all teens. Most had a parent with a polytechnic or university degree.

No data on sleep or diet were collected. Either factor could explain part of the pattern.

Skog’s team collected the data in 2018 and 2019. Screen habits may have differed then from the habits seen today, the authors wrote.

The survey recorded screen time by device or broad category, but it did not break down how much time teens spent on specific activities.

Those hours could have been spent on games, videos, schoolwork or social media.

Screen-time apps could capture that detail, the authors wrote, and wearable sensors could measure how hard teens exercise.

“The results provide latest information on how everyday lifestyle factors, such as screen time, physical activity and physical fitness, are linked to brain structure in adolescence,” Skog stated.

“However, in light of the results, the connections between lifestyle factors and brain structures in adolescence are complex, and more research is needed to understand their long-term significance.”.

The authors called for larger studies that follow teenagers over years, to clarify whether different kinds of screen use cause differences in the brain.

The full study was published in the journal NeuroImage: Reports.
