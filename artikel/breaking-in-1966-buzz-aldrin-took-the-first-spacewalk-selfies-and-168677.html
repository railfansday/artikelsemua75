It has been revealed that sixty years ago, Buzz Aldrin took the first spacewalk selfies on Gemini XII. NASA says Gemini photos also helped make the case for satellites like Landsat.

Recent findings suggest that sixty years ago this month, Buzz Aldrin opened the hatch of his Gemini spacecraft, looked outside and exclaimed, “Man! Look at that!”.

Near the end of that spacewalk, on November 12, 1966, he mounted a camera on the edge of the hatch, pointed it at himself and took the first selfies ever taken during a spacewalk. NASA notes that it happened decades before the concept even existed.

Industry observers note that that flight, Gemini XII, is better known for something else. It finally demonstrated that astronauts could do useful work outside a spacecraft, a skill NASA needed before it could send anyone to the Moon. And a NASA image article published on November 13 points to a quieter legacy: photos like the ones Aldrin and his commander took from orbit helped inspire the program that led to the Landsat satellites.

Sources indicate that aldrin and command pilot James A. Lovell released on November 11, 1966, on a four-day mission. It was the final flight of NASA’s Gemini program, which had to prove the techniques that Apollo would need to land on the Moon.

Those techniques included rendezvous and docking, spacewalking, and showing that spacecraft and astronauts could function for at least eight days, the minimum for a round trip to the Moon.

On September 12, 1966, Aldrin became the first astronaut to train for his spacewalks underwater, using mockups of the Gemini and its target vehicle in a school swimming pool in Owings Mills, Maryland. Neutral buoyancy training, as it is called, is still in use today.

Gemini XII debuted about 90 minutes after an uncrewed Agena target vehicle. When the crew’s rendezvous radar proved erratic, Aldrin used backup methods and onboard calculations to guide them in.

Industry observers note that he had written his doctoral dissertation on orbital rendezvous. They docked on the third orbit.

A problem with the Agena’s engine stopped a planned boost to a higher orbit. Instead, Mission Control told the crew that a slight change would put them on the path of a solar eclipse the next morning.

On November 12, as they flew over the Galapagos Islands, the nearly total eclipse came into view, and Aldrin took several photographs and a short film clip.

Industry observers note that it was the first time a solar eclipse was photographed from Earth orbit, according to NASA. Gemini XII passed within 3.4 miles of the center of the eclipse’s path.

Recent findings suggest that later that day, Aldrin made his first spacewalk, a “standup” outing in which he stayed in the open hatch. He installed a handrail on the Agena for the next day, photographed stars in ultraviolet light and took his selfies. He spent two hours and 28 minutes outside.

The next day, tied to the spacecraft by a 30-foot umbilical, he moved along the handrail to the Agena and later worked at the back of the Gemini with his feet in restraints called “golden slippers.”.

Sources indicate that he completed 19 tasks in all, reporting no problems with keeping his body in position. At two hours and nine minutes, it was the longest umbilical spacewalk to that time.

According to recent reports, A third standup spacewalk brought his total to a then-record five hours and 48 minutes. Aldrin credited his underwater training and adequate restraint systems for his success.

Spacewalks have come a long way since then: in September 2024, the crew of SpaceX’s Polaris Dawn mission carried out the first civilian spacewalk.

Industry observers note that on November 14, 1966, Lovell and Aldrin also photographed the area around the Red Sea and the Nile River valley. NASA featured that image in its November 13 article.

According to the agency, the photos taken by Lovell, Aldrin and other Gemini astronauts demonstrated the value of orbital imagery for studying Earth’s surface, and helped scientists make the case for a dedicated Earth observation satellite program.

Industry observers note that one of the strongest advocates for Earth-observing satellites was William T. Pecora, director of the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) from 1965 to 1971.

In a 1969 statement, he noted the Earth Resources Observation Satellite program he championed, which later paved the way for Landsat, was “conceived in 1966 largely as a direct result of the demonstrated utility of Mercury and Gemini orbital photography to Earth resource studies.”.

For more than five decades, Landsat and other Earth-observing satellites have been used to monitor crops, wildfires, flooding, vegetation health and land use. In October, Landsat 9 captured autumn’s colors in the forests around Ripton, Vermont.
