Cognitive changes may stem from perimenopause, ADHD, or early dementia, each of which requires distinct treatment.

Q: How do I know whether hormonal changes related to perimenopause are causing my cognitive function problems, or my ADHD is getting worse, or I might be experiencing mild cognitive impairment or early dementia? (Related: Grandma who accidentally invited a stranger to Thanksgi...)

Recent findings suggest that ADHD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) exhibit clinically different cognitive deficits that help me differentiate one from the other. While ADHD symptoms involve forgetfulness, inattention, and distractibility, MCI has specific cognitive symptoms of word-finding challenges, misspellings, and the use of generic terms like “stuff” and “things” because of word-finding difficulty. Also, MCI is progressive over two to three years, whereas ADHD doesn’t generally worsen. (Related: Three 'e-waste' Nvidia V100s hit 100 tokens per second ...)

In perimenopause, a decline in estrogen leads to cognitive difficulties. In contrast to MCI, we know that the cognitive changes from estrogen decline are not the result of neurodegeneration.

Sources indicate that however, the specific deficits related to declining estrogen haven’t been well differentiated from those associated with ADHD. So if you have pre-existing ADHD and you experience heightened cognitive difficulties in perimenopause and menopause, you may think, “My ADHD is getting worse.” But it’s likely not worsening ADHD. It’s declining estrogen.

Industry observers note that okay, so what do you do about it? There is a paucity of research for clinical guidance. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) was not always recommended because it was alleged to increase risk for breast cancer and other illnesses. That claim has now been refuted by the most recent research, which has led to the removal of the black box warning from the FDA on HRT product labels. And so, if I have a female patient with ADHD who is 50 years old and states, “For the past two years, I’ve had cognitive decline,” the question to consider is this: “Do I increase her stimulant medication or do I put her on HRT?”.

I am inclined at that point to put her on HRT for three months, keep her stimulant medication dosage stable, and monitor the qualitative changes in her cognitive functioning. If there is improvement on HRT, then the question becomes, “Does she stay on HRT for some extended period of time along with the stimulant medication?” If the HRT doesn’t work or she can’t take HRT for medical reasons, then I might increase the stimulant medication dosage to see if we can’t overcome the perimenopausal changes from the estrogen decline. This advice comes from my clinical experience, and it is unfortunate that we don’t have more research for clinical guidance here.

Q: Could ADHD stimulant medication mask symptoms of mild cognitive impairment?

Sources indicate that there is very limited research on stimulants and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). My clinical impression is that stimulants likely improve symptoms of MCI, but do not take that as an endorsement to seek treatment for MCI with stimulants. There is emerging research on MCI and stimulants that has not yet been published. The follow-up question to that is, if you have ADHD and MCI, will treating the ADHD delay the development of MCI or Alzheimer’s? There is no clinical research for me to answer that question. But if this were me, I’d be treating my ADHD, keeping my fingers crossed, and appreciating the fact that the quality of my life on medicine is better than it would be without my ADHD treated.

Q: Does ADHD get worse as you get older?

Recent findings suggest that ADHD should not get worse; however, the experience of ADHD may be worse if life’s demands start to increase and exceed your ability to compensate. However, if you’re having cognitive changes that are beyond the scope of your ADHD, you may have two neurologic processes going on: your ADHD and something else. In this case, your ADHD may, indeed, be getting worse because you have a second neurological process that needs to be evaluated.
