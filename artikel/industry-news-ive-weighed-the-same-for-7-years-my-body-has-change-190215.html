Weird and kind of random, right?

I thought so, too. I’ve never cared that much about how much I weigh, mostly because I didn’t have to and I didn’t want to. I’ve always been on the leaner side, so for most of my adult life, the extent of what I knew about my weight came down to whether my pants fit.

Then, about a decade ago, I started getting more serious about putting on muscle, and my trainer at the time, the major-armed and bigger-brained David Otey, introduced me to a kind of body-composition testing, a.k.a. “bioelectrical impedance analysis,” or BIA. The tech has been used to estimate body composition for decades, and you’ve probably seen one of the more sophisticated machines at your gym or doctor’s office. Step onto a platform, grab a pair of handles, wait 30 seconds, and the machine spits out a breakdown of how much of you is body fat, skeletal muscle mass, and more. (Related: Breaking: The Age of Machine Learning As Code Has Arriv...)

Sources indicate that david walked me through how BIA works: The machine sends a teeny-tiny electrical current through your body. Electricity moves more easily through lean tissue than through fat, so by measuring how that current travels through you, the machine can estimate how much fat and muscle you’re carrying.

It has been revealed that in 2019, I bought an InBody Dial H20N for my bathroom. By then, BIA was well established in the consumer “smart scale” market, and InBody’s scale was among the more advanced at-home options, using sensors at both your hands and feet to take a more complete reading of your body than the foot-only models that were common at the time. The app tracks every measurement, so I can see not just what my body looks like on any given day but how it has changed over time.

To understand why I love the damn thing, it’s important to know that I weigh roughly the same today as when I first brought the scale home seven years ago.

A conventional (dumb) scale would look at seven years of strength training, workouts I wanted to skip, workouts I probably should have skipped, and a totally unreasonable amount of thought about what I put into my body and deliver its verdict: Congrats, you weigh the same!

Sources indicate that but the InBody tells a different story. When I look back at my results from 2019, it estimated that I carried about 84 pounds of skeletal muscle mass and 33 pounds of body fat. Today, it shows I’ve got around 95 pounds of skeletal muscle and 24 pounds of body fat. Yep: According to the scale, I’ve added 11 pounds of muscle while losing about 9 pounds of fat—all while my total body weight has barely moved. (I won’t mention that I did all this during a stage of life when building muscle, and simply holding on to it, gets harder than it was when I was 25. But I will mention Men’s Health has done an amazing guide to body recomposition that is worth checking out.).

It has been revealed that now, a necessary reality check: Those body-comp numbers aren’t exact measurements in the same way that the number staring back at me from a dumb scale is a measurement. They’re estimates. My hydration levels can throw them off. What I just ate can throw them off. A hard workout can really throw them off.

Still, in studies comparing the InBody to much fancier ways of measuring body composition, the scale holds up surprisingly well for something that lives in your bathroom. There’s even a upcoming model, the H40, that Inbody says is even more precise. (I keep meaning to buy it, but then something breaks in my house and a new scale gets knocked down the priority list.).

None of this means that I obsess over whether I have 94.7 pounds of muscle on Tuesday and 93.9 on Thursday. What makes this useful to me aren’t the exact numbers; it’s the direction they’re headed in. I still don’t really care how much I weigh—I wear the same pants size as when I was 25 and 35, and all my blood work is fine. But I’m trying to carry more muscle, less fat, and a stronger, more capable body into the second half of my life. I just want to know that some of my ass-busting efforts are paying off.

It has been revealed that A dumb scale can’t tell me that. But a smart scale—this smart scale—is giving me all the direction I need.
