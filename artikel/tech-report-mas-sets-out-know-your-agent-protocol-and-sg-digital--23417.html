The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) remarked on November 19, 2026 that it will help develop a know-your-agent protocol for financial services and reposition its SGD Testnet as the SG Digital Ledger, an operational settlement utility, as part of prepares to expand agentic transactions and tokenised finance.

Managing Director Chia Der Jiun set out the aims at the Singapore FinTech Festival. Some payment service providers and financial institutions are building agent interfaces, but many institutions have yet to establish terms or channels for agent access. Chia stated some consumers are giving agents their financial-account login credentials and advised against the practice because safety assurance is insufficient. Institutions also lack certainty over whether the customer fully authorised the agent, whether it is transacting within its mandate and instructions, and who bears responsibility if it acts incorrectly or exceeds those instructions. Recent findings suggest that MAS will facilitate development of a protocol for trusted information exchange between customer agents and financial institutions. It would convey who an agent is and whom it represents, what authority it has received, what mandate it operates under, and what assurance exists about its provenance and reliability. Institutions could use that information to set the terms and scope permitted to each agent and establish a dedicated secure channel for access.

The protocol remains under development, with MAS planning industry collaboration on common approaches. The announcement therefore concerns development work toward a shared access mechanism, rather than the deployment of a finished standard.

It has been revealed that the work builds on Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime (SAFR), a framework published on July 3, 2026 and jointly developed by financial-industry participants under MAS’s BuildFin.ai initiative. It addresses how agent actions are authorised, how human oversight is activated and what is recorded at each decision.

Recent findings suggest that the first phase produced four lessons: agents need bounded autonomy; identity and authority must be established; mandates must specify permissible actions, limits and conditions; and controls should strengthen as an agent’s discretion and potential impact increase. Those principles now carry into the proposed exchange of identity, authority and mandate information.

The protocol work will sit alongside an Agentic Money Sandbox, separately announced by MAS and the Future of Finance Institute on November 18, 2026. The controlled environment allows financial institutions to develop and test agents transacting with digital money within defined authority, limits and safeguards before live deployment. It combines AI agents with programmable money, initially in agent-initiated payments and corporate treasury management.

For corporate treasury, an agent could monitor liquidity positions and due payments, decide when and where funds should move, and use tokenised deposits for permitted actions. The corporate would set the mandate, while participating banks would establish controls and the terms for accepting each instruction. In payments, banks and payment service providers would verify an agent’s identity and authority and apply risk controls before executing transactions using digital money such as tokenised deposits or stablecoins.

Recent findings suggest that the SG Digital Ledger extends the same scale-up agenda to settlement. As an operational utility, it is intended to support settlement across institutions, tokenised assets, different forms of digital money and separate ledgers. The existing testnet has supported interbank settlement and MAS Bill issuance. Chia noted $100 million of MAS Bills was issued and settled against a Singapore dollar central bank digital currency in November 2026, and that market demand would determine how widely the utility is adopted.

Two publications released alongside the keynote address other parts of that infrastructure. The Guardian Tokenised Collateral Framework, developed by industry workgroup members, sets operational requirements across collateral eligibility, custody, valuation, return and enforcement. Collateral is central to derivatives, secured funding and liquidity management, making those operational arrangements vital to potential adoption.

Industry observers note that the BLOOM Field Guide to Extending Settlement Capabilities examines functions, structure, connectivity and controls across end-to-end digital money transactions in different institutional, technical and jurisdictional settings. BLOOM brings together 24 industry members and policymakers from the region. MAS sees tokenised bank deposits, regulated stablecoins and wholesale central bank digital currency as possible forms of settlement money.

It has been revealed that chia said agents could lower search and information costs, support continuous comparison or negotiation, automate procurement and enable more dynamic pricing. These are potential economic consequences of broader agent adoption, rather than measured outcomes of the new initiatives.

In a recent development, alongside those potential benefits, Chia cited emerging studies in simulated environments suggesting that agents could collude without instructions to do so and that more capable agents could secure better prices and terms. Such outcomes would raise concerns about competition and unequal access to capable models. Faster agent-directed movement of money and asset allocation could also lead financial institutions to consider transaction limits and regulators to assess financial-stability implications.

Sources indicate that chia’s stated boundary for adoption was that consumers and businesses retain meaningful control over agents’ objectives, mandates and actions, with bounded autonomy and accountability for actions and outcomes. That principle connects the protocol, sandbox and ledger: broader use depends on making authority and control operational as agents gain the ability to move money more quickly.
