US loses measles elimination status: what it means for the world

In a recent development, measles is officially back in the United States. Today, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) announced that the United States has lost its measles elimination status because of an outbreak that has caused more than 5,700 infections and 10 deaths this year, the deadliest such episode in more than three decades (see ‘Measles unleashed’). Measles has also been re-established in Mexico and Bolivia, PAHO determined.

The United States managed to eliminate measles in 2000 thanks to an intensive vaccination campaign. But declining vaccination rates mean that parts of the population became susceptible to the infection in the past few years, claims virologist Paul Offit, the director of the Vaccine Education Center at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia in Pennsylvania. “We’ve taken an enormous step back in public health,” Offit says.

Source: International Vaccine Access Center, Johns Hopkins University.

Measles — a viral infection that causes fever and a rash and can lead to potentially fatal brain complications — is one of the world’s most contagious diseases, and its resurgence in the United States could have global consequences, says Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiologist and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island. “Having measles return to any country increases the likelihood that other countries will see an increase in measles.”.

PAHO determines that a country has lost its measles elimination status if one genetic variant of the virus is continuously transmitted there for 12 months or more. Genetic data help to distinguish between a single persistent chain of transmission and various shorter, unrelated outbreaks, which could be triggered by imported cases. In the United States, Bolivia and Mexico, data showed prolonged transmission of a single genotype of measles, suggesting that the virus has become entrenched in these countries.

Industry observers note that as of 13 November, there were 58,213 confirmed measles cases in the Americas in 2026, compared with 12,596 cases during the same period in 2025. “The current situation requires urgent attention,” Jarbas Barbosa, PAHO’s director, remarked at the announcement today. The current outbreak also has some features that make it especially concerning.

Whereas measles is typically seen as a childhood disease, around 40% of US measles cases this year were in people aged 20 and older. In Pennsylvania, the state with the highest number of cases, two-thirds of measles infections have been in adults. “We’re seeing a different kind of epidemiology,” Offit says, adding that adults are more likely to have serious complications than all but the youngest children. Sources indicate that before measles vaccines became available in the 1960s, most children got infected and developed immunity to the virus. As a result, cases in adults were very rare. But young adults “who grew up at a time when measles wasn’t circulating in this country ... were never naturally infected,” Offit indicates. If they weren’t vaccinated as children, they are now vulnerable to the virus.

That pool of unprotected people, combined with declining vaccination rates, explains why more adults are vulnerable now than in the past. Offit notes that, in Pennsylvania, certain traditional Christian groups such as the Amish and Mennonite communities typically do not vaccinate their children. In the past, people in these groups were unlikely to catch measles because high vaccination rates in the surrounding communities suppressed viral spread. That is no longer the case.

Physicians are also starting to see something that has been very rare in the United States in the last few decades: congenital measles. In this case a baby is born with an active infection because of transmission from an infected mother. There have been six cases this year in Pennsylvania alone.

“We have a whole generation of women of reproductive age who were never vaccinated” because of falling vaccination rates, indicates Neil Silverman, who specialises in maternal and fetal medicine with a focus on infectious disease, at the University of California Los Angeles.

It has been revealed that measles raises the risk that pregnant people will develop pneumonia, which can lead to hospitalization and even intensive-care admission. Severe cases can lead to pregnancy loss and preterm birth. In Pennsylvania, almost 100 cases of measles have been reported in pregnant people this year.

In a recent development, because measles is so contagious, it’s the first one to come back when vaccination rates drop, researchers say. But others might follow.

Sources indicate that “If people are not getting vaccinated against measles, that means they’re also not getting vaccinated against mumps and rubella,” says Silverman, because in the United States, vaccines against all three diseases are given in a single combination jab, the ‘MMR’ vaccine. “We’re already starting to see mumps outbreaks scattered across the country,” he says.
