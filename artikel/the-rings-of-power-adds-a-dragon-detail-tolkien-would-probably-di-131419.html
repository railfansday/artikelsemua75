Amazon's The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power series has never been shy when it comes to expanding on or even reshaping key events from the Second Age of Middle-earth. With that in mind, it follows that J.R.R. Tolkien would probably take matter with a new dragon reveal that was just made in The Rings of Power season 3.

It has been revealed that across the board, The Rings of Power is an entertaining epic showcasing the Second Age and Sauron's rise as he gets closer and closer to forging his master ring to rule them all. Featuring classic Lord of the Rings characters including Gandalf, Galadriel, Elrond, and more, there's much to love about the Prime Video series. However, the show hasn't been without its controversies due to various lore additions and changes. (Related: Breaking: Introducing Waypoint-1: Real-time interactive...)

Industry observers note that case in point, The Rings of Power season 3 features a crucial conversation between Sauron and Celeborn, Galadriel's husband, who for centuries was thought to have died during the days of the First Age and the war against Morgoth. It's here that the subject of dragons is brought up, and a key detail that likely raised some eyebrows among longtime Tolkien fans. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

Sources indicate that in The Rings of Power season 3, episode 5, Sauron tells Celeborn that he was riding the great dragon Ancalagon the Black when the mighty creature set fire to his entire Elven legion. It was this battle during the First Age that led to Celeborn's capture and imprisonment in Gundabad for centuries while he was widely presumed dead, a brand-new history made specifically for The Rings of Power.

It has been revealed that on the surface, it makes sense that Sauron and Ancalagon fought together as two of Morgoth's most powerful servants. However, J.R.R. Tolkien never depicted dragons as mounts like horses. They were instead intelligent and independent creatures (not unlike the Eagles) while still being under Morgoth's dark influence. Alcalagon the Black was the greatest of Morgoth's dragons, though it's never been cited anywhere on the page that he would have ever allowed anyone to ride him.

On the one hand, it's likely that Rings of Power is using Sauron's claim to highlight his own power and influence as Morgoth's dark successor, while also tying him directly to Celeborn's absence and Galadriel's subsequent pain and grief. That noted, the suggestion that Sauron rode Ancalagon like a personal mount and controlled the dragon to attack Celeborn and his forces feels like too significant a deviation from what Tolkien established on the page.

Recent findings suggest that during the First Age's War of Wrath, Morgoth unleashed his dragons during a final battle for Middle-earth, though Ancalagon the Black was slain by Eärendil, thus changing the course of the battle. Once Morgoth himself was defeated, the few surviving dragons scattered and ventured north, though none were ever as powerful as Ancalagon himself.

According to recent reports, by the Third Age, Smaug was one of the very last great dragons in the region, and his attack on the Lonely Mountain to claim its treasure drove the dwarves from their kingdom of Erebor. However, Smaug was ultimately slain by Bard the Bowman during the events of The Hobbit.

While dragons don't have the greatest prominence in The Lord of the Rings mythology we see on screen, they played a vital role in the First Age of Middle-earth, particularly when it comes to Ancalagon the Black. However, one has to wonder if Tolkien would have supported the idea that Sauron rode the mightiest dragon as a mount. I certainly have my doubts, though it could be argued that the feat helps point to Sauron's own impressive dark powers throughout the history of Middle-earth.

The final two episodes of The Rings of Power season 3 begin streaming November 24th on Prime Video.

Set in the Second Age of Middle-earth, The Rings of Power explores the forging of the iconic rings, the rise of the Dark Lord Sauron, and the epic events leading up to the stories in J.R.R. Tolkien's classic novels.
