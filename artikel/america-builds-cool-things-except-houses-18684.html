America builds cool things, except houses

🌐 Source: Housingwire • 📅 20 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (972 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

  • We no longer know how to build a quality product at an affordable price, and we’re approaching a crisis that may finally force change.
  • When the American auto industry fell behind, higher-quality imports pressured Detroit to innovate.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

If housing were an importable product, most American homebuilders would be out of business. Because it isn’t, our industry has never faced the competitive pressure that drives innovation. Decades of rising home prices have made matters worse. When buyers tolerate every cost increase, nothing pushes us to cut costs.

We no longer know how to build a quality product at an affordable price, and we’re approaching a crisis that may finally force change. (Related: How Much Does a Will Cost? Complete Price Guide...)

When the American auto industry fell behind, higher-quality imports pressured Detroit to innovate. Detroit may have lost market share, but Americans could still get quality cars. If the American homebuilding industry can no longer supply affordable housing, it raises questions about whether homes fit the model of the type of product the USA excels at building. It has been revealed that america’s reign of manufacturing dominance took place during an era when most of the everyday objects we’d now call “products” weren’t manufactured in the modern sense – they were made at home or by local artisans. We excelled at mass-producing novel and exciting products like cars or firearms. By contrast, China’s contemporary manufacturing dominance built a world where almost every household object that exists is industrially manufactured.

Sources indicate that american manufacturing is still the second largest globally, but our dominance applies to sophisticated products like monoclonal antibodies, jet engines, and advanced industrial machines. If a proposed product has near-magical requirements and a large enough R&D budget, we’re the right country to produce it, but we’ve never held anything like China’s role as the dominant mass producer of cheap, quotidian consumer goods.

Recent findings suggest that the products the US manufactures competitively are typically regulated very differently from upcoming homes. The FAA doesn’t require Boeing to use quarter-inch titanium rivets spaced one and a half inches apart. Instead, it lets manufacturers compete to engineer solutions that meet performance standards set cooperatively by a group of industry stakeholders. Many nations use performance-based standards in their housing codes, and stricter standards don’t have to mean less affordable housing. Japan is often cited as a success story for maintaining housing affordability despite having some of the world’s most demanding seismic standards.

Another major regulatory barrier limits where housing gets built. After World War II, federally subsidized highways and mortgages made the car-oriented suburb the default, and cities rewrote their zoning codes to reflect that model. Minimum lot sizes, deep setbacks, density caps, and parking requirements effectively banned the “missing middle” housing that once met demand in the most desirable areas. Instead of letting neighborhoods grow through small, continuous infill, zoning froze them in place and pushed upcoming growth outward.

Under these rules, housing development is now effectively squeezed into just two viable business models: the large-scale suburban single-family homebuilder, and the institutional apartment developer. Both models require large swathes of vacant land where they can run the repeatable processes of a century-old mass manufacturing model.

Recent findings suggest that manufacturing has moved on. The art of producing boring things at hyperscale is a uniquely Chinese invention built around a state-orchestrated model of supply chain networks featuring industrial clustering and dynamic capacity pooling. Widespread adoption of the right technology could coordinate labor resources and supply chains to maximize network resource efficiency. Before any of that matters, we have to admit that the housing we’ve been building isn’t good enough.

Sources indicate that nobody will drive by a contemporary master planned community in a hundred years to admire its architecture. Almost every American production homebuilder returning from a European vacation has had the same unsettling feeling that not a single home we spent our careers building will still be standing a hundred years after we die. Builders have so little unique brand identity that a customer driving through a new development has to rely entirely on marketing signage to know when they’ve left one builder’s tract and entered another’s.

Customers are opting out of choosing between buying in a dull, exurban neighborhood and renting an overpriced “luxury” 5-over-1 apartment building in a more desirable area. They want it cheap and they want it good. When the available housing presents insufficient value, they double up with roommates or family.

Recent findings suggest that unique designs carry a level of risk that’s largely incompatible with the scale of the predominant business models. Even in Hollywood, a few low-budget originals have begun outperforming franchise blockbusters. If buyers are opting out of what we’ve been building, maybe it’s time we built something more congruent with what we’re good at building: products with nearly magical requirements. A home that’s affordable, durable, and worth admiring a hundred years from now is exactly that kind of product.

Industry observers note that people with the means and knowledge to build their own quirky homes have captivated social media onlookers with Earthships, Quonset huts, geodesic domes, and structures made from compressed earth bricks, straw bales, and all manner of materials that are wildly unsuited to production homebuilding. None of those methods is likely to scale, but each of those wacky homes is a testament to the creativity we’re capable of. Our business models leave that creativity nowhere to go.

In a recent development, what might housing look like if we took more risks? Emerging technologies are enabling the rapid iteration of newly introduced designs and accelerating the administrative processes behind bidding out new product. Builders could take more chances on small projects built differently. A small bet that fails costs little, and one that succeeds can prove out a profitable model worth repeating.

Recent findings suggest that as a nation, we’ve never done our best work on bland things. We’re at our best when we’re solving a nearly impossible problem or designing something enduringly lovable. It’s time we treated the attainable American home as one of those problems.
