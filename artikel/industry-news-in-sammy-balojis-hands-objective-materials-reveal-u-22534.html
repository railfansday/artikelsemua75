Industry observers note that in the mid-2000s, after he completed his master’s degree in information sciences and communication at the University of Lubumbashi, Sammy Baloji started working with the French Institute. The director of the institute, also based in Lubumbashi, was interested in colonial architecture and cultural heritage history, and he collaborated with academics researching those topics.

Sources indicate that baloji’s work for the French Institute mainly entailed photographing events and later working with researchers from France. But he was already on the way to becoming the artist he is today. In a recent development, working at the French Institute, he recalled in an interview with ARTnews, “really shifted my perspective, in terms of outlining photography or research, because I was interested in the way that [the researchers] were addressing the city or buildings or cultural heritage from a historical and objective perspective. I was mainly interested in people’s experiences, the testimonies of people who are living [in Lubumbashi like] myself.”. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

The two opposite approaches opened up the question of how he could “use the so-called scientific objective materials and bring them close to the human experience, to the architecture of local sites.”.

The inquiry led the artist to make one of his first significant projects, Mémoire (2006), a photography collage series of colonial-era black and white archival images and colored prints showing contemporary Lubumbashi in the Katanga province, which is rich in mineral resources including copper, uranium, lithium, diamonds and cobalt.

Baloji’s work for the French Institute had allowed him to observe the social, environmental and political impact of mining in Katanga. Now, using a combination of photography and research, he was able to underscore how Belgian colonization had shaped the Democratic Republic of Congo, whose history of extraction he has continued to explore.

It has been revealed that works continuing to build on the themes of Mémoire can at the moment be seen in two high-profile showings. Baloji is making his debut US solo with “When Extraction Becomes Form It Speaks In Borrowed Tongues,” at the Wexner Center for the Arts in Columbus, Ohio, on view through February 14, 2027. The exhibition explores how Europe’s decorative arts and architecture still include elements of Congo’s colonialist history and the extraction of natural resources. It takes a critical look at the impact on land, people and cultural forms, speaking to complicated relationships between Central Africa, Europe, and the Americas.

Concurrently with the Wexner Center show, Baloji’s work can be seen at the Venice Biennale, both in the main show, curated by the late Koyo Kouoh, and in the national pavilion of the Democratic Republic of Congo. In the main exhibition at Venice, Baloji is showing three sculptures and 16 collage prints. Two of the sculptures reference works from a 19th-century Belgian colonial museum and the national collection built in the 1970s by the former President of the Democratic Republic of Congo, Mobutu Sese Seko.

“I started to realize that the colonial system was not just about controlling territory but also occupying societies and imposing new rules,” noted Baloji, who splits his time between Brussels and Lubumbashi.

In the Wexner show, Baloji is exhibiting Suspension (Debris of History, Matters of Memory), a commission conceived with architect Gloria Cabral and in collaboration with art historian Cécile Fromont. Drawing from the design of historical Kongo cloth, the suspended large-scale sculpture is made of mining and construction waste, the latter from recent work at the Wexner Center. Meanwhile, a newly introduced tapestry called Rethreaded Indies, made by Baloji and Fromont, addresses the French Old Indies tapestry series (1637–1730), produced at the royal Gobelins manufactory in Paris, connecting that with Kongolese weaving. The works use textile and architecture to correct misinformation about African histories, showing how European colonization has shaped the continent’s narratives, often for the worse.

Industry observers note that “I would say my interest in his work is how he weaves these histories together, and in a way that I find extremely compelling,” mentioned Julieta González, the head of visual arts at the Wexner Center and the curator of the artist’s exhibition. Baloji’s work, she added, “is vital because it weaves together so many historical events and situations but with materiality that speaks to the particular situation he’s critiquing, which is the legacies of colonialism” in the Democratic Republic of Congo. The show she curated, she said, offers the public “an insight into [the artist’s] very research-oriented practice and how it unfolds in so many different mediums [which includes] video, sculpture and photography.”.

Sources indicate that baloji was born in 1978 in Lubumbashi and grew up with, in some sense, limited knowledge about the history of Belgium’s colonization of the Democratic Republic of Congo. (Connecting that and resource extraction in Katanga with global or political history came later following reports of Congo supplying Iran with uranium, which the Central African country denied.) In secondary school, Baloji cofounded a collective that read and shared books from libraries and schools that they had access to. He was also drawing at the time, producing some of his first artworks.

It has been revealed that his parents did not encourage his plan to study at the Fine Art Academy, so for his master’s degree, he studied information sciences and communication, which happened to align with the artistic practices he was interested in. Ironically, or maybe coincidentally, the course Baloji pursued to appease his parents so he could make his own career choices ended up being formative to his practice.

Industry observers note that baloji is also known for more than just his art. In 2008, he cofounded the Lubumbashi-based Picha Art and Research Center and the Lubumbashi Biennale, whose ninth edition, cocurated by Congolese-Belgian curator Sorana Munsya and Ethiopian-Eritrean-Italian artist Jermay Michael Gabriel, opened in October. The art space and biennial are platforms that help expand access to the arts and support local artists with needed infrastructure, especially because of limited state support.

Recent findings suggest that camille Ostermann, an independent art consultant who worked on Congo’s national pavilion at the Venice Biennale, said, “I’ve admired [Baloji’s] collaborative practice [since the early 2010s]: works co-authored with historians, exhibitions built around artists he invites. But it was at the Lubumbashi Biennale, which Picha organizes, that I saw this spirit at work. In a city shaped by mining, by what is extracted and carried elsewhere, Picha does the opposite: it builds, gathers, gives back. Many artists told me their paths had been changed by Picha and Sammy’s mentorship. Like Ibrahim Mahama in Tamale or Yinka Shonibare in Lagos, Sammy works internationally while staying rooted in Lubumbashi, deliberately keeping the two connected: what he builds abroad can open doors for others at home.”.

According to recent reports, picha Center and the Lubumbashi Biennale are also, in some ways, an extension of Baloji’s research and practice. The biennial, he stated, can start conversations about extractivism and ecological crisis, as well as talk about “the impact of international and economic interests on the environment and societies.” It was a statement that could easily have been applied to many of his artworks.
