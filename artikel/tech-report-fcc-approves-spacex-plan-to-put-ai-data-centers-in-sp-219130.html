Tech Report: FCC approves SpaceX plan to put AI data centers in space

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

SpaceX was given conditional approval by the Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday to deploy and operate a constellation of up to 1 million satellites for its orbital data center business, a regulatory victory for Elon Musk and his bid to move AI data centers into space.

Recent findings suggest that the FCC authorization was granted despite objections from Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society, which flagged concerns over orbital debris and congestion, radiofrequency interference, and environmental consequences. Amazon, which is building a competitor to SpaceX’s Starlink service, argued in its petition that the application lacked basic details and described it as a “lofty ambition rather than a real blueprint.” Despite that lack of detail, Amazon also stated the proposal “risks real harm.”. The FCC dismissed some of the key concerns raised by opponents, including potential threats to scientific research, because the agency determined they fell outside its regulatory authority. (Related: Tech Report: Over $60k raised for famous station cat af...)

FCC Chair Brendan Carr noted in a statement that the action “opens a bold latest frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth.”.

It has been revealed that musk thanked Carr in a post on X, writing “Much appreciate. This will be a great asset to America and the world.”.

This initial regulatory approval doesn’t mean orbital data centers are imminent.

SpaceX faces a number of technical and economic challenges, including how to dramatically reduce the cost of manufacturing and launching satellites, cool processors in space, and even how to secure financing for chips that are destined for orbit.

Sources indicate that the company must also clear other regulatory hurdles. The FCC has not yet approved SpaceX’s plans to handle orbital debris for satellites that would operate above 600 kilometers, for instance. SpaceX requested a waiver of existing FCC rules, which requires satellites ending missions below 2,000 kilometers reenter Earth’s atmosphere within five years of completing those missions. The company is interested in moving some of these retired satellites into other orbits around Earth or even around the sun, according to the FCC authorization document.

Industry observers note that spaceX has said it prepares to deploy the satellites, known as Starmind, at altitudes ranging from 500 to 1,002 kilometers. The FCC’s approval gives SpaceX the authority to use radiofrequency communications to maintain contact with and control its orbital data centers from Earth if their primary communications systems fail. The Starmind satellites would primarily communicate using lasers to connect with other satellites, including SpaceX’s Starlink.

The FCC has given SpaceX time to sort out these issues. Under the approval, SpaceX has seven years to get its first operational satellite into orbit and 14 years to deploy the entire constellation.
