Tech Report: Commerce approved sales of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies, Rep. Mast states

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The subagency of the Commerce Department that regulates and enforces exports approved the sale of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies including Alibaba and Tencent as of May 2026, according to documents released Thursday by the House Foreign Affairs Committee.

Industry observers note that some of those approvals, made by Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security, included sales to companies designated by the Department of Defense as "Chinese Military Companies," according to the documents. From December through May, companies had made 3.4 million requests for sales of U.S. AI chips to China, the committee reported, citing those made by Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices. Industry observers note that "Over the past month, the administration's handling of chip export controls has put our national security and technology edge at risk. The Bureau of Industry and Security has failed to enforce the rules, and Congress must now conduct rigorous oversight of the administration's policies," Rep. Gregory Meeks, D-N.Y., the top Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, mentioned at a meeting of the panel Thursday at which members voted to release the documents. Industry observers note that spokespersons for Nvidia and AMD noted their companies comply with U.S. export control laws.

Nvidia argued it was following the Commerce Department regulations that were in effect at the time.

A spokesperson from the Bureau of Industry and Security remarked it shared information with Congress on both approvals and denials of licenses for Nvidia's H200 and comparable chips. "Licensing decisions are the product of deliberation and consultation among multiple U.S. Government agencies," the spokesperson said.

"The review and licensing process spanned more than half a year, with approvals subject to strict conditions and limited volumes. Despite receiving some licenses, we have since stopped shipping H200s to China," Nvidia spokesperson John Rizzo told CNBC.

It has been revealed that on Tuesday, Nvidia reported $2.4 billion in quarterly revenue from H200 and related chip sales in China. In total, customers based in China and Hong Kong made up about 9.5% of all revenue.

In a recent development, "AMD complies with applicable U.S. export control laws and regulations and maintains rigorous processes to ensure compliance across our global operations," AMD said through a spokesperson.

The approval of 879,000 AI chips is "not that many," Bernstein Senior Analyst Stacy Rasgon told CNBC, adding that this news is not surprising.

"It doesn't sound to me like they're approving everything they got willy nilly," Rasgon said of the Commerce Department. With 879,000 approvals out of 3.4 million requested AI chip sales, he added, "There was a process in place, and it sounds like they approved about one out of every four.".

Despite a rapid succession of China export controls on AI chips that started under the Biden administration in 2022, Nvidia and AMD both re-engineered some chips to comply and be sold legally in China. And in December, Trump said Nvidia could ship H200s, its older generation Hopper chips, to "approved customers in China" if the U.S. got a 25% cut of sales.

The U.S. then licensed Nvidia H200 sales to Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and other Chinese companies, although sales were limited because Beijing didn't approve large-scale imports, chip analyst Patrick Moorhead of Moor Insights & Strategy told CNBC last week.

H200 sales numbers remained low due to those restrictions on China's end. Nvidia mentioned H200 shipments to China made up less than 1% of data center revenue for the quarter that ended in July.

It has been revealed that "In the grand context of the size of this market and how much they're shipping, it's a rounding error. It's tiny," Rasgon told CNBC.

Sources indicate that the committee cited the Export Control Reform Act as justification for releasing the data. The panel stated the law allows it to declassify and release export data if it "determines that the withholding of the following information is contrary to the national interest.".

The license requests released by the Foreign Affairs Committee include requests to sell to major firms including ByteDance, Tesla (Shanghai) and Baidu.

The release of documents comes in the wake of Jensen Huang's resignation from the chip supplier Nvidia after news surfaced that he had several extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese spy that led to concerns about Nvidia chips being diverted to China.

In the weeks since, Republicans and Democrats in Congress have called for legislation to be passed that would impose stricter export controls on the sale of advanced AI chips to China and other adversaries.

Recent findings suggest that "The license information we are disclosing today will help inform the American public about how BIS has handled licenses for Nvidia H200 and equivalent AMD chips to China. The public has the right to know how these decisions were made," Meeks said.

Mast mentioned two of the firms listed in Nvidia license applications to BIS — Maginfra and New Matrix — are suspected "smuggling fronts." The New York Times reported in November that both companies had empty offices and had ties to companies under national security sanctions.

In a recent development, "Both are fake companies. If BIS approved the sale of these chips to these firms, obviously that is of tremendous concern to us — especially given that President Trump made clear last year that the sale of any AI chips to China will have very real national security conditions," Mast said at the Thursday meeting.

Maginfra and New Matrix did not reply to requests for comment.

Shares of AMD fell Thursday after the investment firm Hunterbrook Media issued a report alleging the manufacturer "has its own China problem." The report claims that "after the U.S. blacklisted Chinese server maker IEIT Systems, the company kept getting newer AMD firmware, which enables AMD chips to run in servers.".

CNBC did not independently verify this information.

Fallout from the Huang scandal on Capitol Hill has intensified in the last week, as lawmakers question the degree to which the former head of Nvidia's relationship with the suspected Chinese spy could have jeopardized national security and American AI dominance.

Huang is due for a closed-door transcribed interview with the House Foreign Affairs panel and two other committees on Dec. 1. Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter is scheduled to appear before the panels Dec. 2.

Senate banking committee Chair Tim Scott, R-S.C., revealed on CNBC's "Squawk Box" on Wednesday that Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had also agreed to testify.

It has been revealed that lutnick has come under fire over his relationship to Huang and for his failure to take action on Megaspeed, the Singaporean tech giant where Alice Huang, with whom the former Nvidia CEO had an affair, was an executive until 2025. U.S. investigators suspect Megaspeed diverted Nvidia chips to China.

Amid that criticism, the Commerce Department on Monday issued an interim final rule to close a loophole that has allowed Chinese companies to lease Nvidia's advancing computing power from data centers in third countries.

The rule was "written by and at the direction of" the Treasury Department and the White House National Security Council, BIS said in a press release.

— Megan Cassella, Daren Fonda and Kif Leswing contributed to this report.
