A fresh study estimates that about three in four former NFL players who died from 2008 through 2023 had CTE, even after accounting for brain donation bias.

Almost every former NFL player’s brain that researchers have examined has shown signs of chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), the degenerative disease tied to repeated hits to the head.

The study estimated that families were more likely to donate a player’s brain if he had clear cognitive symptoms.

Researchers at Boston University have now worked out how much that choice skews the picture.

Industry observers note that the team’s estimate: about three in four NFL players who died between 2008 and 2023 had CTE. That includes the roughly five in six whose brains were not examined. (Related: Industry News: Hyperparameter Search with Transformers ...)

Industry observers note that three in four is lower than the figure from donated brains alone, where more than nine in ten had the disease. But the researchers noted the skew in who donates can’t explain the high rate away. (Related: Industry News: How we sped up transformer inference 100...)

Julia Gallini, a doctoral candidate in biostatistics at the Boston University School of Public Health, led the analysis with Yorghos Tripodis, a professor of biostatistics there.

Colleagues at the university’s CTE Center, Mass General Brigham and the Concussion & CTE Foundation joined them.

The figure applies to players after death only and notes nothing about living former players or current ones.

Gallini said a precise number helps size the problem.

“An estimated 75% prevalence among NFL players who died over a 16-year period is concerning, and suggests we need to increase efforts to support families, diagnose CTE during life, and treat CTE-related symptoms,” she remarked.

CTE can only be confirmed after death, by examining the brain. So nearly everything known about it in football players comes from brains donated to a bank in Boston, and a 2017 study of 111 of them found CTE in 110.

Families don’t donate at random. A player who grew confused or forgetful in his last years is the one whose family thinks to call a brain bank.

So the donated brains carry more disease than the full population of former players would.

According to recent reports, epidemiologists call this selection bias. Earlier attempts to allow for it could only bracket the true figure somewhere between roughly 10% and 100%.

The team started with every former NFL player who died between January 2008 and December 2023, 2,362 men in all.

Recent findings suggest that they were found through the National Death Index, the national register of death certificates. Of those, 396 had donated their brains and 1,966 had not.

Sources indicate that the death certificates gave the researchers one fact they could compare across both groups: whether dementia was recorded as a cause of death. It was for 19.3% of donors and 11.2% of the others.

Because many people with failing memory die of something else, the team widened that category using survey estimates of milder cognitive trouble in former players.

Then they ran the comparison 1,000 times to capture the uncertainty.

According to recent reports, the result was a ratio. A former player with clear cognitive symptoms was about five times as likely to have his brain donated as one without them.

Donors also died younger, at 67 on average against nearly 75. They had played longer too, seven years against under five.

Among the 387 donated brains with a clear verdict, 362 had CTE, or 93.5%.

After adjusting for differences in donation likelihood, the researchers estimated that 74.5% of all 2,362 players had CTE at death.

According to recent reports, the team then tested how much that number depended on their assumptions. They restricted the sample to players who died at 55 or older, then 60 or older, and tried other definitions of who counted as symptomatic.

Across those versions, the estimate ranged from 70% to 86%.

The main figure sat toward the lower end of that range. The most conservative analysis put the figure at 63.7%.

Industry observers note that suppose symptomatic players had been ten times as likely to donate, rather than five. Even then, the authors calculated, more than 60% of the dead players would have had the disease.

CTE in the brain is not the same as symptoms in life.

It has been revealed that using the same correction, the team estimated that 27.2% of the players had a history of dementia before they died. In the donated group alone, the figure was 64.9%.

The authors mentioned the true lifetime figure is probably higher than 27.2%. The donors it rests on died about eight years younger than the other players, with less time to develop dementia.

Many of the brains with CTE, they added, may have been at an early stage and symptom-free at death.

Matt Hasselbeck, an 18-year NFL veteran who serves as an ambassador for a research study called DIAGNOSE CTE, remarked the numbers read to him like a scouting report.

Industry observers note that the correction rests on a stand-in. Death certificates and survey estimates of memory trouble stood in for a direct look at the players whose brains were not examined. Dementia is also often left off a death certificate.

The authors also could not say whether CTE itself shortens life. That is one more reason the estimate stops at the dead and does not reach anyone still living.

Nothing here covers college or high school players, whose death records and symptom surveys don’t exist in the same form.

The authors noted those estimates would be far harder to build, and that building them matters for sizing the problem.

Tripodis said the group recognizes the findings may worry living players and their families.

“Discoveries from the brain bank have already brought us significantly closer to a diagnosis during life, at which point our most key goal will be to develop effective treatments for CTE,” he said.

Until a living player can be tested for CTE, the dead are the only ones who can be counted.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.
