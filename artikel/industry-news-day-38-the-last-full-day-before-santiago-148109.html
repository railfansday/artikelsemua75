Knowing it was the last significant walking day before Santiago, I got up early and started under the stars.

The shadow of the trees made it nearly impossible to see where I was going, but I leaned into the challenge.

My legs had been consistently sore for a week.

And yet, I didn’t want to stop walking.

As the sun came up, though, so did my mood.

I wove in and out of towns, trying to find a cafe that was open, but every single one I passed was closed. Many of the restaurants and albergues were closing for the season. In a way, it felt like we were politely being asked to “wrap it up.”.

Well, a beautiful stone-walled restaurant was my savior. The WiFi network was called “A Cova de Meiga” (a cove of the witch), and it felt like a warm sanctuary.

A fire in the corner, old sewing machines and instruments on the wall, and the restaurant owner’s two young boys brought a childlike joy to my morning.

My Argentinian brother found me there and we shared a tarta de Santiago with our coffees as we warmed up by the fire.

After that cafe, we made it two kilometers before we found our Italian brother at another cafe.

He asked if we wanted anything, and even though we all had just stopped, I suggested we get another coffee and take the day very slowly.

They were more than happy with that idea, and we all sat with another round of café con leche while we shared a tortilla.

When we started walking, silence quickly became the conversation.

The presence of the moment felt potent as we saw the trail markers giving a countdown.

Each leaf glowing with the wind.

The smell of pine from the needles I crushed in my hand.

The chill of the wind combined with the warmth of the sun.

Every detail felt more vibrant because it was the last full day.

In O Pedrouzo, we decided to stop again for lunch. With a table full of food at the Mexican restaurant, A Catriña, we talked and ate and talked and ate.

There were still 15 out of the 36 kilometers to do, so together we set out into the afternoon sun.

Industry observers note that we walked through what felt like a magical forest, with everything around us was alive with the most intense colors of green. Like little kids, we hugged the trees, climbed them, and hung from their bark. (Related: Industry News: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Fa...)

In a recent development, once we hit the trail marker that read we were under ten kilometers, I saw my Italian brother put a sticker on the column. I stopped next to him and he suggested we walk together till the end. In agreement, I plopped on the road to wait for our third. A few minutes later, our Argentinian brother jumped on us in greeting. We lay there in that spot, soaking up the reality of being so close to Santiago.

Recent findings suggest that together, side-by-side-by-side, we finished the last stretch of the day. Our strides naturally matching, we marched onward, each taking turns singing our country’s national anthems. It felt like our individual patriotism fed the energy of the group. The exact energy boost we needed for the end.

It has been revealed that at the municipal albergue in Monte de Gozo, the Pilgrims Hostel was basically a compound. We were in a building labeled 29, and the others were in a terraced line down the descent. They were newly constructed, and there were enough to fit 400 pilgrims. We only ended up with 9 pilgrims on the whole property. (Related: Latest Update: How to train a newly introduced language...)

Nearby at the pilgrim monument overlooking Santiago, the three of us caught the sunset at the perfect time as it was about to go over the horizon. I couldn’t believe I was looking at the cathedral that we’d talked about for so long. Leaning against the metal monument, I cried for the end of the journey.

Tomorrow, I’d touch that cathedral.

It has been revealed that sitting on the hill, I realized I felt more present and at peace today than I had for most of my life. Then it hit me….

Those had been my intentions for the year, and tomorrow starts a newly introduced cycle of intentions.

Our last full night was spent making a steep descent to the grocery store just to climb back up and eat.

Our Italian brother entertained the pilgrims in the kitchen. He ate hot dogs raw while he cooked fish sticks in the microwave, initially thinking they were chicken and didn’t need to be fried. They did need to be fried.

Sources indicate that one of the girls from Germany had been carrying a candle for her whole Camino. She had only used it once, but in celebration of my upcoming birthday, she lit it and set it in front of me to make a pre-birthday wish. How special.

Industry observers note that once everyone was done eating, we played Uno with seven people. The four other people had walked different Caminos (the Primitivo and the Norte) and weren’t as accustomed to the crowds. To us, the numbers in the albergue were few, so getting to hear different stories and perspectives was fascinating.

I laughed the perfect amount for the last night. It was joyful.
