Recent findings suggest that formnext is the world’s biggest additive manufacturing trade show, gathering the best 3D printer manufacturers, large and small, to show off their latest goods in Germany each year. And this year was a blast.

Recent findings suggest that the event is open to the public, but the true purpose of the show is for businesses, factories and retailers to come and do some shopping. This year the show shrank to three halls from the usual four, with consumer grade 3D printing having a larger presence than ever before. (Related: Course Launch Community Event...)

My maker’s heart sang at all the newly introduced desktop 3D printers now available, from some of the best CNC machines to the cutting edge in UV machines, to trusty glitter coated plastic! (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

But AI had to go and make its presence known! It stamped a huge footprint at the show, and response was predictably mixed.

Sources indicate that the shocker this year is the SnowPod, a prosumer desktop metal printer that uses a laser to melt thin layers of steel powder within a chamber of nitrogen. It’s launching soon on Kickstarter with a VIP price starting at $4999. Printing in metal is tricky stuff, with more safety hazards to overcome than FDM or resin. The metal dust is not only flammable, but hazardous to breathe, but SnowX seems to have a handle on safety protocols.

I counted eight completely new tool changers, plus two more that debuted last year at Formnext. Prusa, Bambu, Snapmaker, Elegoo, Creality, and Flashforge now have tool changers from four to nine tools, with Anycubic being the lone single nozzle holdout. Crowdfunding hopefuls Axiloop, Co Print and WonderMaker have entered the chat, with nozzle maker Phaetus tagging along with the new Hydra system. Bambu’s H3C is at the moment the machine to beat, shipping with 8 normal tools and one bonus 2.85mm tool for printing super soft 75A TPU.

Sources indicate that UV printing is gaining momentum as manufacturers figure out how to shrink massive industrial machines into desktop printers that are more affordable for entrepreneurial makers. Being able to 3D print full color resin miniatures is enticing, but I’m worried that newbies will be blindsided by the hidden cost of printhead replacement. These easily cloggable printheads are similar to what you see in a paper ink jet printer, but easily cost $900 to replace. And it’s not a question of if you’ll need to replace it, but when.

Recent findings suggest that prusa Research's newest innovation wasn’t a 3D printer, but a fresh way to run one. Prusa Connect Local keeps your printers off the internet without resorting to sneaker net and a USB stick. It’s similar to LAN mode, but runs off a local server so you can coordinate jobs for a whole print farm. The server can be as simple as a Raspberry Pi and your “farm” could be a fleet CORE One or a pair of MK4S. The software is free to download, open source and at the moment just for Prusa machines. I’m guessing that will change as the tech nerds start tinkering.

In a recent development, AI integration in 3D printing is inevitable, and quite useful in some cases. I don’t mind if my printer is monitoring print fails with a smart camera, or if my 3D scanner uses AI to clean up a model. But things get complicated when AI takes over the creative process of model design. Some say AI modeling is no different than cameras taking the job of painters…but is it? Hi3D and Tripo both had impressive booths to sway users to their side.
