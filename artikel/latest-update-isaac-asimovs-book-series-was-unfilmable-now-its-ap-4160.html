Recent findings suggest that sci-fi fans would collectively agree that some stories of the genre are better off staying on the page. For long, it was also believed that Isaac Asimov's books could not be translated to the screen, and for good reason. Especially when it comes to the Foundation book series, it was hard not to believe it was largely "unfilmable.".

Some of the greatest sci-fi books are written in a way that it is easy to directly adapt them for the screen. Isaac Asimov's Foundation books, in contrast, defy almost every rule of visual drama. It unfolds less as a fictional drama and more as a deep dive into everything from sociological mechanics to human psychology. While at it, it also spans across centuries, shifting its focus from one character to another.

The books' massive time jumps, anti-cinematic action, and prioritization of ideas over character beats made it impossible to adapt for the screen. This explains why many planned big-budget adaptations of the books also struggled to see the light of day in the past. Fortunately, however, Apple TV's Foundation took a massive risk and achieved the impossible by successfully adapting Isaac Asimov's books.

Impossible feats demand extreme risks. Owing to this, Apple TV's take on the Foundation books significantly changed the source material's approach. The biggest adaptation challenge came from the books' revolving characters. Every few chapters, the books experience time jumps, where old characters die of age and fresh ones emerge.

On the screen, viewers need rootable visual anchors who appear for longer periods.

Apple TV's Foundation carefully navigated this matter by introducing cloning, cyro-sleep, and digital consciousness. These became narrative devices that allowed the series to logically sustain the same characters and cast members across centuries and seasons. Even an immortal humanoid, named Lady Demerzel, emerges in Foundation from Asimov's wider universe. (Related: Latest Update: Introducing 🤗 Accelerate...)

It has been revealed that unlike the books, Apple TV's Foundation also visualizes key conflicts and collapses from the original story instead of solely focusing on their political implications. Even "psychohistory" from Isaac Asimov's Foundation gets a visual and more tangible representation in the series.

According to recent reports, armed with the Socratic method, Apple TV's Foundation understands that "The beginning of wisdom is the definition of terms." Instead of treating Asimov's story as a plot to be faithfully transcribed, the show first understands the true essence of its terms. It then turns into a more sprawling space-opera by wisely redefining its ideas.

Many readers have complained that Apple TV's Foundation is too distinct compared to its source material. They argue that it is unfair to even call it a worthy adaptation. It is hard not to agree with them. An adaptation cannot be considered great if it is a far cry from its source material.

Similar to Stanley Kubrick’s The Shining and Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, Foundation fails as a book-to-screen adaptation on many levels.

However, if purely judged as a modern television space opera, Foundation is easily one of the best sci-fi indicates of our time. While Asimov's books seemingly argue that historical forces matter far more than individual stories of heroism, Apple TV's Foundation carefully bends the original narrative to focus on almost mythic heroes and solo characters. Sources indicate that it is also arguable that a direct page-to-page TV adaptation of Isaac Asimov's Foundation books would likely have failed. Whether Apple TV's adaptation approach is ideal is still debatable, but it at least gives us an epic sci-fi space opera.
