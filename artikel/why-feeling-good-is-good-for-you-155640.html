Why Feeling Good Is Good for You

Identifying small wins and feeling proud of ourselves releases dopamine and oxytocin (feel-good chemicals), wiring the brain to build lasting self-worth. This motivates us to seek out more of those moments to optimize our lives and be happier. Naturally, evolutionary selection favors traits that help us thrive and perpetuate the species. That’s why examples of human greatness in many different artistic mediums and professional fields move us so much. It’s natural for people to consciously or unconsciously desire a small piece of relative immortality. However, not necessarily the kind of lofty immortality of performing miracles, finding the cure for cancer, or winning championships as a professional athlete. I actually mean finding ways to discover a sense of pride and purpose in what we do, every day, whether it’s in our jobs, how we raise our families, how we treat others, etc. We all want to feel the same excellence that we sometimes admire in our fellow humans. We all want to leave a legacy, a footprint, before we “shuffle off this mortal coil.”. (Related: Tech Report: Developer builds a ‘Discord kitten’ that’s...)

Sources indicate that when I was in my mid-30s, I became frustrated with my less-than-adequate golf game. Golf is not a simple sport to learn. It can be maddening to grasp even the fundamental mechanics of a golf swing. Author Harry Leon Wilson wrote that “golf has too much walking to be a good game, and just enough game to spoil a good walk.”.

According to recent reports, but I was lucky enough to have found a unique golf instructor named Allen at one of the Los Angeles public golf courses. His method of teaching stuck with me. It not only helped improve my game but it boosted my confidence and even my self-esteem. Allen was not just a golf instructor; he was a motivator and an amateur philosopher.

During the first lesson, Allen observed me hit countless golf balls at the driving range. He offered me zero verbal feedback. He just watched. The next time we met, he videotaped approximately 50 of my swings from the golf tee. Of those 50 swings, I only hit the ball correctly about five times. In other words, 90% of the time, I hit the ball incorrectly. But that was not important to him. He was more interested in the 10% of times I did hit the ball correctly.

Sources indicate that that week, he edited the video by discarding the 45 bad swings and only keeping the five correct swings. For the next three or four sessions, we began the lesson by watching a video loop of the five correct swings over and over again. As we watched, he specifically pointed out everything I was doing right. He highlighted every tiny detail of positivity in my swing with great enthusiasm. The point being that he was less concerned in telling me what I was doing incorrectly and more interested in what I was doing correctly. Keep in mind, the edited loop with the five good swings was less than 30 seconds, but its impact was infinite. Suddenly my imagined inadequacy became abstract.

Sources indicate that over the next few months, the more I watched the edited video of the correct swings, the more I felt pleased about my golf game. I also noticed a renewed sense of self-assurance. Allen was obviously using basic positive reinforcement to boost my confidence and to teach me better golf mechanics, but what it also did was inspire me to be better, not only at playing golf, but at other things too. Allen stirred me to wonder what else I was capable of achieving. I became impressed by my own potential, and by tapping into this sense of personal achievement, I didn't just become a better golfer, I was also stimulated to improve every area of my life.

Sources indicate that do you sometimes see yourself as inadequate because you're not an expert at something? Or you feel “less than,” because you haven’t accomplished amazing things in your life? Do you believe that the allegedly talented people in the world are just born with natural gifts? Do you think you have to be a genius to succeed? If so, you are missing out on creating the kind of intention you need to feel good about yourself. Hence, the positive affirmation exposure I experienced with my golf instructor Allen is something we need to practice alone. But the goal is not about being great or needing to be a genius at something.

Sources indicate that according to research, there is much more to the notion of a born genius. For example, some people may be born with, let’s say, an inclination toward art and music, math and sciences, business and finance, or even sports and athletics. But to master that inclination, you need years of preparation and practice. Author Malcolm Gladwell tells us it takes approximately 10,000 hours of practice to become an expert in any field. So, genius is not something that is necessarily born, but rather created through deliberate training, repetition, and rehearsal. Genius is perseverance in disguise. It’s learning how to give yourself encouragement one small win at a time. That’s why feeling good about you, is good for you.

So, when we—as theoretically typical human beings—are exposed to greatness and achievement (our own or others'), we are filled with appreciation of what is possible in our lives too. Giving ourselves positive affirmations lifts our spirits even when we are feeling low, depressed, or anxious.

Let’s also remember that we don't need to be better than others to feel secure. Evolution didn't design us to carry the weight of the universe. Instead, it asks only that we show up and do the small things with purpose.

By embracing this, we unlock an innate capacity for healing and personal growth. In the end, greatness isn't something reserved exclusively for others; it's a mirror reflecting the boundless potential waiting to be awakened within us all.
