It has been revealed that the K-shaped economy strikes again—and this time it’s impacting the country’s ailing Social Security system. As you may have heard, Social Security’s trustees have estimated that unless the federal government acts soon, the retirement trust fund will become insolvent as early as the fourth quarter of 2032, in about six years, while the combined trust fund will run out in 2034—leaving Social Security able to fund only about 80% of promised benefits. The benefit cuts would affect tens of millions of Americans. Some 71 million—54 million retired workers and 9 million of their survivors and dependents—currently receive Social Security benefits. But what you may not know is the reason for the potential shortfall: Up until now, many critics and politicians have primarily blamed the program’s insolvency on the country’s growing older population, and mismanagement of the trust. (Republican Senator Ron Johnson from Wisconsin even called it “an illegal Ponzi scheme.”) But in a latest report, the AARP argues there is another main driver: changing income patterns, and the growing wealth inequality in the U.S. that’s eating into Social Security’s revenue base, which is primarily financed through payroll taxes applied to earnings up to a statutory cap. According to the report, those shifting income patterns have shortened the Social Security trust fund’s lifespan by 25 years. “Over time, a declining share of the nation’s gross domestic product has been subject to this [payroll] tax, as earnings above the cap have grown rapidly, driven by rising wages among high-income workers, whereas earnings below the cap have increased more slowly,” the AARP report claims. In 2027, earnings over $192,300 won’t be taxed for Social Security at all. “It implies the wealthiest Americans aren’t paying their fair share,” Robert Laura, a financial planner and founder of the Retirement Coaches Association, told Fast Firm. In a K-shaped economy, the gap between the richest and poorest widens, as high-income individuals continue to prosper while lower-income consumers increasingly struggle. As the AARP argues, that means less money is going toward funding Social Security, adding pressure on the trust’s finances. Vermont Senator Bernie Sanders has been trying to rally his colleagues to come up with a way to avoid a Social Security crisis when the 120th Congress convenes in January. Sanders recently reiterated his strategy, known as the “Bernie bump,” that would raise benefits “by at least $2,400 a year and extend Social Security’s solvency for generations.” If Sanders’s previous Social Security Expansion Act proposal passed, it would extend benefits beyond the current annual cost-of-living adjustments, which are meant to keep pace with inflation, and will increase payments 3.6% in 2027.
