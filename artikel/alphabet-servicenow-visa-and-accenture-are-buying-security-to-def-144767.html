Alphabet, ServiceNow, Visa and Accenture are buying security to defend their core

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, alphabet, ServiceNow, Visa and Accenture bought security companies to defend their core businesses. See what each deal buys and what is still a forecast.

For years, companies bought cybersecurity from specialist vendors the way they bought insurance: as a cost of doing business. That's changing. Some of the biggest buyers of security companies now are businesses whose main products are cloud computing, office software, card payments and consulting. (Related: My Journey to a serverless transformers pipeline on Goo...)

These buyers don't see security as a side business. They use it to make their core product harder to leave and harder to copy. A cloud provider that owns the tool protecting its cloud, or a payments network that owns the tool catching fraud, gives customers one more reason to stay. (Related: Tech Report: How we sped up transformer inference 100x ...)

Sources indicate that the catch, which the size of these deals can obscure, is timing. Each buyer pays now, but the return comes later, if it comes at all. Some of the payoff depends on customers adopting new tools across an entire network. Some depends on buyers getting their profit margins back after absorbing the companies they bought. And in at least one case, the deal hasn't even closed yet.

In a recent development, so the useful question isn't how massive each deal is. It's what the buyer gains by owning a security firm outright, and how long it'll take to find out whether the bet paid off.

24/7 Wall St. looked for the largest and most strategically important security acquisitions by companies outside the security industry. The buyers include a card network buying a company that studies how people type and swipe, a consulting giant moving into industrial security and a major cloud provider buying a company that protects workloads in the cloud. In each case, the buyer is spending on whatever it most needs to defend, and each deal below is judged on what it gains and how much of the payoff is still to come.

According to recent reports, google $GOOGL has finished buying Wiz. Now the question is whether it pays off. At $32 billion, Wiz is the biggest of these deals, and Google closed it on March 11, 2026, according to Cybersecurity Dive. The case for the price is that better security helps Google Cloud win more customer workloads, at a time when the business is booming: Google Cloud revenue grew 82% in the second quarter. Google is betting Wiz helps keep that growth going.

Sources indicate that but Wiz protects more than Google's cloud. It also secures customers on Amazon $AMZN Web Services, Microsoft $MSFT Azure and Oracle $ORCL Cloud, according to Cybersecurity Dive. That means Google is now a security vendor to companies running on its competitors' clouds.

Thomas Kurian, CEO of Google Cloud, framed the deal in those broader terms. "With the addition of Wiz, we will provide customers with a comprehensive platform to secure their cloud and hybrid environments, as well as accelerate threat prevention, detection and response," Kurian remarked.

According to recent reports, that creates a measurement problem for investors. Security Google sells to a company running on Amazon's cloud won't show up as a workload moving to Google, so much of the deal's success may not appear in the cloud growth numbers investors usually watch.

Sources indicate that wiz also rounds out a security business Google had already built through Mandiant and its Chronicle security operations tools. Now Google can sell cloud protection, Mandiant consulting and security operations tools under one roof.

Whether it pays off depends on how many companies, including those on Amazon's and Microsoft's clouds, decide they want Google protecting them.

Recent findings suggest that serviceNow $NOW sells software that moves work through large companies, and it was selling security to its biggest customers before it bought a single security firm. Security was part of 16 of its 20 largest customer deals in the second quarter, according to 24/7 Wall St. In other words, its acquisitions followed demand that already existed.

ServiceNow then bought two companies to handle a security incident from start to finish. Veza makes tools that show and control who and what can reach a company's data, apps and AI agents, according to ServiceNow. Armis tracks nearly 7 billion devices. The bigger of the two deals, Armis, cost about $7.75 billion.

Both deals are recent. Veza closed on March 2, 2026, and Armis closed on April 20, after the first quarter ended. Any boost from Armis is still largely something ServiceNow expects rather than something it has shown.

The deal also comes with a cost. ServiceNow expects Armis to weigh on profit margins in fiscal 2026 and doesn't expect margin growth to return to normal until fiscal 2027. And some of Armis' revenue is less locked in than it looks, since some customers can cancel their contracts for convenience.

ServiceNow bought what its customers were asking for. Now it's paying in profits while it waits to see whether those customers stick around long enough to make it worthwhile.

According to recent reports, visa $V is buying security to protect the business it already runs. It's paying $2.4 billion for BioCatch to cut fraud losses in its payments network, according to 24/7 Wall St. The deal was declared in August and is expected to close by March 2027.

This isn't Visa turning into a security tech giant. It's buying a better way to block fraud on its own payments, building on its 2024 purchase of fraud detection firm Featurespace. BioCatch tracks how people type, tap and swipe, so a bank can flag an impostor even when they enter the right account details. Andrew Torre, Visa's president of value-added services, claims BioCatch will help Visa's clients catch fraud earlier, before any money moves.

For a payments network, every fraud stopped before checkout is one less bad transaction that could cost Visa the trust of cardholders and banks.

Recent findings suggest that the catch is reach. BioCatch counts roughly 350 banks as customers, according to company figures reported by CNBC, while Visa connects nearly 14,500 financial institutions. Visa only gets the fraud savings it wants if many more of those institutions sign on.

The size of the problem explains the price tag. Scams and account takeovers cost more than $1 trillion worldwide each year, according to a Visa estimate reported by CNBC, far more than what Visa is paying. And BioCatch isn't pretending the industry has it under control. "The reality is, as a society and industry, we are not winning this fight," the company wrote in a blog post about the deal.

Visa is betting it can get ahead of a kind of fraud that even the manufacturer it's buying notes the industry is losing to.

Accenture $ACN spent roughly $4.18 billion to add industrial security products to a business that mostly makes money from consultants, according to 24/7 Wall St. It's hardly new to security: Its cybersecurity business already brings in around $10 billion in revenue, according to Silicon Republic.

It has been revealed that what's new is the target. Operational technology security protects the systems that run factories, plants and other industrial operations, not office computers. Here, Accenture wants to own the tools instead of advising clients on tools other companies make.

Julie Sweet, Accenture's CEO and chair, says clients across industries want to get ahead of attacks and treat security as one coordinated effort. Owning the products lets Accenture sell that as a single package instead of stitching together tools from outside vendors.

Rather than folding the deals into its consulting practice, Accenture is building a separate product unit around Dragos. It now holds a majority stake in Dragos and owns runZero and NetRise outright. Dragos will keep running as its own business, and three executives from the two smaller companies are joining it, combining all three into one industrial security unit.

According to recent reports, worth noting: The $4.18 billion covers all three deals, and one of them is only a controlling stake. It's not the price of owning three companies in full.

Sources indicate that the price is a bet on growth, not current earnings. Accenture expected the three companies to bring in about $208 million in annual recurring revenue as of June 2026. That's small next to the security business Accenture already runs. Sweet herself expects a slow payoff, saying the deal "may not make as major a contribution ... in the first couple of years.".

Sources indicate that for now, what Accenture owns is a plan for an industrial security products business. The price will only look smart if that business gets big.
