A synthetic user is a large language model told to answer as a particular person. The tool writes a persona (a job title, age, income, location, sometimes a personality profile or notes from real interviews), asks your question, and records the answer. Then it does that dozens or hundreds of times with different personas and adds the answers up.

In a recent development, this is the paper people quote when they say synthetic users work. The authors, from PyMC Labs and Colgate-Palmolive, took 57 concept surveys "supplied by a leading personal care corporation", with 9,300 real responses, and asked LLM personas the same purchase-intent question (arXiv 2510.08338).

The finding that matters is about method. When they asked the model for a 1 to 5 rating directly, it mostly answered "3" and rarely used 1 or 5, while real people mostly picked 4 and 5. The distribution match was poor: a KS similarity of 0.26 for GPT-4o (full paper). So they had the model answer in words, then scored those words against reference statements by meaning. They call this semantic similarity rating (SSR). With SSR, GPT-4o reached 90% "correlation attainment", meaning its ranking of the 57 concepts agreed with humans about 90% as well as one half of the human panel agreed with the other half. Distribution similarity rose to 0.88.

Sources indicate that two more results are easy to miss. Without demographic personas, the model's average purchase intent still matched humans, but its ability to tell good concepts from bad ones fell to 50%, against 92% with personas. And it reproduced age and income patterns but not gender or region.

Recent findings suggest that the Harvard and Microsoft team prompted GPT as "a customer randomly selected while shopping" and repeated each prompt hundreds of times (slides, Israeli, May 2024). Demand sloped down as price rose, income mattered, and a conjoint study put willingness to pay for fluoride in toothpaste at $3.40, close to the $3.27 a human study found. The 10,800 responses cost $3 and took 35 minutes.

It has been revealed that the same slides flag that GPT "gravitates toward the first option presented". And the later version of the paper adds a hard limit: the model needs fine-tuning on prior human results to predict willingness to pay for newly introduced features, and fine-tuning "may not improve LLMs ability to extrapolate to unrelated product categories" (MSI working paper, 2025).

NN/g ran the commercial tool Synthetic Users and ChatGPT against three studies it had already done with real people (NN/g, 2024). Real learners admitted they'd quit online courses. Synthetic ones mentioned "Yes, I completed all the courses I mentioned." Real learners mostly avoided discussion forums. Synthetic ones said forums mattered. Synthetic users produced long lists of needs with no sense of which ones counted, and liked concepts because the model tends to please whoever's asking.

NN/g's 2025 follow-up reviewed three more academic studies and landed in the same place: AI-simulated customers can fill data gaps and forecast population-level trends, but should "complement, not replace, human-centered research" (NN/g, 2025).

It has been revealed that park et al. interviewed 1,052 people for two hours each and built an AI version of every one. Built from interviews plus surveys, the twins matched their person's General Social Survey answers 72.8% of the time. The people themselves only matched their own answers two weeks later 79.5% of the time. Twins built from demographics alone managed 60.4%, and the authors say demographic-prompt simulations "often misjudge individuals and distort sample-level patterns.".

Notice the pattern. Synthetic people do best on aggregate questions ("which of these concepts will people prefer?") in categories with lots of public data. They do worst on individual depth ("why did this person quit?") and on anything the model hasn't seen.

Use them where the research says they hold up, and build in the fixes the papers found.

In a 57-survey study with 9,300 real respondents, AI consumers ranked personal care concepts at about 90% of the consistency real panels have with themselves. But Nielsen Norman Group found synthetic interview answers too shallow and too agreeable to replace real customers. Use them to rank and stress-test options, then confirm with real buyers.

A large language model is given a persona, like a job, age, income or a set of past interview notes, and asked to answer as that person. The tool repeats this many times with different personas and adds up the answers. Better tools ground personas in real data and ask for free-text reactions instead of forcing a number.

Recent findings suggest that NN/g re-ran three of its own real-user studies with synthetic users and found the answers shallow, over-positive and lacking priorities. Its advice is to use synthetic users for desk research and hypotheses, treat the output as hypotheses to test, and never present it as real-user research.
