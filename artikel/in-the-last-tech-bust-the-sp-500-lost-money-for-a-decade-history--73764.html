Industry observers note that the AI trade seems to be unwinding fast. The Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -5.28%) was recently down more than 15% from its early October peak, while the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) was approaching correction territory, with a roughly 10% slump from its all-time high. In a recent development, however, history offers a useful reference for what held up the last time a technology boom ended. From 2000 to 2009, a period that featured the dot-com crash, the S&P 500 posted negative returns, averaging a 0.8% annual decline. High-yielding dividend stocks, on the other hand, delivered an average annual total return of 5.4% over the same period, according to a Wellington Management study published by Hartford Funds. Here's what investors can learn from this history lesson.

In a recent development, the dot-com bubble burst in March 2000. It led many stock investors to return to fundamentals, focusing on metrics such as a manufacturer's price-to-earnings ratio and dividend yield instead of future monetization potential. Companies that generated real revenue and profits, and returned a portion of their income to investors via dividends, delivered the best returns during this period.

Sources indicate that wellington sorted dividend-paying stocks into five groups by yield, from the top 20% of payers to the bottom 20%. In the 2000s, the highest-yielding group gained 5.4% a year, while the lowest-yielding payers lost 1.7% a year. To put this in dollars, $1,000 compounding at 5.4% for 10 years grows to about $1,690, while $1,000 shrinking at 0.8% per year ends up near $923.

The decade before the dot-com bust looked very different for high-yielding dividend stocks. In the 1990s, the lowest-yielding payers kept pace with the S&P 500, while the highest-yielding group trailed it by a meaningful amount. Meanwhile, the last six years look a lot like the late 1990s. Since 2020, the lowest-yielding payers have beaten the market, while the highest-yielding group has trailed. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

The highest-yielding dividend stocks have outperformed the market in five of 10 time periods from 1930 to 2025. That's the same percentage as the lowest yielders. The best performers were companies with higher dividend yields, though not the highest. These companies prioritized dividend payments (and growth) without sacrificing financial flexibility. This cohort outperformed the S&P 500 in six of those 10 periods, including in the 2000s (4.4% annualized return). They also delivered solid performance leading up to the dot-com crash (15.6% returns in the 1990s).

Here are three practical tips investors can employ in their own portfolios based on the data on dividend stocks:.

A high yield can be as much a warning as a reward. A stock's yield rises when its price falls, and sometimes the market is pricing in a dividend cut. The data on cutters show how costly that can be. From 1973 through 2025, S&P 500 companies that cut or eliminated their dividends returned an average of -1% a year, compared with 10.2% for companies that raised theirs and 7.7% for the average stock, according to Ned Davis Research data published by Hartford. Cutters were also the most volatile group.

Sources indicate that the last time a technology boom ended, the stocks that paid the most were the ones that held up best, while the lowest-yielding payers lost more than the market. That doesn't mean history will repeat itself, and the biggest yields carry their own risks, as dividend cutters show. However, the data makes a case for adding some yield to your portfolio before the next cycle turns.
