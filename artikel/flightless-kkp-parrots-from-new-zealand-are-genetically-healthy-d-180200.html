Flightless kākāpō parrots from New Zealand are genetically healthy despite 10,000 years of inbreeding

Scientists sequenced the DNA of Upcoming Zealand's endangered kākāpō and found that 10,000 years of inbreeding actually helped natural selection clear out harmful genetic mutations.

Industry observers note that on New Zealand’s Stewart Island, flightless parrots called kākāpō spent about 10,000 years cut off from the rest of their species, breeding with relatives. The researchers wrote that the finding is evidence a small population can survive hundreds of generations of isolation. The species is still critically endangered, though, and many of its eggs fail to hatch.

Nicolas Dussex, a researcher at the Centre for Palaeogenetics in Stockholm, led the study. Few birds anywhere are as inbred or as endangered as the kākāpō, Dussex said.

Kākāpō likely numbered in the hundreds of thousands before people reached New Zealand. Overhunting and predators introduced by people reduced them to 51 birds by 1995.

In a recent development, fifty came from Stewart Island. The other was a mainland male named Richard Henry.

When the study came out, 201 kākāpō were alive, all on island sanctuaries.

In a recent development, for 49 kākāpō, Dussex’s team analyzed each bird’s complete set of DNA, called its genome. Thirty-five were Stewart Island birds.

The rest were mainland birds: Richard Henry and 13 museum specimens. Those specimens were collected between 1847 and 1924.

An inbred bird’s DNA has a recognizable pattern. A chick gets one copy of its genome from each parent. When the parents are related, long stretches of the two copies match.

Recent findings suggest that in the Stewart Island birds, matching stretches made up 53% of the genome on average. In the mainland birds, matching stretches made up 15%.

It has been revealed that so the island birds were far more inbred. The team then counted the most damaging kind of mutation, the kind predicted to stop a gene from working.

Mainland birds carried 34.1 of those mutations on average. Island birds carried 17.6 of them, about half as many.

It has been revealed that many of these mutations seem to do harm mainly when a bird inherits the same one from both parents. That happens more often when the parents are related.

Chicks that inherit the mutation from both parents are less likely to survive or breed, the team suggested. Over many generations, the mutation becomes rarer. Geneticists call that process purging.

Industry observers note that the team tested the idea with computer simulations spanning 25,000 years. A simulated population that stayed small, like Stewart Island’s, ended up with fewer harmful mutations than a larger one like the mainland’s.

It has been revealed that but in a simulated population one-tenth as large as the island’s, the harm from mutations grew instead. So purging seems to need enough birds for natural selection to work.

After the kākāpō’s recent decline, the team wrote, the birds may now face the same growing harm as that smallest population.

Conservation managers have favored matings with Richard Henry and his offspring, to help the population keep its ability to adapt.

“We show that the single male survivor from the mainland, Richard Henry, has more harmful mutations than Stewart Island birds,” mentioned Love Dalén of the Swedish Museum of Natural History, who supervised the study.

“Therefore, there could be a risk that these harmful mutations spread in future generations.”.

Richard Henry was also genetically distinct, Dalén added, and may have carried useful genetic diversity. The team recommended weighing that benefit against the risk and, if necessary, limiting breeding between the two family lines.

Dussex’s team counted mutations in DNA and didn’t measure what they do to a living bird. Their effects on survival and breeding weren’t known.

The team called for research into the genetic causes of the kākāpō’s low fertility and hatching success.

In some other shrinking species, harmful mutations increased as numbers fell, the authors wrote. In others, the mutations decreased.

Next, the team planned to examine other highly inbred birds and mammals, to learn whether the kākāpō is a rare exception.

The full study was published in the journal Cell Genomics.
