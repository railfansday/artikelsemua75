Exclusive: Guards told their jobs will be transferred to a private contractor that has rejected union recognition.

The British Library has sparked outrage over intends to outsource its security workforce and hand over to a private manufacturer three of its public reading rooms, to be “repurposed” as business spaces for hire – despite having reportedly received millions of pounds from the public purse to refurbish those spaces. Almost 70 guards have been told that their jobs will be transferred to a private contractor, STM, on 1 December. Their union, the PCS, has in turn been informed that STM has rejected union recognition for those staff.

The union is balloting for strike action over what it describes as management’s “appalling approach to industrial relations”.

Nick Marro, the PCS culture group secretary, remarked serious questions around public money had been sparked because the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport was understood to be investing up to £14m in renovating the British Library’s reading rooms,“which the British Library is then going to immediately privatise”.

Marro said: “That’s absolutely ridiculous. We believe this raises notable questions about the future of publicly funded cultural institutions, the treatment of workers and the government’s own commitments on insourcing and trade union rights.”.

The library is proposing to close three of its 11 reading rooms – the science and social science spaces – turning them into a privately operated “flexible business workspace”. Sources believe this will involve the loss of 340 reader desks. (Related: Hosting your Models and Datasets on Hugging Face Spaces...)

As one of the world’s most notable research libraries, the British Library holds more than 150m items, including a copy of Magna Carta. Security staff affected by the latest looks include guards, supervisors and managers. Some patrol the reading rooms; others monitor CCTV.

Fran Heathcote, the PCS general secretary, has written to Lisa Nandy, the culture secretary, calling for her “swift intervention”, saying that outsourcing is “in defiance of government policy”.

The letter, seen by the Guardian, reads: “What on earth is a government department doing investing in a resource only to privatise it, and outsourcing workers and stripping them of union recognition?”.

She pointed out that at the Labour party conference, Louise Haigh, the first secretary, had announced an insourcing unit that promised to deliver on the pre-election pledge of “the biggest wave of insourcing in a generation”.

The PCS is questioning why the library is proposing to privatise reading rooms that are a vital resource for students and academics, among others.

Industry observers note that on its website, the library notes a “huge demand in London for high-quality workspace”, while arguing that it is “running a financial deficit”. It says: “This year we expect to spend around £7m more than our total income … At the same time, research behaviours have changed. As online demand increases, in-person visits to our reading rooms have dropped.”.

It estimates that the aims will affect about 20% of the more than 1,400 reading room desks across the St Pancras site, and that “the income generated by the upcoming flexible business workspaces will make a vital contribution towards ensuring that our core library services – including reading room access – remain free to consumers”.

Charles Darwin is among famous scientists who have used the British Library, whether in its previous home in Bloomsbury or its current site in St Pancras. Marro noted that these scientists would be turning in their graves over the latest plans, while today’s scientists would also be appalled as it was “already difficult to get a desk at the reading rooms”.

Marro added: “Any time I’m in the building, it’s hard to find a place to work. The library has pointed to falling use in the past five or 10 years, but that’s slightly disingenuous as there was a catastrophic security attack where they lost complete access to their systems and of course there was a drop in use when nobody could access it.

“The library has not stated that any jobs are at immediate risk of redundancy. However they have refused to rule out redundancies or a restructure in the future. It’s impossible to envision a situation in which they’re going to be closing reading rooms and losing hundreds of reader desks, without redundancies.”.

Although the British Library’s chief executive, Paul Bate, revealed the DCMS’s multimillion-pound refurbishment funding to dozens of staff members who attended a meeting last week, the DCMS yesterday denied that it has been allocated.

But documents seen by the Guardian include an internal slide from a British Library presentation delivered to staff, which states: “The cost of fitting out the space is £8m to £13m.”.

Insiders remarked that the DCMS denial could mean that the funding simply had not been formally allocated yet.

Industry observers note that the British Library declined to confirm the figures, initially suggesting that it does not recognise them. It stated that its “commitment to science remains steadfast”, adding: “The new space will enable businesses, researchers, entrepreneurs and innovators to work and collaborate at the heart of London’s Knowledge Quarter, while generating income that can be reinvested in the library.”.

STM has been contacted for comment.
