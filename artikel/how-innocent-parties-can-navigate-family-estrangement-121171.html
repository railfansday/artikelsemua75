How 'Innocent Parties' Can Navigate Family Estrangement

The holidays are upon us, and with them comes the annual anxiety over family encounters. The messaging we receive is all about traditions, togetherness, and reunions, whereas many families experience grief, loneliness, and more due to estrangement within those families.

We go through it every year, and I can say candidly, as a therapist, it doesn’t get a lot easier. But whereas most of the articles and advice columns we read address the family members experiencing the friction—the son who won’t reconcile with his father, the sister who can’t forgive her brother—there are a lot of people gravely affected by the estrangement even though they do not actively take part in it. These are estrangement’s forgotten people.

Recent findings suggest that when two or more relatives cut off contact, the people caught in the middle often absorb the emotional fallout. They get pulled into triangulation in different ways, depending on the specifics of the family dynamics and situation. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Recent findings suggest that one of my clients, John, experiences this triangulation every year at the holidays. His mother and sister haven’t spoken to each other in years, having fallen out due to the sister’s choice of husband. John loves and is in close communication with both, and this works—more or less—during the year; but the holidays bring greater expectations of “togetherness,” and he feels forced to make decisions, none of which are satisfying to him. Does he choose one holiday party over the other? Manage somehow to rush from one to the other to make both parties happy? Eschew celebrations with both of them?

His mother and sister pull him into their dynamic by each pressuring John to prove his loyalty to each of them by attending their gathering. When he does, he feels forced to take sides, as each of them used the time to vent about the other. When he refuses to engage in this negative talk, there’s fallout from that, too.

According to recent reports, other families handle the triangulation differently. Rebecca’s large extended family has effectively shunned her cousin Peter after he came out as gay. But while they’ve cut off direct communication with him, they still expect Rebecca to act as the go-between, wanting to make sure Peter gets all their news and, again, passing on subtle negative messages to Peter through Rebecca.

Industry observers note that no one isn’t affected by estrangement, and families often seek to keep tabs on the “missing” relative or relatives. It can be as blatant as, “Where is Dad spending Thanksgiving?” or as subtle as, “Have you talked to your sister recently?” My client Ellen claims this kind of questioning makes her feel like she has to edit everything she says, so that she doesn’t open doors to becoming the conduit, and so that she doesn’t accidentally betray any trust the missing family member has placed in her. Not exactly a jolly family gathering.

And the result is that the family member who is absent from the dinner table gets as much airtime as everybody else.

Recent findings suggest that it helps to remind yourself that you’re not the one who chose the estrangement, and you don’t have to be the stand-in family member. But as we all know, that knowledge isn’t going to make everything all right. Chances are, your people-pleasing tendencies get fired up now, and your choices need to be balanced with what you need and want, and how you want your own family to experience the holidays.

It may help to name the boundary you’re setting. “Remember, I spent last Thanksgiving with you, and I’m planning on going to Jason’s house this year. Maybe we can get together sometime next week to share leftovers.”.

In a recent development, this may not have been the message your family of origin gave you, but it’s true for you now: You get to decide. You can set the boundaries you need, and you can reflect them calmly to family members who don’t want to honor them.

According to recent reports, for example, you might say to the information-seeking family member, “You're both important to me, so I don't want to talk about either of you with the other. Now, tell me about what you did this week.".

Industry observers note that pausing before answering can also be a great tool. When someone catches you off-guard with a comment or question about a missing family member, taking a breath may help you to not immediately try to smooth things over.

In a recent development, some mental-health practitioners encourage non-contact between family members who have fallen out over an event or a belief. That’s a topic for a different article, but I have to say that as a family therapist who works to improve communication, I’m optimistic that challenges within families are reparable to a point where agreeing to disagree and coming up with boundaries and limits can be incorporated into the family system and members can be in contact.

In a recent development, the invisible family members do not—and in fact should not—have to be the vehicles for reconciliation, but staying hopeful that it could happen will ease some of your feelings of frustration and resentment. Always staying open to that possibility can help you cope.

The best thing you can do for yourself in these situations is to get in the habit of establishing and maintaining the boundaries you choose and not succumbing to curiosity or hostility in the moment.

For those of us who want everybody to just get along—that’s good. Encourage your family members to meet with a mediator or therapist. It isn’t your job.

And always remember that it’s perfectly reasonable for you to have a close relationship on your own with each party and enjoy them for who they are.
