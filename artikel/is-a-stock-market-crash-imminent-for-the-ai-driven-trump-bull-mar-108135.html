In a recent development, but things may not be as ideal for Wall Street as the major stock indexes have made them appear. History suggests the probability of a stock market crash occurring and driving the AI-driven Trump bull market notably lower is climbing by the day.

Sources indicate that there's no argument that AI is the hottest trend on Wall Street since the advent and proliferation of the internet. Empowering software and systems to make autonomous decisions is a $22.5 trillion addressable opportunity through 2031, according to IDC, and it's the primary catalyst that's lifted the Trump bull market to upcoming heights.

According to recent reports, but history also demonstrates that every next-massive-thing technology since (and including) the advent of the internet in the mid-1990s has navigated a bubble-bursting event. These bubbles repeatedly form and burst because investors persistently overestimate the pace of adoption and/or optimization of upcoming technologies.

Stock market bubbles throughout history…AI stocks now ~40% of the market. pic.twitter.com/RxSAh09k6F.

To be clear, AI doesn't have an adoption problem. The amount of capital being thrown around by neoclouds and hyperscalers shows that AI has been welcomed with open arms.

However, the businesses deploying AI solutions aren't particularly close to optimizing them. It took until several years after the dot-com bubble burst for internet-driven companies to maximize this technology to boost their sales and profits, and we're likely looking at a similar timeline for AI, heightening the odds of a bubble-driven stock market crash.

Premium stock valuations are another reason the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite could endure elevator-down moves in the presumed not-too-distant future.

In a recent development, on the one hand, valuation is a tricky subject to tackle given that there isn't a one-size-fits-all way to evaluate individual companies or the broader market. Some combination of investor subjectivity and emotion leads to unpredictability on Wall Street.

Stock Market Shiller PE Ratio on the verge of taking out its Dot Com Bubble all-time high 🚨 🤯 👀 pic.twitter.com/CtCmSgWnLt.

It has been revealed that on the other hand, the S&P 500's Shiller Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio, also known as the Cyclically Adjusted P/E Ratio (CAPE Ratio), is time-vetted and leaves little room for interpretation. The stock market entered this year at its second-highest valuation since January 1871 and only became more expensive throughout the summer.

The S&P 500's CAPE Ratio has exceeded 30 only six times over 156 years, including the present, and the previous five instances were all eventually followed by declines of 20% to 89% in the Dow Jones Industrial Average, S&P 500, and/or Nasdaq Composite. While the Shiller P/E Ratio doesn't tell investors when these downturns will begin or if a crash will occur, it does have a knack for foreshadowing substantial downside.

Wall Street's crudest measure of investors' willingness to take risks also points to a heightened probability of a stock market crash.

Recent findings suggest that every month, FINRA publishes the aggregated outstanding margin debt at all brokerages. Margin represents the money investors borrow, with interest, to short-sell or purchase securities. When it's used to buy stocks or exchange-traded funds, it acts as leverage.

Recent findings suggest that investing on margin can be incredibly risky. It can magnify your gains if a security moves in the desired direction, but also rapidly amplify losses if it moves in the opposite direction.

Total Margin Debt hits $1.5 Trillion, a upcoming all-time high 🤯 👀 pic.twitter.com/1IXqZGgrqs.

According to recent reports, in June 2026, outstanding margin debt hit a record high of $1.502 trillion. While margin debt is expected to steadily climb with the overall stock market over time, parabolic moves higher over short time frames have historically been bad news. Between April 2025 and June 2026, margin debt soared 77%, marking the fourth time it's skyrocketed by at least 65% since 1998 over the span of just 12 to 19 months.

Industry observers note that the prior three occurrences immediately preceded the bursting of the dot-com bubble in 2000, the start of the financial crisis in 2007, and the onset of the 2022 bear market. Some of these events featured crash-like moves.

While none of these three history-backed events or metrics guarantee that a Trump bull market crash is imminent, the odds of a jaw-dropping downturn appear to be growing by the day.
