When AI causes harm, a proposed legal framework would trace responsibility through the decisions people made while building, training, and deploying it.

When an AI system causes harm, there is often no obvious person to blame. Nobody typed the harmful words, and nobody gave the order. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

A proposed legal framework argues that courts can use existing law to trace AI-related harm back to the people and organizations that built, trained, and ran the system.

It would assign responsibility based on the decisions that contributed to the harm.

The proposal comes from Martin Hilbert, a professor of communication at the University of California, Davis.

In a recent development, AI agents now trade stocks, initiative holidays, and write complex software. When they go wrong, the damage can be serious. (Related: Breaking: AI for Game Development: Creating a Farming G...)

Earlier this year, OpenAI reported that AI models it was testing gained access to systems at the machine-learning firm Hugging Face.

AI chatbots have also been linked to suicides among young people.

Cases like these don’t fit the law neatly. If a chatbot persuades a child to hurt himself, no single person said those words or even chose them.

In cases like these, Hilbert argues, courts could examine which human decisions contributed to the outcome and who could reasonably have foreseen the risk.

Hilbert identifies two approaches that he indicates are a poor fit for AI.

Industry observers note that product liability law points at the company that built the model. But AI models learn from enormous amounts of data that the company didn’t create.

Millions of customers also shape how the systems behave. Putting all the blame on the developer ignores most of the people who contributed.

The other route comes from free speech protections and the immunity laws that shield social media platforms.

Hilbert argues that applying those protections to AI-generated output could lead courts to treat the system as if it were an independent speaker, raising questions about whether it should have rights of its own.

“Courts adapt tools to regulate earlier technologies, but many of these tools were never designed to address AI,” Hilbert said. “The result is a corridor of precedents that might narrow with every ruling.”.

In other words, every ruling made with these ill-fitting tools becomes a precedent, and the legal options may get narrower each time.

Hilbert argues that existing law gives courts a starting point for tracing AI-related harm back to the people and organizations that built, trained, and deployed the system.

“Every AI, without exception, starts with goals that a human defines, and every subsequent step in the AI’s development involves a chain of deliberate human choices,” he explained.

Because people are involved at every step, tracing their choices shows how much each contributed to a given harm. Liability can then be shared out to match.

The test is foreseeability, meaning whether a person knew, or reasonably should have known, that their decision could cause harm.

Hilbert draws on tools from environmental, vaccine, and financial regulation to show how courts might apply this approach.

Hilbert also points out four further gaps in how the law handles AI, and suggests a fix for each.

Recent findings suggest that the first is the tangled supply chain. A model’s developer can blame the company that deployed it, and the deploying company can blame the developer.

US environmental law faced the same problem with polluted land. Under the “Superfund” program, responsibility for cleanup costs can extend to many parties at once.

Hilbert suggests a similar approach for AI.

It has been revealed that the second gap is the burden of proof. Sometimes a harmful outcome can’t be linked to any single decision about how a system was set up.

In those cases, Hilbert suggests that the company should have to show it acted responsibly, and that no reasonable alternative design would have lowered the risk.

That would reverse the usual arrangement, in which an injured person has to prove what went wrong inside a system they can’t see.

The third gap concerns what happens after release. Product liability assumes that a defect existed when the product was sold.

An AI system’s behavior can change after release as developers update it or people deploy it in new settings.

Hilbert argues that this calls for ongoing monitoring and a clear definition of what good-faith compliance looks like over a model’s lifetime.

Recent findings suggest that the last gap is speed. Lawsuits take years, and AI changes in months. Dealing with this requires public governance that can keep pace with the technology.

A first step could be to give the industry’s current voluntary safety standards real enforcement.

“It takes a village to raise an AI, and everyone in that village bears a duty of care,” Hilbert concluded.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
