Waymo pulling its robotaxis off freeways (again) and Zoox robotaxis launching on the Uber app illustrate the push and pull between progress and setbacks in the autonomous vehicle industry.

According to recent reports, there are signs of growth. For instance, Tesla has now surpassed Waymo in Texas in the number of robotaxis registered with the state (Tesla has 1,507 and Waymo has 1,345). But does that translate to market dominance?

According to recent reports, A better metric, if we’re trying to gauge who is leading, would be commercial driverless miles. And if we’re measuring cost-efficiency, another useful metric would be driverless miles per vehicle. By those measures, Waymo is still ahead, based on data available to me. But with Waymo’s recent freeways setback and Tesla’s push to expand its robotaxi fleet with Cybercabs, that lead could shrink.

Recent findings suggest that not that there needs to be a winner, by the way. Transportation is deeply fragmented, and there is room for multiple players in the U.S., not to mention the rest of the world. (Related: GTA 6 players report game-breaking crashes on PS5 and X...)

Recent findings suggest that A recent discussion with my Autonocast co-host Ed Niedermeyer, whose book, “Elon Take the Wheel,” comes out December 1, got me thinking about how Las Vegas could soon become a real world A/B test of sorts for human-driven vs. autonomous ride-hailing services.

Industry observers note that uber, as mentioned above, just added Zoox to its ride-hailing app in Las Vegas. For now, riders don’t have much of a choice. Uber customers can increase their chances of getting a Zoox robotaxi, and they can always decline a robot ride. But they can’t directly select a robotaxi. Instead, Uber riders will be matched with one if it’s available. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

Uber stated that will soon change. The company plans to introduce a dedicated autonomous option in its app as “availability and reliability improve,” Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi said during the company’s third-quarter earnings call in November.

Las Vegas will also become the first U.S. city where riders can access two fully driverless partners through the Uber app, he remarked. (Hyundai’s self-driving subsidiary Motional is also an Uber partner.).

In a recent development, that would give Uber riders a meaningful choice: a human driver or a robotaxi, and potentially even which autonomous service to use. And it could offer a revealing look at what customers actually prefer when given that choice. Price could tip the balance, too.

It makes me wonder whether Las Vegas will become the next battleground for organized labor as robotaxis begin competing directly with human drivers.

You might have seen the Wall Street Journal scoop that General Motors is working with an executive search firm to identify possible candidates to replace CEO Mary Barra. Rumors of her imminent departure have swirled for months, but this time there appears to be real work happening behind the scenes.

Sources indicate that I reached out to a few little birds to learn more and here’s what they’re telling me. And, for the record, GM declined to comment.

GM has hired executive search firm Heidrick & Struggles, according to one source, who noted it’s possible that the board has other executive search firms working on this as well. The board is taking a two-track approach to its search, which is occurring simultaneously.

It has been revealed that the first is focused on traditional yet transformative automotive CEOs. One name that has come up from the executive search firm is José Muñoz, who is president and CEO of Hyundai. The board is also interested in someone less traditional. The firm has apparently floated names like Doug Field, who recently became CEO of Mind Robotics, and Larry Culp of GE, according to a source with direct information.

There are internal GM folks who are also in the mix, including Sterling Anderson, co-founder of self-driving trucks firm Aurora who became chief product officer at GM in May 2025, and Paul Jacobson, the company’s executive vice president and chief financial officer.

Elysian Aircraft, the Dutch aerospace manufacturer developing aviation electrification technologies, raised €12 million in private and public funding from FideFlux, SecFund, and ROM InWest, as well as from individual investors and family offices.

Mahindra Last Mile Mobility, an electric three-wheeler business under Mahindra & Mahindra, is looking at an IPO in about 18 months, per Bloomberg.

Numa, a startup developing an AI operating system for dealerships, raised a $45 million Series C round led by New York-based Blue Cloud Ventures. Threshold Ventures, Costanoa, Touring Capital, Gradient Ventures, and Tokio Marine Future Fund also participated.

It has been revealed that stark Future, a European electric motorcycle company, raised at least €150 million in equity, Reuters reported. The raise was led by Highland Europe, alongside Morgan Stanley Investment Management’s 1GT and Axon Partners Group.

Vincue, an automotive tech manufacturer that sells software to dealerships, raised $93 million in a round led by Wavecrest Growth Partners, with continued backing from FM Capital.

Also, the electric micromobility startup that spun out of Rivian last year, has paused deliveries of its fresh e-bike over problems with its software and batteries, The Verge reported.

Edmunds released new data that found the average new vehicle is selling for a record $50,087.

Redwood Materials is using computer vision and machine learning to identify and sort batteries by chemistry before they’re fed into the organization’s rotary calciner — a massive cylindrical industrial furnace used to process 40,000 metric tons of raw feedstock a year.

Tesla said it will spend about $3.3 billion to expand its Berlin gigafactory to produce Cybercabs — yet another signal of the company’s global autonomy ambitions.

Over the weekend, Tesla invited “select customers” to preorder the tech giant’s autonomous, golden Cybercab “for personal use.” The Cybercab is priced at $29,990, but there is a lot more to ownership. The company will charge owners $299 a month to use the vehicles, delivery will only occur in a handful of cities, and operations will be limited to specific locations.

In a recent development, uber opened its ride-hailing service to children as young as 10 in three U.S. cities. Uber already has a service for teenagers. If this program proves successful, it would open Uber up to another market of customers: busy families.

Meanwhile, Uber noted it’s notifying close to 150,000 drivers that their personal information was stolen in an October data breach.

Sources indicate that zipline continues to scale its autonomous drone delivery business, this time in its partnership with Uber. The companies had previously revealed a partnership for drones to deliver for Uber Eats, but it officially launched this week in a Houston suburb. The aim is a big one: Uber and Zipline say they aim to make 1 million deliveries a day using the startup’s drones by the end of 2029.
