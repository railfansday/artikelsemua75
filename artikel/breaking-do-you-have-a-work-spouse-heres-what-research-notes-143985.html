Last week, I found myself running against one of my closest friends for an elected leadership position in a professional organization.

When it was my turn to introduce myself, I blurted out that my opponent was my "work spouse" and that I would be absolutely thrilled if they won. And I meant it (although they may have wished I'd not introduced them in that way). (Related: Latest Update: How to train a newly introduced language...)

It has been revealed that the two of us collaborate on research, are currently co-editing a book, and have developed a friendship that extends well beyond our professional responsibilities. We celebrate each other's accomplishments, offer advice, and understand the sometimes ridiculous aspects of academia that can be difficult to explain to anyone outside it.

It has been revealed that just as I was about to post a selfie of us on Instagram with a quick little caption about appreciating my work spouse, I remembered that my dear friend, the late Dr. Chad McBride, had actually published research on this very topic. So, naturally, I decided to blog about it instead.

Sources indicate that you've probably heard someone refer to a colleague as their "work wife" or "work husband." Maybe you've even used the term yourself. But what makes someone a work spouse rather than simply a friend or colleague at work?

In a recent development, in 2015, communication scholars M. Chad McBride and Karla Mason Bergen examined how people characterized their work-spouse relationships. Analyzing responses from 269 participants, they identified recurring characteristics, including trust, honesty, loyalty, respect, emotional connection, and mutual support. Recent findings suggest that based on these findings, they defined work spouses as close, platonic workplace friends who share a strong emotional connection and provide support for one another. Despite the marital terminology, these relationships are generally understood as nonromantic and nonsexual.

I've always found the term particularly interesting because my parents are both actual spouses and work spouses. They're professors in the same discipline, and I grew up watching them navigate their personal and professional lives together. So when I call my friend my work spouse, I'm describing something quite different from the relationship I've watched my parents share. Still, both relationships involve professional collaboration, mutual support, and an understanding of each other's work.

It has been revealed that think about how much of our lives we spend working. We navigate stressful deadlines, celebrate accomplishments, complain about frustrating policies, and occasionally wonder why we chose our particular professions in the first place. Naturally, we develop relationships with the people experiencing those things alongside us.

And sometimes, those relationships become particularly meaningful because our co-workers understand things about our professional lives that people outside our workplaces might not.

For instance, my work spouse understands why I might be nervous to present a fresh idea, excited by a revise and resubmit, or frustrated about a particular obligation. I don't have to spend time explaining the context before getting to the actual story, and sometimes that can be really nice.

But it's more than having someone who understands your workplace. Sometimes, that person is also helping you get the work done.

The two of us work on projects together, but we also spend time working independently alongside one another. We bounce ideas off each other, provide feedback, and hold each other accountable for making progress. I find that I'm often more productive when we're working together, even when we're tackling entirely different tasks.

It has been revealed that these experiences are reflected in McBride and Bergen's findings. Participants described work spouses as people they could confide in, seek advice from, and rely on during stressful periods. Others talked about having someone who motivated them, challenged them professionally, or simply made difficult workdays more enjoyable.

According to recent reports, and I imagine these experiences aren't unique to academia. Think about teachers planning lessons together, nurses navigating difficult shifts, or colleagues working toward a shared deadline.

What I find interesting is that these friendships aren't just about having someone to vent to. They can also shape how we experience the work itself.

Of course, mixing friendship and work isn't always straightforward. Management scholars Whitman and Mandeville (2021) explored how work-spouse relationships blur the boundaries between our professional and personal lives, including potential complications involving favoritism, romantic partners, and competing responsibilities.

But I think sometimes, the challenges are simply those that come with being close friends who also work together.

My work spouse and I have certainly had our rough patches. We've had to figure out how to support each other as friends while also being honest and productive collaborators. We've had plenty of conversations about what we need from one another, including when one of us needs encouragement and when one of us needs to be told we're being unreasonable and need to chill out.

Industry observers note that this is something McBride and his colleagues explored further. In two subsequent studies, McBride, Allison Thorson, and Bergen (2020a, 2020b) examined how work spouses manage privacy and navigate one another's feelings and professional identities. Their research illustrates that even close workplace friendships involve decisions about what to share, when to speak up, and how to support one another.

And, for what it's worth, I don't think every close workplace friendship needs a special label. But I do think these relationships deserve the same attention and care we give to our friendships outside of work.
