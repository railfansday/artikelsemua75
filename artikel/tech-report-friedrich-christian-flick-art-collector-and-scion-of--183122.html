Friedrich Christian Flick, a major collector of contemporary art and scion to one of Germany’s wealthiest industrialist families, died at 82 in Rosenheim in southern Germany. Flick died after suffering injuries from a fall days earlier, according to Monopol magazine.

Industry observers note that flick was a fixture of ARTnews Top 200 Collectors in the early 2000s, appearing on the list consecutively from 1999 to 2007. Flick’s collection is comprised of some 2,500 works, including major pieces by Bruce Nauman, Alberto Giacometti, Marcel Duchamp, Gerhard Richter, Georg Baselitz, Paul McCarthy, and Martin Kippenberger. According to recent reports, his patronage of the arts was not without controversy. His grandfather, Friedrich Flick, who died in 1972, was a top arms supplier to Nazi Germany and had built his company, which included production of coal and steel, on forced labor. It has been revealed that in 2001, Flick hired Rem Koolhaas to design a newly introduced museum to house the collection in Zurich. The project was met with protests both from writers and artists, as well as major Jewish groups, who criticized Flick for not contributing to a $6 billion fund for survivors of slave labor factories like those run by his grandfather.

The museum project was abandoned, and in 2003, Flick sought a newly introduced path for showing the works from the collection through a partnership with the Prussian Cultural Heritage Foundation, which operates Berlin’s museums. Flick loaned the collection for seven years to Hamburger Bahnhof museum and paid to renovate a 19th-century warehouse into an annex for the museum, as the New York Times reported at the time. According to Monopol, Flick also commissioned the Prussian Cultural Heritage Foundation to conduct research into his family’s Nazi past, founded a foundation against xenophobia and racism, and donated €5 million to the forced labor fund.

Those moves did little to quell the controversy. In a 2004 op-ed for Handelsblatt, a German business newspaper, Salomon Korn, the vice president of Germany’s Central Council of Jews, called the deal “a kind of whitewashing of blood money into a socially accepted form of art property.” In an open letter, Korn asked further, “Will there soon be a ‘Göring Collection’ in Berlin?”.

Per Monopol, Flick later stated the response “deeply distressed” him, but that it “forced me to grapple with my family history.” Still, in a 2004 interview with NPR, Flick argued that his family’s Nazi past should not be presented with the works in collection.

“My family history led me to collect works from the kinds of artists who ask irritating questions and challenge people,” he stated. “But the family story has to be separated from the presentation of the collection. It would abuse the artwork to present it in the context of my family history. The art is not there for that. It stands on its own.”.

In a recent development, the partnership between Flick and the Hamburger Bahnhof continued until 2021, when the halls renovated by Flick to house his art were sold off to a private real estate manufacturer, which decided to demolish them. Some of Flick’s works remain on view elsewhere in Berlin: In 2008 and 2014, Flick donated 268 works, including pieces by McCarthy, Pipilotti Rist, Stan Douglas, and others, to the National Gallery. It is not yet clear what will happen to the rest of the collection.
