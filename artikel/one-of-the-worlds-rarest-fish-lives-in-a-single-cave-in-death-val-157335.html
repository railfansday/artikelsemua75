One of the world’s rarest fish lives in a single cave in Death Valley National Park, and earthquakes have left it on the verge of extinction

According to recent reports, devils Hole pupfish live in just one cavern of Death Valley National Park. Earthquakes are rapidly pushing this fish towards extinction.

One of the rarest fish on Earth lives in just one place: a water-filled cavern in the Nevada desert called Devils Hole, part of Death Valley National Park. In the spring of 2025, after two earthquakes shook its tiny home, biologists counted only 38 of them.

The National Park Service declared the newly introduced figure in November. Divers and surface counters found the 137 Devils Hole pupfish during a survey on October 10, nearly double the 77 seen in the spring of 2026 and about double the 69 recorded in the fall of 2025. It has been revealed that devils Hole is a cave about 12 feet wide and more than 500 feet deep. The bottom has never been mapped. The water stays at a constant 92 degrees Fahrenheit (33 degrees Celsius).

Pupfish have been found as deep as 66 feet, but they feed and spawn only on a shallow rock shelf near the surface, where algae grows.

Industry observers note that the species has been isolated there for 10,000 to 20,000 years, longer than any other pupfish in the Death Valley system. It has been revealed that it is the only naturally occurring population of the species, which was listed as endangered in 1967. Biologists from the National Park Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service have counted the fish almost every spring and fall since 1972, with scuba divers in the cavern and counters at the surface.

Sources indicate that one of the fish’s main threats can come from thousands of miles away. Large earthquakes as far away as Japan, Indonesia and Chile have made the water slosh in Devils Hole like water in a bathtub, according to the park.

Sources indicate that waves can splash as high as two meters (about 6.5 feet) up the walls and sweep the shelf clean. The park calls it a tsunami in the desert, although it is tiny next to the ocean tsunamis that big earthquakes can unleash.

That is what happened in the winter of 2024 to 2025. Two earthquakes, one in December and one in February, set off waves that moved algae, organic material and fish eggs off the shelf.

The spring count in 2025 found 38 fish. One year earlier, the official spring count had recorded 191.

On July 29, 2025, just 21 minutes after a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula, waves rippled through Devils Hole, nearly 4,000 miles away. Those waves were only about 10 inches high and removed just some material from the shelf.

Recent findings suggest that then, after an earthquake in Panama on October 9, 2026, nearly 3,000 miles away, biologists at first saw signs of stress in the fish. On a later visit, they found them in healthier condition.

The team had prepared for a crash like this for years. Pupfish eggs from Devils Hole had been collected regularly to build a backup population at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility.

Sources indicate that in 2025, biologists added captive-raised fish to the wild population for the first time. Between March 2025 and February 2026, scientists released 72 of them into Devils Hole. They also gave the fish extra food while the algae grew back.

According to recent reports, the fish live an average of only 10 to 14 months. Because of that, biologists believe most of the 137 fish counted this fall hatched and grew up in Devils Hole itself.

“It’s exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year,” said Death Valley National Park Superintendent Mike Reynolds.

It has been revealed that “These results reflect the dedication of our scientists and conservation partners working to protect this extraordinary species. While challenges remain, we’re encouraged by this progress and committed to its long-term survival.”.

According to recent reports, the pupfish has survived human threats before. In the late 1960s, a farming operation drilled large wells in the Ash Meadows area, and the water level in Devils Hole began to drop toward the shelf the fish need.

According to recent reports, biologists even hung a floating, artificially lit shelf in the cave as a substitute. The fish never used it.

A federal court halted further pumping in 1971, and the fight reached the U.S. Supreme Court. Its 1976 decision in Cappaert v. United States recognized the water right of Devils Hole as part of a national monument and limited pumping so the natural shelf would stay underwater.

Until the mid-1990s, spring surveys counted more than 200 fish. In the early 2000s, spring counts averaged about 90, and in 2013 the number fell to 35. The cause of that long decline has not been determined.

The new count is an encouraging step, but the park states the population naturally fluctuates and remains highly vulnerable.

It calls continued monitoring, research and habitat management essential if one of the world’s rarest fish is to keep its foothold in a single desert cave.

The information was obtained from the National Park Service’s fall 2026 count announcement, its spring 2025 update, its report on the Kamchatka earthquake and its Devils Hole page.
