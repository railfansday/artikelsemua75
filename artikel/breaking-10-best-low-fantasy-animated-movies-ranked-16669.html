It has been revealed that it was American author Lloyd Alexander who's credited with coining the term "high fantasy" in his 1971 essay High Fantasy and Heroic Romance, while "low fantasy" appeared later in the 20th century as a contrasting classification. Where high fantasy tells stories set in secondary worlds with their own unique set of rules, low fantasy finds the genre's magical and supernatural elements intrude into our own world—or a setting very heavily based on it.

Though live-action cinema has produced several exceptional low fantasy masterpieces over the years, there's something special about the way animation allows low fantasy filmmakers to really let their imaginations fly. From anime gems like My Neighbor Totoro to modern American blockbusters like How to Train Your Dragon, these are films which show why animation is such a perfect vehicle for low fantasy storytelling.

According to recent reports, the 1980s saw the birth of Studio Ghibli, the studio that quickly became the quintessential spearhead of anime filmmaking—a title they most definitely still hold. One of their earliest masterworks was Hayao Miyazaki's My Neighbor Totoro, about two young sisters who move with their dad to the Japanese countryside to be closer to their ill mother, where they find comfort and wonder in the organization of the forest's friendly spirits. In a recent development, director Henry Selick met author Neil Gaiman just as he was finishing his children's novella Coraline, and since Gaiman was such a fan of Selick's The Nightmare Before Christmas, he invited him to make a film adaptation of Coraline. It's the story of the titular girl, who discovers a secret door in her latest home leading to an idealized parallel world that's actually far more sinister than it initially seems.

Coraline was stop-motion animation studio Laika's first-ever feature film (and the only one they've ever made that has been profitable), and what an introduction to such a marvelous studio this was. It's one of the best dark fantasy movies ever made, and even though portal fantasy often makes it tricky to distinguish between high and low fantasy, Coraline's idealized parallel world makes this a clear example of one of the 2000s' best low fantasy gems.

It has been revealed that based on their trilogy of stop-motion-animated documentary short films, Dean Fleischer Camp and Jenny Slate made Marcel the Shell With Shoes On, with Camp directing and Slate voicing the titular one-inch-tall seashell. This is his story, as he and his grandmother search for their missing family after a documentary filmmaker's online videos turn Marcel into an overnight Internet sensation.

According to recent reports, one of the most emotional animated movies in recent memory, Marcel the Shell is a beautiful tale about grief, community, and finding wonder in everyday life. It's silly, it's simple, it's funny, yet it's more than emotionally complex enough to satisfy even the most cynical of grown-up viewers. Blending live-action and animation has always resulted in really interesting projects, and as far as the last decade goes, this is probably the best of the bunch.

Recent findings suggest that irish animation studio Cartoon Saloon have been making some of the fantasy movies with the best lore for years, all thanks to their stories often being heavily inspired by Irish folklore. Case in point: Tomm Moore's Song of the Sea, about a young boy and his mute selkie sister journeying across a fading world to return home after their mother vanishes into the sea.

According to recent reports, for one, the colorful visuals are absolutely gorgeous, more than enough to remind anyone of why it's still crucial to have 2D-animated features in modern times. Add to that a beautiful story, an airtight plot that's perfectly paced from start to finish, and deeply memorable characters, and there should be no question left as to why this is one of the most universally beloved low fantasy films of the 2010s, animated or otherwise.

As if any more proof were needed that Cartoon Saloon is one of the most interesting animation studios presently in operation, there's also Wolkfwalkers, one of the best fantasy films of the 2020s so far. Set in 1650s Ireland, it follows a young English apprentice hunter who befriends a wild native girl from a mystical tribe of wolf-spirits, being pulled into the magical world of the very creatures her father has been ordered to exterminate.

Stories about forbidden friendship in children's animation are aplenty, but what makes Wolfwalkers so special is the gorgeous animation, the deep low fantasy world-building, and the deeply moving father-daughter dynamic that often serves as the film's beating heart. A potent critique of colonialism, authoritarianism, and ecological destruction, this is a must-see for anyone who loves modern animation.

Industry observers note that toy Story completely revolutionized the film industry by introducing the world to fully CG-animated feature filmmaking, and Toy Story 2 somehow managed to be even better. It's Toy Story 3, however, that many still recognize as Pixar's best film to date. In it, Andy's toys are accidentally sent to a daycare center as he prepares to leave for college, forcing them to decide whether to stay together or fight their way back home.

From the phenomenal character writing to the deep themes to one of the most perfect movie endings of the 2010s, pretty much everything about Toy Story 3 is low fantasy animation perfection. Toys being alive is the only high-concept premise that makes this franchise low fantasy, and Toy Story 3 takes that premise to unprecedentedly ambitious and emotional heights.

In a recent development, it's not all Miyazaki: Studio Ghibli has also made films directed by other filmmakers who are just as talented, perhaps the most talented of whom was the great Isao Takahata. His final film as director was Ghibli's The Tale of the Princess Kaguya, an adaptation of the 10th-century Japanese story The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter. In it, a rapidly-growing child is found inside a bamboo stalk and raised by a humble cutter, eventually thrust into high society as a noble princess.

Princess Kaguya is not only one of Ghibli's greatest films to date, but also one of the best fantasy movie masterpieces of the last 15 years. With its wonderfully unique watercolor animation and its profound emotional and thematic maturity, this is both a fantasy masterpiece and a gorgeous period piece. It's complex, it's deeply moving, and it may just have the most unforgettable ending in Ghibli's entire filmography.

Based on the series of children's books by Cressida Cowell, How to Train Your Dragon gave birth to one of the best fantasy movie trilogies of all time. It tells the story of the awkward young Viking Hiccup, who lives on a dragon-raiding island and befriends a wounded dragon that challenges his tribe's traditions. What ensues is further proof that, though abundant, low fantasy stories of forbidden friendship are genuinely special.

Sources indicate that dragons have always been prevalent across both high and low fantasy stories even before cinema was a thing, but few dragon-centric fantasy films have ever been quite as universally beloved as How to Train Your Dragon. That's for good reason. With Roger Deakins as a consultant and DreamWorks' phenomenal team, the animation is obviously fantastic; but what really makes this stand out as one of the studio's best animated films is the emotional story, the tight plotting, and the excellent character work.

Brad Bird's Ratatouille is many people's favorite Pixar film to date, and it isn't the least bit difficult to see why. Visually wonderful, tonally complex, and emotionally and thematically mature, it tells the engrossing story of a Paris rate with an extraordinary palate, who teams up with a clumsy kitchen garbage boy to secretly become a master chef at a prestigious restaurant.

According to recent reports, it's a silly premise that any lesser filmmaker or animation studio may have turned into an equally silly comedy, but Bird treats the premise with such beautiful and sincere earnestness that Ratatouille rapidly becomes one of the most wonderful works of art that animation has delivered in years. It's also one of the fantasy movies with the most interesting characters, which is only one of the many reasons why it's an absolute must-see.

Who, if not Hayao Miyazaki, could be behind yet another one of the most ambitious fantasy movies ever made? It is, of course, Ghibli's Princess Mononoke, which is set in the Muromachi period of Japanese history. In it, a young prince cursed by a demon journeys into a war between an industrial mining town and the forest gods, who fight back alongside a girl raised by wolves.

Sources indicate that it's one of the darkest, most violent, and most mature movies that Studio Ghibli have thus far made, and it also just so happens to arguably be the greatest low fantasy animated feature ever made. Though its world-building prioritizes historical realism over fantasy, there's still more than plenty of it to satisfy fans of the genre. An enrapturing story about the destructive cycle of hatred and ecological exploitation, it's easily one of the best outings in Miyazaki's filmography.
