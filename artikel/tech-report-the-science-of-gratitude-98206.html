Gratitude gets a lot of airtime around the holidays. Usually it sounds like an instruction: Count your blessings. But the science of gratitude is more interesting than that. And kinder.

In one well-known study, psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked people to write down a few things they were grateful for once a week. Another group wrote about their hassles. After 10 weeks, the gratitude group felt more optimistic, reported fewer physical complaints, and even exercised more. A small practice led to a measurable change. In a recent development, other studies show similar findings. Writing a thank-you letter to someone and delivering it in person gave people a lift in happiness that lasted for weeks. And in a study of people already in counseling, those who wrote gratitude letters reported better mental health one and three months later. Brain scans taken at the three-month mark indicated greater neural sensitivity to gratitude. It turns out gratitude is less a feeling we wait for, and more a muscle we can exercise.

It has been revealed that for many people, the holiday season can be tough. There’s an empty chair at the table. A family you can’t go home to. A new diagnosis. A layoff. A year when the floor kept giving way. Does this sound like you? Then know that gratitude isn’t some holiday-sanctioned demand to feel “fine.”.

None of these studies asked anyone to pretend. The people in counseling weren’t happy when they started. They were struggling. They didn’t write their pain away. They set it aside for a moment and found something to be grateful for.

It has been revealed that remember that gratitude can live right next door to grief. You can mourn someone terribly and still be grateful you had them in your life. You can feel lonely and still wave at the neighbor on their porch when you pass them in the morning. Both things can be true.

According to recent reports, so if this season starts to feel heavy, write down one small thing you’re grateful for. Not the biggest thing. Just one small thing. And if you can’t think of one today, that’s okay. Wake up tomorrow, wave to your neighbor, and try again.

Gratitude isn’t about having a perfect holiday. It’s about noticing what’s holding you up when so much is pulling you down. And according to the science, that’s a perfect place to start. (Related: Stop treating agents as one-off projects and start runn...)
