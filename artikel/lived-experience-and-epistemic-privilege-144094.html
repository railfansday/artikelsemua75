"Ich habe Lebenserfahrung [I have life experience]."—E. Rudolf Krüger, putting his son in his place.

"Experience keeps a dear school, but fools will learn in no other."—Benjamin Franklin, 1743, meditating on the benefits of heeding advice.

A current trend in the psychological scene is seeking to rehabilitate lived experience as an element of scholarship (Krueger, 2026). The importance of lived experience as part of the human condition is undeniable (Casey, 2023). It is also undeniable that there is value in sampling reports of lived experience widely to ensure that a broad spectrum of voices is being heard; indeed, there is also value in oversampling voices from small populations to ensure that minority views are not drowned out by a majority din (Kalsbeek, 2003).

Industry observers note that there are good reasons to think, however, that a chorus of voices will not amount to scholarship unless it is tamed, disciplined, and critically examined. Scientific methods can do this. They impose standards on the collection, the organization, and the interpretation of evidence (Godwin & Goodwin, 2016). Although stories and narratives are obligatory elements of the human condition (Gottschall, 2012), they cannot replace science; they can only be objects of scientific study. Stories are data, that is, material to be curated by theory and explored with standardized methodology. The idea that stories of lived experience can stand on their own is as mistaken as the idea that quantitative data, and especially “major data,” reveal reality all by themselves, without help from or intervention by a theoretically minded intellect (Felin et al., 2021). Sources indicate that sir Karl Popper (1963) made this point jokingly. Beginning in 1928, he invited his students to “observe,” and when they asked, "What?" Popper exclaimed, "Aha!" Observation needs guidance. Then, the act of making an observation can create value; indeed, it can bring forth a reality, but only inasmuch as the observation is embedded in a web of expectations, knowledge, and prior experience. A phenomenologist might say that the lived experience of sensing and perceiving is the thing: It is Kant’s (1783) Ding an sich. Sources indicate that important as they are, it is doubtful that lived experiences can supersede systematic scientific work. Yet, stories and narratives populate the literature. How do they get in? One pathway is for everyone so inclined to write up their story and get it published. This genre of literature exists. When asked, the AI engine Claude estimates the number of published biographies to exceed 10,000 annually. A search of the term "Google n-gram viewer" indicates that during the post-WWII era, the term “memoir” has risen in popularity, in tandem with the term self-esteem, suggesting a cultural drift to greater concern with the individual self. Yet, the term "memoir" already enjoyed an earlier flowering during the era of romanticism (see Figure 1).

Industry observers note that to see how stories and narratives of lived experience enter the psychological literature, we have to look to the pathways offered by the field of qualitative psychology. Here, an increasingly popular view is that both the conductors of qualitative research and their informants gain epistemic privilege from lived experience relevant to the subject matter at hand (Levitt & Pierorazio, 2026). This view is critical of investigative neutrality, a traditional value of the scientific mindset. Thomas Nagel (1986) referred to this mindset when asking whether we can take “a view from nowhere,” a perspective which privileges no one (also, Weber, 1904; 1917).

Of course, the lived experience of informants is critical when it underlies the expertise that needs to be brought to the investigation. When researching, for example, what the experience of post-traumatic stress is like, informants must be found who have had that experience. Insisting that researchers ought to have had that same type of experience would be an odd demand. At best, an investigator’s lived experience is irrelevant; at worst, it sets the stage for methodological bias.

The terms of lived experience and epistemic privilege are increasingly seen in the literature, as shown by the second n-gram graph (Figure 2). The relative popularity of the term epistemic privilege seems to be lagging, perhaps because this term is more difficult to comprehend. One reason for this difficulty is that the term has been used in more than one way. One usage is to refer to a knowledgeable person who has privileged access to the material under investigation. To the early school of introspective psychology, pioneered by Wilhelm Wundt, privileged access to one’s own mental contents was the foundation of their scientific efforts. A very different way of referring to epistemic privilege is political (e.g., Hartsock, 1983). Here, the position of the researcher is called into question (or “interrogated”) as a source of bias or perhaps even oppressive intent. Wundt is turned on his head.

Many authors feel strongly about critically assessing researchers’ epistemic privilege. Alas, limiting access to research outlets because of concerns regarding the researcher’s positionality sets up a slippery slope that can lead to unwarranted exclusion. It is worthwhile asking whether psychological science is not best served by an ethos of epistemic neutrality. Let the quality and rigor of the research speak for itself. This ethic allows authors of different positionality and authors with different kinds of epistemic privilege—or lack thereof—to contribute to the conversation. Authors in the progressive vein, for example, may wish to recall that from Marx to Lenin and to the Frankfurt School, the most influential authors emerged from the bourgeoisie. Should they have been denied the privilege of championing the interests of the proletariat?

My sympathies lie with the conventional ethic of blind reception. Let the research speak for itself, and let it be evaluated in light of explicit, rigorous, and shared standards. In my teaching, though, I seek to help students situate individual research articles within the authors’ life’s work. When students give a presentation in class, I ask them to include a short author biography with key data from their career and even a photo if they can find one. This exercise helps students understand why the researchers care about their work and see connections across studies. The goal is not to “interrogate” the researchers’ motives or use their demographic data as grounds for speculation about their intentions.

It has been revealed that robust scientific results should stand regardless of who produced them. In time, memory for findings should crystallize, while memory for the authors’ identity should suffer the fate of source amnesia. May the findings last—if they are true and replicable—while the finders’ identities fade.
