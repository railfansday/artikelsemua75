Fixing Netflix’s TV Problem: 5 Changes the Streamer Should Make for Better, Sturdier, and Buzzier Series

The other day, one line in an otherwise unremarkable marketing email caught my eye: “Discovery is exactly what fans come to Netflix for.”.

Recent findings suggest that even with a dangling preposition (in a press release littered with errors), the claim felt like the more glaring mistake: Netflix is where fans go for discovery. They do? Still? Are you sure about that?

Recent findings suggest that but when it comes to Netflix’s core business — streaming television — the service is largely a preferred destination by default. Being an industry pioneer helped establish Netflix as a utility among TV fans, many of whom still turn to it when they want to see “what’s on,” but it’s hard to imagine why anyone would head there first when searching for their next favorite show. Recent findings suggest that that would require the customer to trust Netflix — either as the service offering the most bang for their buck, the highlights everyone is talking about, or, most likely and simplest of all, the best series on TV. Can the streamer really stake a claim to any of those distinctions? (Related: Breaking: Living Trust vs Will: Complete Comparison Gui...)

Industry observers note that not anymore. Not after this year.

Throughout 2026, Netflix has been plagued by problems and complaints. Viewership for returning seasons is dwindling, subscription prices are rising, hot fresh franchises are scarce, and high-quality programming — a subjective designation, to be sure — isn’t what it used to be. Netflix is no longer the most affordable SVOD service. It’s not the most award-winning, nor is it the buzziest.

It has been revealed that A sustainable hit could quiet things down, but those have been hard to come by. Netflix’s most popular newly introduced highlights this year were limited series (like “His & Hers” and “I Will Find You”), which only offer short-term juice. Promising first seasons (“The Boroughs”) faced quick cancellations that left fans fuming and analysts searching for answers. Even touted chart toppers (“Man on Fire”) don’t get a quick Season 2 renewal, let alone a quick turnaround between seasons.

It has been revealed that ignoring these very-online narratives, none of the aforementioned shows earned rave reviews, and the streamer’s awards output echoed what’s felt like a dearth in quality. Total Emmy wins were cut nearly in half this year, from 30 to 16, causing Netflix to fall to third place (behind HBO and Apple) for the first time since topping the town in 2021.

A month after Emmy night, all the criticism reached a crescendo when a plunging stock price and lack of hits forced mass layoffs, including the dismissal of chief content officer Bela Bajaria and other key executives. Peter Rice is at the helm now, and his new team is setting the table for changes in 2027 — much like Netflix did in 2022 after The Great Streaming Correction opened the door for an ad-tier and shut the door on password sharing.

While it would be foolish to believe fixing one aspect of a diverse business would make all its other problems go away, it would be equally foolish to pretend there’s not a TV problem at Netflix. A healthy TV slate only benefits engagement across the platform, and the streamer is still the major dog in SVOD — it’s just losing ground year by year. So what’s to be done?

Well, that depends on how much Netflix is willing to change. If it’s all on the table, there are a handful of reasonable shifts they could make to improve their reputation. If not, well, I’ve got an idea for that, too.

At the root of Netflix’s TV problem is that its demonstrates feel disposable. Sometimes that’s by design: Netflix makes a lot of programs that can be watched while scrolling, which can be OK. TV has long been a medium built for background entertainment, and it’s still nice to have an easily digestible distraction while doing dishes or folding laundry.

Recent findings suggest that but Netflix may have gone too far. Setting aside quality (for a moment), the streamer’s focus on “discovery” paired with its faith in the almighty algorithm to sort through its substantial monthly programming influx emphasizes new reveals above all else. There’s always another new series, new special, or new live event to watch, but there isn’t always more of the show you already love. Some original series take years between releasing new seasons. Some only dole out six or eight episodes every 18 months. Some get canceled after one or two seasons, and very few run more than three or four seasons in total.

According to recent reports, whether or not the approach works from a financial perspective, it fails to foster audience investment, and investment is where TV thrives. Just look at this year’s most-watched demonstrates on streaming, per Nielsen: “Stranger Things” holds down the No. 1 spot, but then it’s “Bluey,” “The Pitt,” “Grey’s Anatomy,” and “The Major Bang Theory.” Keep scrolling and it’s even easier to spot the dominant trend: Shows with huge libraries that first aired on network TV.

These are the kind of demonstrates that people keep up with for years, decades, or more. They like the characters, they like the structure, they like the setting — they like being in the show’s world, whether it’s a hospital filled with hot doctors or a nerd’s living room. Offering access on a regular basis encourages investment, which breeds loyalty, which creates longevity. Producing more episodes, more often — like broadcast TV has done all along — creates bigger libraries that fans will happily live in for years on end because they enjoy spending time with those characters and within those stories.

Sources indicate that streamers have been reluctant to adopt their predecessor’s production model (for reasons both valid and ridiculous that I’ll get into in the next section), but the success of long-running (and long-gone) broadcast series on streaming, paired with the recent popularity of “The Pitt,” makes it hard to argue against making longer seasons, consisting of more episodes, that are released on a regular basis. It’s what audiences want now, and it’s what they’ve shown they’ll want later.

Sources indicate that which brings us to the binge model. One of the reasons Netflix hasn’t experimented with more broadcast-style streaming shows (like “The Pitt”) is because it can’t. Its release model is different. The binge model requires everything be done before anything premieres, including timely post-production work like subtitles and dubbing, which Netflix needs to serve its global base. While episodes of most network series are still being written, shot, and edited as earlier, completed episodes roll out, episodes of a Netflix series drop all at once.

Luckily, Netflix already made the change. Or started to, at least.

Nowadays, Netflix’s most popular original series are often released in batches (or “parts”), including the last two seasons of “Stranger Things” and “Bridgerton.” Both highlights are among the most-watched programs on the planet, so the shift in release strategy hasn’t hurt their popularity. Other shows are already released weekly, like “The Great British Baking Show,” or close to weekly, like “Love Is Blind.”.

Sources indicate that moreover, the addition of live events has forced Netflix to implement fresh tactics to raise awareness for time-sensitive content (instead of just relying on the algorithm), which should limit fears over viewer confusion. (For seasons released in parts, the Netflix platform tends to list how many episodes will be released and when. You can even set reminders for upcoming programming, even if none of it is available yet — like, say, the NFL game on Thanksgiving Eve, which is a thing, I guess.).

Netflix decided long ago to break up the binge, now they just need to keep going. After all, the binge model was created to disrupt Hollywood. No one was releasing full seasons at once, and Netflix executives recognized how DVDs had primed viewers to marathon TV demonstrates. Instead of old favorites (like “The Office”), why not give them fresh ones to devour?

But Netflix isn’t a disruptor anymore. It’s the industry leader. It doesn’t need a gimmick to draw attention. It needs a strategy to help keep it.

A weekly release model could help. Netflix doesn’t need to enforce it on every series — let people binge “Ginny & Georgia” — but for shows that could benefit from the regular cycle of anticipation, fulfillment, dissection (repeat), the tried-and-true strategy could do wonders for audience investment. In the short-term, it could help prevent dips in engagement and help foster engagement with newly beloved series. In the long-term, it could help build franchises, libraries, and loyalty.

You can’t just make the next “Pitt.” You have to create the system to support it.

Speaking of support, it’s time to talk about quality. What constitutes a good show and a bad show will always, in the end, remain in the eye of the beholder, but a tech giant aiming to “replace all television” can’t rely on subjective interpretations to define its brand. Nor can it rely primarily on an algorithm to elevate the best options instead of the easiest ones. Otherwise, you risk a narrative like this year’s, where objective measurements like ratings (where the three most-watched new highlights average a 56 rating on Metacritic) and awards (where Netflix landed one more win than NBC and Peacock combined) create the impression of less-than-“gourmet” service.

Some such schemes are already underway with pre-show ads running before episodes and prominent brand campaigns helping connect viewers with their upcoming favorite programs. Hopefully, that signals more old-school advocacy efforts are coming. Eventize episodes, not just new seasons. Build channels for episodic programs to elevate their best moments. Add permanent subsections to Netflix’s genre listings.

This is not a fresh idea. At various times for various reasons, various experts have suggested Netflix launch a free subscription tier in order to promote its growing library to a wider audience. FAST (free, ad-supported streaming) platforms like Pluto, Tubi, and The Roku Channel are growing swiftly mainly by replicating the broadcast model for the streaming age, and Netflix could take a page out of their playbook to expand their business in any number of ways. But in terms of solving their TV problem, they should use it to distinguish their premium original programming from their recent obsession: YouTube slop.

Industry observers note that I’ll be honest: I don’t think of Netflix and YouTube as competitors, mainly because Netflix isn’t free. Sure, there’s YouTube Premium, but YouTube is instantly accessible, sans subscription, and that’s how most people get hooked. I don’t care how many more hours people spend watching YouTube than Netflix because the hours I spend watching movie trailers, how-to-videos, and late-night clips are not the same as the hours I spend watching “Little House on the Prairie,” “Long Story Short,” and “East of Eden.” One I’m willing to pay for, one I’m not. So the idea that Netflix, as a business, should be trying to surpass YouTube never made sense to me.

It still doesn’t, but I understand the market demands constant growth, and I understand why Netflix would want YouTube’s viewers, especially in the younger demographic. So Netflix has been busy signing content creators, adding podcasts, and filling the service with easy-listening videos (aka videos you don’t need to watch, but can play in the background without much disturbance). If that helps bring the next generation to Netflix so they can discover the glory of “BoJack Horseman,” great! But right now, BoJack is sitting side by side with random content like “Conspiracy Theories,” “Small Town Murder,” and “Bill Simmons.”.

That’s a problem. We shouldn’t equate those very distinct experiences, and Netflix shouldn’t suggest we do. At best, it builds a false equivalency that steadily lowers our artistic standards for visual entertainment. At worst, it leads to more fans of this “Bill Simmons.”.

So let’s create some tiers. Launch a free version of Netflix where all the podcasts, creator videos, and short-form content can live. Make money the old-fashioned way — from ads — while offering select TV shows to sample in the hopes viewers will get hooked and pay for an upgraded subscription. Keep original series, movies, and other top library offerings on the paid tiers, and structure those however you like. (Presently, there’s Standard with Ads, Standard, and Premium, each with their distinct perks.).

It has been revealed that is creating a FAST version of Netflix a whole lot of work to dispel the notion that Netflix makes too much disposable TV? Yup, and I won’t pretend to know how feasible it is, financially or otherwise. But Netflix needs to take a significant swing or two if it hopes to fix its reputation, which brings us to my last option.

According to recent reports, netflix is still the dominant streamer in Hollywood. Plentiful stats support its case, but why look any further than how many other streamers license content to their biggest competitor? Disney+, Hulu, Paramount+, and more all lend their once-exclusive series to their rival in the hope of getting the Netflix bump (or a quick influx of cash). Never did I think I’d see the day where HBO would be available anywhere else, and yet everything from “Sex and the City” to “Ballers” has graced Netflix in 2026.

With that in mind, Netflix may choose to stay the course. The once-and-future tech firm is notorious for sticking to certain brand tenets, like trusting the algorithm over traditional marketing and its binge model over traditional release strategies. Even when Hollywood pushes back, Netflix’s broader goals continue to evolve. Originally, it wanted to be the HBO of streaming. Then it wanted to replace cable. Then it wanted to replace all television.

Industry observers note that what it wants to be now isn’t so clear. Wall Street keeps forcing comparisons to YouTube, some of which are invited by Netflix’s pursuit of its content and podcast creators. Maybe Netflix executives believe they can raise their market cap with vertical videos and video game previews, with latest decision-makers and added theatrical grosses, with a few small tweaks here and a few surprise wins there. It has the leverage to wait out complaints and keep making TV exactly as it has been, if it so chooses.

Recent findings suggest that but because of its relative health, I hope Netflix sees its TV problem as more of a TV opportunity. Yes, it needs more hits. Yes, it needs to build back trust. But it also wants to grow, and the suggestions above are all about sustainable growth through better TV. Longer seasons, more episodes, weekly releases, stronger support, clearer delineations, a FAST service — none of these ideas are revolutionary. They’re not meant to be. They’re meant to give the people what they want.

If Netflix is going to be where fans go for discovery, it first needs to be a place they can trust to get what what they need.
