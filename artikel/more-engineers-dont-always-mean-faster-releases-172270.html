More Engineers Don't Always Mean Faster Releases

​Headcount is an easy lever to pull—and often the wrong one. Before approving the next hire or the next AI license, look at where the work is actually waiting.

“We’re growing our engineering department. Why is it taking longer to get a product to market?”.

I’ve heard some version of that question from founders, CEOs and boards in medical devices, industrial tech and software. The budget goes up, the org chart grows, and release dates still slip. (Related: How to Budget for a Wedding Without Going Broke...)

In a recent development, the instinct to hire through a delivery problem is old, and so is the evidence against it. Computer scientist Fred Brooks, drawing on IBM’s OS/360 program, concluded that “adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.” Upcoming people need to learn the product and the organization. Experienced contributors spend time bringing them up to speed. Communication grows, and some work just can’t be split across more people. The same problem demonstrates up outside software when work is tightly connected and integration is hard. (Related: Is Buy Now Pay Later (BNPL) Worth It? The Hidden Costs...)

The first question isn’t asking how many more people you need. It’s asking where the work’s getting stuck. AI makes that question more important, not less.

In a recent development, my own path ran from software engineering and information security in defense aerospace, through product and engineering leadership at industrial manufacturers and a dental implant maker, to a health tech startup. I saw the same thing more than once: A structure that worked with 15 engineers didn’t work with 40, but the organization had grown without redesigning how decisions were made.

Jim Collins described getting the right people in the right seats in Good to Great. The catch is that the seats change as the company grows. A capable engineer is promoted into management without the interest, skills or support to lead. A technical lead still reviews everyone’s work personally. Upcoming developers add coding capacity, while testing remains the bottleneck.

The promotion problem is measurable. Researchers at Yale, MIT and the University of Minnesota conducted a study of more than 200 firms and found that firms promoted top performers into management roles they weren’t well-suited for. Among those promoted, prior individual performance was negatively correlated with managerial quality. Management isn’t the next stage of engineering. It’s a different job.

Sources indicate that as teams grow, management often adds reviews and sign-offs to keep track of the work. Each one looks reasonable on its own. Together, they create waiting time. If approvals are covering for unclear ownership or missing capability, another gate rarely fixes that. It just makes the queue longer.

It has been revealed that at one dental technology software business, the delivery problem wasn’t a shortage of developers. Verification sat at the end of the line; defects surfaced late, and a fix sent work back through the queue. Each market or customer variant was effectively its own build, which multiplied the verification effort every time we released.

We changed two things. First, we shifted testing left. Test engineers joined design reviews, acceptance criteria were written alongside requirements, and automated verification ran with every change. Second, we moved to build once, configure many: a single validated core with market and customer differences handled through controlled configuration rather than separate code branches. Verification stopped growing with every new variant, and design-control evidence got simpler to produce.

In a recent development, we didn’t fix that by hiring. We changed how the work moved and who owned the decisions.

According to recent reports, 1. Review roles before approving more hires. Where is work waiting, what capability is actually missing, and who owns the result? Another developer adds little if completed work is already sitting in a verification queue.

2. Match responsibilities to capabilities and interests. Give strong engineers a technical career path that doesn’t require managing people. Support newly introduced managers with training, mentoring and clear expectations from the start.

3. Keep one visible set of priorities. When urgent manufacturing or customer work arrives, decide explicitly what moves. I once worked with a firm running 19 active projects with 14 design engineers. We didn’t need a fancier planning system. We needed fewer things competing for the same people.

Sources indicate that AI coding tools can make a developer faster without speeding up delivery overall. DORA’s 2026 analysis of AI in software development described exactly this tension: AI speeds up initial code generation, but some of the time saved goes into checking the output. It also linked higher AI adoption with both higher delivery throughput and higher delivery instability.

That matters because the bottleneck doesn’t disappear; it moves. If developers generate more code while testing, review, integration and decision rights stay the same, more work piles up at the same choke points. A team can feel flat out without getting much more product into customers’ hands.

Buying AI licenses can become another way of hiring through the problem. The tool may well be useful, but if the system around it is slow, more output at the front end will expose that weakness, not fix it.

The TGA requires device manufacturers to run a quality management system for the design, production, packaging, labeling and final inspection of a device, referencing ISO 13485:2016. Design controls define what evidence must exist and who is accountable. They don’t require a senior leader to approve every drawing. Clear roles can make compliance easier because ownership is visible when the auditor asks.

The same problem applies whether it’s an ASX 200 software company or a 30-person startup. More people, or more AI-generated output, won’t make up for unclear ownership, competing priorities and slow decisions.

According to recent reports, before adding people, tools or another approval step, find where the work is stuck. The answer may be a different assignment, a missing skill, fewer priorities or a decision that management needs to let someone else make.

Then ask the harder question: Will the structure that works today still work at next year’s scale?
