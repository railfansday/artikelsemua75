The mature-stock-driven Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -1.93%), broad-based S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%), and tech-focused Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -5.28%) have all catapulted to several record highs this year, driven by the artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure build-out, better-than-expected corporate earnings, and initial public offering euphoria. Recent findings suggest that though any number of headwinds can knock the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite off their respective pedestals, one stands out. We're currently witnessing the stock market do something for only the fifth time this century, and history doesn't mince its words about what comes next for stocks.

Despite a parabolic move in outstanding margin debt sending all the wrong signals, the biggest worry for Wall Street and investors is premium stock valuations.

To be clear, past events can't guarantee what the future holds. Nevertheless, history has shown a tendency to rhyme on Wall Street more often than not, making it an excellent teaching tool for those investors willing to listen.

Most investors rely on the time-tested price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio when evaluating individual companies or the broader market. While the P/E ratio, which is derived using trailing 12-month earnings per share (EPS), is incredibly useful for quickly sizing up mature businesses, it can lose its utility entirely when recessions occur and EPS turns negative.

For Wall Street's broad-market stock indexes, the S&P 500's Shiller P/E Ratio, also known as the Cyclically Adjusted P/E Ratio (CAPE Ratio), is a far superior valuation tool. The Shiller P/E is based on average inflation-adjusted EPS over the trailing 10 years, meaning recessions won't render it useless.

Sources indicate that though economists introduced the S&P 500's Shiller P/E Ratio less than four decades ago, it's been backtested to January 1871. Over these 156 years, the average reading is a relatively modest 17.45. As of the closing bell on Nov. 17, it was 38.76, or 122% above this long-term average.

What's particularly interesting is how the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite have behaved once the CAPE Ratio surpasses 30, which has happened six times since January 1871, and five times this century, including the present:.

On the one hand, the S&P 500's Shiller P/E can't pinpoint when the major stock indexes will peak or which catalyst(s) will send stocks tumbling. But this arbitrary line in the sand has an impeccable track record of foreshadowing significant downturns (20% or greater) in the stock market's major indexes.

History makes it crystal clear that premium valuations aren't sustainable on Wall Street.

Sources indicate that sometimes, history doesn't offer the most glowing forecast for the stock market. But altering your perspective can vastly change the projected outcome.

To state what can be an unpleasant truth, stock market corrections, bear markets, and even occasional crashes are normal and healthy events. Given that most of these events are driven by investor emotions, there's not much that can be done from a fiscal or monetary policy perspective to prevent them. Consider these events the cost of admission to take part in one of the world's greatest wealth creators.

But stock market corrections, bear markets, and crashes share an key trait: they're historically short-lived.

The analysts at Bespoke Investment Group examined every S&P 500 bull and bear market since the start of the Great Depression in September 1929, finding a sizable gap between optimism and pessimism on Wall Street.

According to Bespoke's data set, released in late May, the average S&P 500 bear market finds its trough in 286 calendar days (about 9.5 months), while the typical bull market persists for 1,023 calendar days, or nearly 3.6 times as long.

The analysts at Crestmont Research took things even further by examining the rolling 20-year total returns of the benchmark S&P 500 since 1900. Since the S&P wasn't incepted until 1923, this meant tracking the total return of its components in other major indexes back to 1900.

Crestmont's data yielded 107 separate rolling 20-year periods (1900-1919, 1901-1920, and so on, through 2006-2025), all of which generated a positive average annual return.

Hypothetically speaking, since S&P 500-tracking funds didn't exist on U.S. exchanges until January 1993, if an investor had purchased an S&P 500 index fund at any point between 1900 and 2006 and simply held it for 20 years, they would have generated a profit every single time, including dividends. It didn't matter if they bought near a top, before a recession, depression, war, pandemic, or high inflation... the result was always the same.

Even when the stock market gives investors lemons, history notes make some lemonade and be patient.
