Disney’s sports profit jumped 53% as it bets on live games. It also wants $3 billion in cost cuts

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

For now, the bet is paying off. Disney’s Sports segment posted $4.6 billion in revenue in its fiscal fourth quarter, up 16% from a year earlier, while operating income jumped 53%. But Disney is pairing that push with a blueprint to cut about $3 billion in annual costs by fiscal 2029, signaling how far it is moving away from the traditional TV business that once paid for everything.

That tension defines the strategy. Disney, No. 44 on the 2026 Fortune 500, is raising its content budget, with sports rights a major line item, while trimming the rest of the manufacturer to fund growth.

It has been revealed that ESPN offers something most streaming libraries can’t: appointment viewing. Fans watch live, in large numbers, and advertisers pay a premium to reach them. (Related: Latest Update: Large Language Models: A New Moore's Law...)

It has been revealed that disney deepened that advantage in January, when ESPN closed its acquisition of NFL Network and NFL Fantasy, along with the rights to distribute the RedZone channel to cable and satellite operators. In exchange, the NFL took a 10% stake in ESPN. Disney now holds an effective 72% interest in the network, according to its fiscal fourth-quarter shareholder letter, and Hearst owns 18%. Audiences are rewarding the bet. Monday Night Football drew its best Week 1 and Week 2 audiences ever this season. ABC’s September broadcast of Ohio State vs. Texas pulled in about 14 million viewers, ESPN’s most-watched regular-season college football game in a decade.

College football is the backbone. In November, ESPN secured exclusive rights to the expanded 24-team College Football Playoff beginning in the 2027-28 season. The upcoming format grows the postseason from 11 games to 23, and the deal runs through the 2031-32 season.

The direction is clear: Disney wants sports woven into its global streaming business, not walled off inside a cable package.

Recent findings suggest that the quarter’s numbers explain the appeal. Sports subscription and affiliate fee revenue rose 18%, and advertising revenue grew 20%.

Industry observers note that those growth rates come with caveats. Disney’s fiscal 2026 ran 53 weeks, and the extra week landed in the fourth quarter. The comparison was also easier: a year earlier, ESPN was spending heavily to market the launch of its Unlimited streaming service.

Premium rights are not cheap. The Sports segment’s costs and expenses rose 5% in the quarter, to $3.2 billion, partly reflecting contractual rate increases and costs associated with new sports rights. Disney expects to spend about $26 billion on content in fiscal 2027, roughly 9% more than this year, including more sports rights and international programming.

In a recent development, CFO Hugh Johnston has argued that the biggest bills are already known. “In terms of sports rights, we’re actually pretty well locked up through 2029 or 2030,” he told a Goldman Sachs conference in September, adding that Disney had passed on Formula 1 because it had become too expensive.

In a recent development, not everyone thinks sports can carry a streaming business. “At the end of the day, all these streamers and platforms have to keep putting new stuff on screens, and sports isn’t going to solve the need for 75% of viewership,” Alex Iosilevich, co-founder of media, entertainment, and gaming investment firm Alignment Growth, told Business Insider in 2024.

Disney is targeting about $3 billion in annual run-rate operating expense savings by fiscal 2029. The program has already included several rounds of layoffs this year affecting thousands of employees, and more cuts are expected. Johnston said savings will also come from technology and real estate consolidation, with shared services another opportunity.

According to recent reports, CEO Josh D’Amaro, who succeeded Bob Iger in March, acknowledged the toll. “Changes like these are not easy,” he said, calling them “important if we want to be a company that moves faster, adapts to a rapidly changing environment.”.

The savings are not simply a war chest for rights fees. Disney says the money will primarily fund future growth, including streaming, with a portion reaching the bottom line over time. D’Amaro has also pushed to make Disney+ the company’s front door; executives have reportedly discussed a “super app” that would fold park tickets, merchandise, and cruise bookings into the streaming service.

None of this means Disney is abandoning movies, television, or its franchises. It is changing where the money goes as traditional television faces mounting pressure.

But the full ESPN lineup still sits behind separate subscriptions, and the week’s biggest college football games have largely stayed off the Disney+ simulcast schedule. That leaves viewers sorting out which events come with which blueprint.

Disney is betting that live sports can draw audiences, strengthen its streaming business, and command premium ad rates. The test is whether it can make that spending pay without pushing subscription prices past what consumers will tolerate. The first major reading comes Feb. 14; the next arrives when the 24-team playoff kicks off in December 2027.
