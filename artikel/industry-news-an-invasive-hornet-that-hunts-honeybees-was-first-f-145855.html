Industry News: An invasive hornet that hunts honeybees was first found in the U.S. in 2023 and has since reached North Carolina

The invasive yellow-legged hornet has spread to Georgia, South Carolina and North Carolina, threatening honeybees as nest numbers continue to rise.

It has been revealed that the yellow-legged hornet (Vespa velutina), an invasive predator of honeybees, has now been detected in three U.S. states: Georgia, South Carolina and North Carolina.

North Carolina confirmed its first established presence on July 27, 2026, in Matthews, the North Carolina Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services announced.

Sources indicate that the hornets were reported by a homeowner in Union County, North Carolina, who noticed suspicious activity at her hummingbird feeder. More than 200 survey traps were then set around her home to target the nest.

A beekeeper in Savannah, Georgia, found an unusual hornet on his property and reported it to state officials. On August 9, 2023, federal officials confirmed the identification.

It was the first time a live specimen of the species had been detected in the open United States, the Georgia Department of Agriculture states. The first nest, about 85 feet (26 meters) up a tree in a residential area, was eradicated within two weeks of that confirmation.

Sources indicate that south Carolina’s first known detection came on November 9, 2023. In spring 2024, several queens and one early-stage nest were found in Beaufort County, according to Clemson University‘s Department of Plant Industry. In December 2025, a nest was found in York County, South Carolina, just south of the Charlotte metropolitan area. It was the first known nest outside the state’s Lowcountry.

According to recent reports, “This detection prompted us to ramp up surveillance this year in southern Union and Mecklenburg counties,” said Dr. Bill Foote, director of the North Carolina agency’s Plant Industry Division. (Related: Industry News: Faster TensorFlow models in Hugging Face...)

The North Carolina nest was “located and eliminated rapidly,” NC State Extension wrote on August 7. A separate confirmed case had by then developed in High Point.

The species is native to tropical and subtropical areas of Southeast Asia. Adults are 0.75 to 1 inch (1.9 to 2.5 centimeters) long, with a mostly dark body, a banded abdomen and distinct yellow legs.

In a recent development, yellow-legged hornets hover near the entrances of honeybee hives, a behavior called hawking. They pick off workers returning from foraging flights in midair.

Because the hornets attack honeybees, they can cause reduced foraging and colony failure, according to Don Hopkins, North Carolina’s state apiarist.

“This would result in reduced honey and other honeybee products, reduced crop production due to decreased pollination by honeybees and native pollinators, making the hornet a serious threat to North Carolina’s beekeeping community,” Hopkins added.

A 2019 study in Western Europe reported evidence of homing failures among foraging bees and of “foraging paralysis” in colonies with hornets at their entrances. Its simulations pointed to that paralysis as an notable mechanism underlying winter colony collapse.

Georgia’s Yellow-Legged Ledger of October 22, 2026, lists five nests for 2023, 47 for 2024 and 63 for 2025.

For 2026, the bulletin counts 144 nests found by that date, for a total of 259 since the first detection.

In South Carolina, 643 Lowcountry nests had been removed or scheduled for removal in 2026 as of October 26. Seven more were removed in the Upstate and Midlands.

That month, the first nests in Anderson and Kershaw counties were discovered and removed. Clemson’s program had 4,336 traps set across 14 South Carolina counties.

In a recent development, the hornet builds egg-shaped paper nests above the ground, often in trees. Nests can hold up to 6,000 workers and last a single year.

Recent findings suggest that late-season nests usually sit at least 33 feet (10 meters) above the ground. In November, most of the colony dies, and only newly produced queens survive to lie dormant from December to March.

In spring, each queen starts a nest about the size of a tennis ball.

The hornet moves to new areas primarily through human activities such as trade, travel and tourism. In Europe, where it is known as the Asian hornet, the species has been established since 2004.

“The genetics indicate it’s not from the European population,” stated Lewis Bartlett, an assistant professor at the University of Georgia, of the U.S. hornets. “It likely came directly from its native range in Asia.”.

Industry observers note that on April 30, 2026, a grain inspector photographed a live hornet on a vessel at the Port of Vancouver in Washington. State entomologists agreed it appeared to be a yellow-legged hornet, the Washington State Department of Agriculture reported.

Federal guidance is to never disturb a hornet or a potential nest. Residents should take a photo only if it is safe and record the exact location.

Only state or federal responders should remove nests. Attempting removal is dangerous and may help the hornet spread if the queen escapes.

The insect can be confused with other common wasps and hornets, including the northern giant hornet.

“When in doubt, point it out,” stated Jamie Ellis, director of the University of Georgia Bee Program.

In North Carolina, homemade traps should be set in March to catch new queens, in May to catch workers, or both. Trapping can continue until the first frost in the fall.

The information was obtained from the USDA Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service.
