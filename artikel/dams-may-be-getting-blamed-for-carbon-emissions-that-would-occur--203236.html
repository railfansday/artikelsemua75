Dams may be getting blamed for carbon emissions that would occur naturally

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A global study suggests that conventional carbon accounting may substantially overestimate certain greenhouse gas emissions directly attributable to dams.

When a river is dammed, decomposing organic matter in the resulting reservoir can release carbon dioxide and methane.

That is why reservoirs now sit in national greenhouse gas inventories. It is also why some people question hydropower’s green credentials.

But much of the organic carbon carried into reservoirs would have decomposed naturally in rivers and estuaries even without dams.

A new study estimates that dams add about 21.5% to the emissions from the carbon they trap, once a free-flowing river’s own emissions are counted.

For the type of carbon tracked in the study, the net emissions attributable to dams were less than a fifth of conventional estimates.

The research was led by Wenqing Shi of Nanjing University of Information Science and Technology.

The co-authors came from the Nanjing Institute of Geography and Limnology, the University of North Carolina’s Institute of Marine Sciences, and Aarhus University in Denmark.

“High carbon emissions from reservoirs have sparked controversy over dams,” the team wrote.

In an interview with Earth.com, Shi noted that a fair test compares the same river with and without the dam, from the reservoir down to the estuary.

“We need to distinguish emissions that occur in a reservoir from emissions actually added by the dam,” Shi noted.

Recent findings suggest that rivers carry about 940 million tons (850 million metric tons) of land-grown carbon to the ocean every year. Some of it decays in the channel on the way down.

Most of it settles in estuaries, where it decays too, releasing the same two gases.

A dam interrupts the trip. More than 70,000 large dams now stand on the world’s rivers, and each one catches carbon that would otherwise have kept moving.

It has been revealed that the usual accounting charges every ton of gas rising from a reservoir to the dam that made it. That treats the carbon as if it would have stayed put without the dam.

Shi’s team ran the numbers both ways for 5,346 dammed reservoirs around the world: once with the dam in place, and once with the river flowing freely to the sea.

Sources indicate that methane traps far more heat than carbon dioxide, so the team converted it to a carbon dioxide equivalent, at 27.2 times the warming over a century. That put the two scenarios on one scale.

The two rivers look very different. In the free-flowing version, about 4.9% of the carbon decays in the channel and the rest reaches the estuary.

In the dammed version, 88.3% of the carbon arriving from upstream is caught in the reservoir, 0.5% decays in the channel below, and 11.1% makes it to the estuary.

Industry observers note that nearly every dam, 99.4% of them, raised emissions compared with the undammed river. The average increase was 21.5%, and the team’s uncertainty range runs from 8.3% to 32.6%. (Related: Industry News: huggingface_hub v1.0: Five Years of Buil...)

The conventional method counts all reservoir emissions as the dam’s doing. Measured against it, the net effect is 18.6% of the old figure.

“We expected the baseline to matter, but the size of the difference was striking,” Shi told Earth.com.

Shi emphasized that the 18.6% figure covers only the land-grown carbon particles the study followed, not every source of reservoir emissions or hydropower’s whole carbon footprint.

“The emissions themselves have not disappeared; what changes is how much can reasonably be attributed to the dam,” Shi noted.

Added up, the world’s dams release an extra 6 million tons (5.4 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide equivalent a year, by the team’s estimate.

Hydroelectric dams account for just over half of that, with irrigation dams next and flood control dams third.

According to recent reports, per ton of trapped carbon, hydroelectric dams add the least, 16.5% against more than 19.4% for every other dam type. They lead the list because there are so many of them and they drain such large areas.

It has been revealed that A few dams even cut emissions. On rivers flowing from cool regions toward warm coasts, carbon held back in cold water decays more slowly than it would in a warm estuary.

What a dam changes most is which gas comes out. In reservoirs, slow water lets organic-rich mud pile up and run out of oxygen, and that is where methane forms.

Free-flowing rivers generally have more oxygenated conditions that are less favorable to methane production.

In the team’s model, methane’s share of the warming from decaying river carbon rose from 4.4% without dams to 22.8% with them, more than a fivefold jump. (Related: Tech Report: Easily Build and Share ROCm Kernels with H...)

“Reservoir conditions can favor methane production, so shifting decomposition upstream can still increase the climate impact, even after accounting for the no-dam baseline,” Shi said.

Reservoir depth and how long water lingers behind the dam were the strongest controls.

Shallow reservoirs and very deep ones both pushed emissions up, while reservoirs between about 65 and 260 feet (20 and 80 meters) deep held them down.

Shi said that spread is one more reason not to apply a single correction factor to every reservoir.

The model follows only one kind of carbon, the particles washed in from the land. It leaves out carbon in soil flooded when a reservoir fills, carbon dissolved in the water, and algae grown in the reservoir itself.

All three can add to a reservoir’s emissions. So the team called its 18.6% a conservative figure, and the true share pinned on dams could be higher.

Those other sources, and hydropower’s full life-cycle impacts, still have to be assessed separately, Shi noted.

The researchers also treated all incoming carbon alike, though carbon from different watersheds decays at different rates.

No global dataset yet describes how a dam changes the flow of the river below it, so that effect is missing too.

According to recent reports, “A priority is to make the estuary baseline more site-specific,” Shi said. That means measuring the carbon arriving, how much of it is buried or decays, and the gases that come off.

The team also wrote that any carbon accounting for a dam should run from the reservoir all the way to the estuary.

Until that happens, the figure in a hydropower plant’s carbon ledger depends on where the ledger decides the river begins.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
