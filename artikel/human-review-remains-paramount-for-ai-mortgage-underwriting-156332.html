According to recent reports, artificial intelligence is helping mortgage lenders and due-diligence firms review loan files and extract borrower information, but integrating AI into underwriting processes isn’t a one-and-done experience. The technology still needs human oversight, experts say, especially when loans involve unusual circumstances or require judgment beyond standard guidelines.

Industry observers note that at Incenter Diligence Solutions, AI has accelerated document indexing and data extraction, according to president Pamela Hamrick. Technology can now sort and categorize thousands of pages of loan documents in minutes, replacing a process that once required employees to manually separate and label files. Hamrick said that while AI can extract information from standardized mortgage documents like loan estimates and closing disclosures, borrower-provided records — including pay stubs and bank statements. — are harder to process because their formats vary.

AI, however, can identify inconsistencies across documents and summarize them for underwriters to investigate.

“The technology is identifying the questions, and the inconsistencies and the anomalies, between all of this documentation, and summarizing that for an underwriter or a reviewer … to dig deeper and make a decision about whether or not what the AI has identified is a real risk — or if it’s a minimal risk or maybe it’s no risk at all because of the particular scenario,” Hamrick mentioned in an interview with HousingWire. Julia Curran, senior managing director of residential AI products at SitusAMC, remarked the organization uses AI to re-underwrite closed loans in the secondary market, checking regulatory and underwriting guidelines as well as data accuracy before loans are reviewed for securitization.

According to Curran, SitusAMC builds AI agents around processes established by human subject matter experts and tests their results against loans previously reviewed by people. She said the company runs repeated tests until the system reaches at least 95% accuracy.

Sources indicate that but the technology still needs the human touch. Curran mentioned the system sends blank or inconsistent fields to human reviewers, and final credit decisions require judgments that cannot be reduced to individual data points.

“A final credit decision shouldn’t be made by the machine because there’s other things to consider besides the specific data points,” Curran mentioned.

Hamrick added: “Companies that fully rely on AI are potentially going to have misses in some other areas where a human might have been able to make a different decision.”.

Recent findings suggest that curran also warned about the AI model supplying information not supported or provided by the documents submitted. She described a case in which a model supplied a solar panel number that was not included in a particular loan file. The answer was correct, but the system had apparently drawn on information from other files.

It has been revealed that “Even though it’s right, I don’t want that answer,” Curran remarked. “I want to stay within the four walls of this file.”.

The executives noted lenders should test AI against established human processes before relying on it for underwriting.

It has been revealed that hamrick recommended introducing the technology in one part of the workflow at a time, comparing its results with human reviews before expanding its use. She also remarked lenders should prepare employees for changes by retraining them for tasks that require more human intervention.

“The best thing that we can do as leaders is look where the technology can ultimately take over, and then take the folks that are doing those roles and train them to be better at the things that require more human intervention,” she mentioned.

It has been revealed that curran said quality control requires testing models across different loan types and scenarios. SitusAMC maintains a test bank of hundreds of previously reviewed loans, including purchases, refinances, adjustable-rate mortgages and loans with rate buydowns. When a model changes, the firm runs its prompts against the test bank to check whether results remain consistent.

These tests add costs that lenders may overlook. Curran noted companies should ask AI vendors how their systems were developed, whether subject matter experts helped establish the processes and whether ongoing regression testing is in place.

It has been revealed that “You want the same answer every time,” Curran said. “The process of taking a non-deterministic process and making it deterministic takes a lot of time and effort.”.
