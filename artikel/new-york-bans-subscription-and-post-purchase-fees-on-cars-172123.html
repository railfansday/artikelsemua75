There's two distinct types of "post-purchase services", and this law is only applying to one of them.

In a recent development, on one hand you have services that act more like an "interface" for an external service. This could be red light camera warnings, or in-car wifi over cellular, or maybe keeping your navigation maps updated. Those are allowed because they're a genuine ongoing service that by design must be maintained and provided externally. It's like a newspaper subscription, and it makes sense for that to be an ongoing charge.

In a recent development, the other type is a hardware-activation. This is to enable and allow continued use of hardware that's already included in the vehicle, where no substantial outside service is being provided, such as BMW's heated seats. There are several reasons manufacturers do this, including it being easier to make cars with a standard feature package rather than custom ones a-la-carte, and also to be able to sell you a subscription later that you didn't originally want to purchase without expensive installation after purchase. This is the category Newly introduced York is going after. It's been getting abused in several ways, including forcing users to 'rent' the use of hardware they have purchased and own, and in cases of one-time payment to be able to de-activate it if the car is resold and charge the new owner again to re-activate the hardware.
