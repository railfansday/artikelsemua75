Tech Report: The note should be in the language you read, not the one you spoke

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A meeting in Oulu rarely stays in one language.

Sources indicate that two people start in Finnish, someone joins from Berlin and switches to English, and one sentence goes to Turkish because that is the word that fits. Everyone in the room understands. The recording is fine. The note is where it falls apart: if the note keeps the language it was spoken in, the person reading it at 21:00 reads three languages in one document. Gistsmith separates two things that usually travel together: what was spoken, and what you read.

Sources indicate that the note language is yours to pick. It does not follow the first speaker, the loudest person, or the system language. You set it once, and the note comes out in that language, whatever the room did. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Industry observers note that the numbers as they stand today: you can speak in 35 languages, notes and live translations come in 37, and the app itself speaks 12 — English, Turkish, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese (Brazil), Dutch, Japanese, Korean, Chinese (Simplified) and Finnish. The interface follows your Mac's language. The note language is a separate choice.

The obvious alternative is a per-meeting default: look at the first five minutes, take the dominant language, use it for the note.

According to recent reports, it demos well and fails in real use, because the dominant language of a meeting is a property of the room. The person who needs the note at the end of the day is usually not the one who spoke most in the call.

Industry observers note that A per-meeting default also asks a question at the worst possible moment: right after the call, when nobody wants to answer anything. One setting asks nothing.

There is a real trade-off: a phrase in the original language often is the point. "Se on ihan sama" carries a tone that "it doesn't matter" flattens into nothing.

Recent findings suggest that I have not found a rule that keeps every phrase and still gives you a readable document in one language. Choosing a note language buys readability and pays for it in tone. That is a trade I would make again: a note you skim before the next call has to be readable first.

According to recent reports, the mismatch I did not expect was between the two languages a person uses. Almost nobody changes the language they read in by accident. Almost everybody changes the language they speak in, depending on who is in the room.

Fix the reading side, make it boring, and the writing side can stay as messy as the meeting was.

If you are building anything that turns speech into a document, the question is not only "which language is this?". It is also "which language does this need to be read in?". Those are two settings, and only one of them belongs to the user.

Gistsmith is a meeting notes app for Mac and iPhone, no bot joins the call: https://gistsmith.com.
