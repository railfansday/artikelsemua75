Lindsay Crouse wants people to stop treating "quitter" like a bad word.

In fact, the former New York Times journalist believes that knowing when to quit is a crucial component to a successful, healthy life.

In a recent development, but Crouse didn't always feel that way. In her recent book "The Case for Quitting: The Surprising Benefits of Opting Out," published in September, Crouse shared that she used to push herself to the point of exhaustion at work and as a distance runner and marathoner.

"I'd achieved things I'd never dreamed were in reach, yet it didn't feel like enough. I was doing too much, and somehow it amounted to less than before," she wrote.

Industry observers note that now, Crouse wants to challenge the popular narrative around quitting. She spoke to psychologists, endurance athletes and fellow recovering workaholics about what they gained when they decided to let go. Sources indicate that "We're conditioned to see perseverance and quitting in these very binary ways," she tells CNBC Make It. "We think of people who never give up as people who succeed, and people who quit as people who fail.". (Related: Latest Update: Dividends Aren't What You Think The...)

But in Crouse's view, quitting can actually be a strategic move that allows us to focus on what's vital.

"If we're going to succeed and do our very best, we need to learn to walk away," she indicates. "We need to learn to abandon paths that aren't working in order to find paths that might succeed.".

When you're already struggling with something, "it's really natural to want to escalate your commitment to achieving your goal," Crouse notes.

That could mean putting even more hours into a job that you know deep down isn't a good fit for you, or pushing yourself harder during marathon training because you're frustrated by an injury slowing you down.

But what many people don't realize is that investing more energy into a failing cause "actually only creates more failure," she says.

In those cases, doggedly persevering only "increases the amount that you lose, whether that's energy, whether it's money, whether it's time, or whether it's just your happiness," she indicates. "If you don't cut your losses, those losses are only going to grow.".

To determine whether your approach to a task is becoming "a little unhealthy," Crouse recommends asking yourself these questions:.

Recent findings suggest that "How are you talking about achieving your goal? Are you getting more and more desperate? Are you constantly telling yourself that success is just one beat away, even as evidence might be telling you otherwise?".

If the answer is "yes" to the latter two questions, it might be a sign that you need to take a step back and reevaluate whether it makes sense to keep pushing — or whether it's time to pivot, Crouse says.

It can also be helpful to ask your friends whether they think you're on the right track, she says: "A lot of times, our friends will tell us the truth that we don't want to hear.".

If you still feel like quitting is a sign of failure, Crouse recommends looking at it from a different angle.

Quitting is "one of the most courageous moves you can make," she says, because it "inherently involves risk, and being brave enough to do it means that you have confidence that you can look at something uncertain, take it on and make it work out.".
