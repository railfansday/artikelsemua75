Bitcoin's (BTC -7.22%) price has declined by over 20% in November. At just under $60,000, it's trading more than 50% below its all-time high of about $126,000 from last October. Let's see what caused that decline -- and what its historical performance suggests could happen next. (Related: How Much Does a Will Cost? Complete Price Guide...)

A year ago, many investors expected the Fed to cut interest rates in 2026. Lower rates would presumably weaken the U.S. dollar, making blue chip cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin more attractive, and spark a rotation from conservative, higher-yielding investments into riskier ones. (Related: Compound Interest Calculator: See Your Money Grow...)

Recent findings suggest that long-term catalysts also supported Bitcoin. Its spot-price exchange-traded funds (ETFs), initially approved in early 2024, were attracting more retail and institutional investors. It was also attracting more attention from corporations and governments as a hedge against the debasement of fiat currencies. Investors also expected its next halving -- which halves the rewards for mining Bitcoin every four years -- to exacerbate its scarcity in 2028.

Recent findings suggest that but in 2026, those tailwinds dissipated as the Iran war exacerbated inflation and prompted the Fed to raise its rates for the first time in three years in September. The Democratic sweep in the U.S. midterms also increased the likelihood of tighter restrictions on the crypto market. Those challenges curbed the market's appetite for cryptocurrencies, and Bitcoin's price plummeted.

CRYPTO · As of 2:13 PM ET 11/20/26.

Recent findings suggest that past performance never guarantees future gains, but Bitcoin has a solid record of bouncing from steep peak-to-trough declines. However, each drawdown shook out many investors.

Data source: Bitcoin by the Numbers.

As of this writing, Bitcoin has declined 53% from its all-time high. Therefore, investors shouldn't be surprised if it sinks even lower over the next year before stabilizing. An 80% drop from $126,000 would reduce its price to about $25,000.

According to recent reports, that would be a painful pullback -- especially for investors who bought Bitcoin in late 2025 -- but its historical performance suggests it could bounce back sharply after it bottoms out. Every time Bitcoin crashed, it set newly introduced all-time highs within a few years.

Industry observers note that I'm not saying that's guaranteed to happen this time, but plenty of catalysts -- including interest rate cuts and its next halving -- could drive the bulls back to Bitcoin. While it could certainly get worse before it gets better, I believe Bitcoin will be trading much higher in just a few years.
