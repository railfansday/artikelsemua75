While the administration says its blockade has largely stopped Iranian oil exports, Monday's sanctions detail how Tehran has relied on currency dealers, trading companies and middlemen to conduct business abroad.

Treasury identified approximately $14.7 billion in financial activity potentially linked to Iranian shadow banking between January 2025 and August 2026, underscoring the scale of the networks Washington is now targeting.

Just three days earlier, Treasury declared that Iran had exhausted its seaborne oil supply outside the U.S. blockade, saying no new oil had been loaded onto vessels since the launch of Operation Economic Outcast in August.

Industry observers note that "The regime has exhausted its seaborne oil supply, and it may never see the revenue from the oil it has already sold. The ship is sinking," Bessent said Friday.

In a response to Fox News Digital, Treasury said Iran has not loaded a single barrel of oil onto vessels since Aug. 25, 2026, and claimed Tehran is struggling to access foreign currency and recover proceeds from previous oil sales.

Industry observers note that the department also provided a report showing the Iranian rial has lost roughly 25% of its value since August, while inflation reached 90% in September. Treasury said it is continuing to target alternative financial channels, including cryptocurrency exchanges, as Tehran looks for ways around U.S. restrictions.

Recent findings suggest that but even as the administration claims success in cutting off new oil shipments, Treasury is still pursuing Iranian operatives accused of collecting billions of dollars from previous sales. Friday's sanctions targeted four individuals involved in recovering oil revenues held overseas, some of which Treasury stated had been diverted by middlemen for personal gain.

Bessent's September prediction was hardly his first warning that Tehran was approaching an economic breaking point.

Months earlier, he said U.S. sanctions were "asphyxiating" Iran's economy and warned that the country could soon be forced to shut down oil wells as storage facilities filled up.

By late August, Bessent was predicting Iran's economy could collapse within "weeks or months," as the administration launched Operation Economic Outcast, a campaign aimed at cutting off the regime's remaining revenue.

The warnings continued in October, when Bessent projected Iran's economy could shrink by more than 25% over the year ending in March 2027. Earlier this month, he described the country as experiencing one of the fastest economic collapses in history.

The administration had increasingly turned to economic pressure after months of military strikes failed to force Tehran into an agreement. Washington reimposed its naval blockade in July after an interim deal collapsed, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said in September that the price Iran would pay going forward would be primarily economic, although military action remained an option.

In a recent development, but neither the military campaign nor the mounting economic pressure has produced a broader agreement. Iran continues to demand sanctions relief, access to frozen assets and an end to the blockade, while Washington is pressing Tehran for concessions on its nuclear program and regional activities.

Despite months of escalating pressure, Treasury is still identifying overseas financial networks that have allowed Iran to conduct business outside traditional banking channels.

Iran has spent decades developing ways around American financial restrictions, relying on middlemen, shell companies and foreign currency exchanges to keep trade flowing even as Washington moves to cut off its access to international markets.
