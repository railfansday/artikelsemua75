IBM Consulting’s cyber chief tells companies to assume failure as AI speeds up attacks

Mark Hughes, Global Managing Partner, Cybersecurity Services, IBM Consulting.

Attackers now move at machine speed, but most companies still fix their systems at human speed. That is the view of Mark Hughes, Global Managing Partner of Cybersecurity Services at IBM Consulting. (Related: Restaurant owners left in tears after discovering their...)

Recent findings suggest that hughes leads IBM Consulting’s cybersecurity business worldwide. Before IBM, he was president of security at DXC Technology and chief executive of BT Security. He spoke to TNW by email, after a run of US outages and AI-found flaws in widely used software. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

AI is helping attackers automate large parts of an attack, Hughes mentioned. That covers researching targets, finding weaknesses, and building and refining the attack itself. Agentic AI could let them automate even more of that process.

“We’re not at a point where every attack is autonomous or even AI-driven, but organizations should blueprint for and anticipate this to accelerate in the coming year,” he stated.

According to The Washington Sun, bank chief executives and the NSA have told Congress about the recent US outages. They blamed rushed patches for flaws found by AI, as we reported on 18 November. The Trump administration denies any link between the outages.

Hughes did not comment on the outages themselves. AI is making vulnerabilities “incredibly easy” to find, for defenders and attackers alike, he stated. The major gap is remediation, which still depends on engineering context, testing, validation and coordinated development.

“So we have attackers moving at machine speed, finding and chaining vulnerabilities into viable attack paths, while businesses are still largely remediating at human speed,” Hughes said.

Sources indicate that “The answer isn’t to just patch everything faster. You need to understand which vulnerabilities actually create the most risk to your specific environment, and prioritize those, while making sure the fix itself doesn’t disrupt the systems organizations depend on,” he stated.

Hughes did not say how many IBM clients are struggling with the volume of fixes. He pointed instead to Lightwell. IBM and Red Hat revealed the programme on 28 May. It is a $5bn commitment backed by more than 20,000 engineers.

It has been revealed that lightwell combines AI with engineering expertise to identify, validate and fix vulnerabilities in foundational open source software, Hughes mentioned. That includes versions already running inside complex company systems. It aims to deliver validated fixes that organisations can use in active workflows.

In October, IBM and Red Hat gave an revision. Lightwell had found and fixed more than 400 previously unknown vulnerabilities in widely used Java libraries, they stated. IBM also prepares to bring Anthropic’s Claude Mythos to enterprise customers.

According to recent reports, open source matters so much now because the critical infrastructure people rely on every day runs on it, Hughes remarked. That ranges from banking and healthcare to transportation.

“That widespread reliance also heightens the stakes when something goes wrong,” he noted.

In a recent development, AI is speeding up discovery, and fixes are not keeping pace, he added. Many open source projects still rely on volunteers, he stated, and that explains part of the gap. They have few resources to match the upcoming speed of threats.

Asked what one change a defender should make this quarter, Hughes noted: “Assume failure.” He pointed to the outages that hit critical infrastructure organisations in quick succession. He also pointed to flaws in widely used software such as PHP, uncovered at unprecedented scale. Attackers are already exploiting the PHP2Shell flaws, the US cyber agency CISA confirmed in October.

It has been revealed that “You still want to prevent as many attacks as possible, but no organization can stop every attack, outage or software problem. Disruption is going to happen. What matters is how prepared you are when something goes wrong,” Hughes stated.

Organisations need to be ready for systems to fail, he remarked. The cause might be an attack, a software problem or something else.

According to recent reports, hughes expects 2027 to extend what this year has brought. Attack cycles will keep speeding up as more capable AI becomes easier to get. He also expects more vulnerability discoveries and more automation across attacks.

Sources indicate that companies are also scaling their use of autonomous agents, he said. That brings a whole new set of risks around identity, access and control.

To keep pace, he stated, companies will need to change how they run security.

“Organizations are going to have to reimagine their security operations, moving towards autonomous security, while also making sure the agents acting on their behalf have the proper controls in place to prevent unintended behaviors,” he remarked.
