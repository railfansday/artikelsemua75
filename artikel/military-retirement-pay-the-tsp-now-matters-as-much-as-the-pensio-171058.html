Military Retirement Pay – The TSP Now Matters as Much as the Pension

Under the military’s Blended Retirement System, 20 years earns a pension of 40% of your highest 36 months’ average basic pay, against 50% under the old High-3 proposal. Put at least 5% of your basic pay in the Thrift Savings Plan to get the government’s full 5%, yours after two years even if you leave before 20.

If those 36 months average $60,000 of basic pay, a 20-year career pays $24,000 a year under the blended system and $30,000 under High-3. High-3 still covers most members who joined before 2018 and didn’t opt in. (Related: Breaking: Porting fairseq wmt19 translation system to t...)

Sources indicate that most members never get that far. Military OneSource indicates about 81% of members under High-3 leave with no retirement benefit, and it expects about 85% under the blended system to leave with one. The difference is the Thrift Savings Proposal, the military’s 401(k), where the government puts in 1% of basic pay and matches up to 4% more.

Recent findings suggest that between 7 and 12 years of service, continuation pay gives active-duty members at least 2.5 times their monthly basic pay for agreeing to serve at least 3 more years. At retirement, you can take 25% or 50% of your pension up to age 67 as a discounted lump sum, and your monthly check shrinks by that share until 67. The Survivor Benefit Plan costs 6.5% of the pension you cover and pays your spouse 55% of it after you die.

After you retire, a yearly cost-of-living raise keeps the pension in step with consumer prices. It’s taxed as federal income, though many states exempt it, as the Military Officers Association of America’s state tax map shows. You and your family can stay on TRICARE health coverage for a yearly enrollment fee by enrolling within 90 days of retiring, then switch to TRICARE For Life alongside Medicare at 65.

Recent findings suggest that A medical retirement pays a pension before 20 years to members found unfit for duty with a Defense Department disability rating of at least 30%. VA disability pay is tax-free, but by law it reduces the pension dollar for dollar. Retirees with 20 years and a VA rating of 50% or more get the full pension back, and a combat-related disability can qualify for a separate payment that covers some or all of the cut.

Use the Defense Department’s Blended Retirement System calculator to project your own pension before you sign for continuation pay or pick a lump sum.
