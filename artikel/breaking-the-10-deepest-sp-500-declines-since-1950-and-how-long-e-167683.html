The biggest stock market corrections since 1950, from the 1957 slide to the 2022 bear market, show how deep S&P 500 declines ran and how long recoveries took.

It has been revealed that how far the market falls is only half the story. The other half is how long it takes to climb back.

In early 2020, the S&P 500 lost 33.9% in just 33 days, then hit latest records within five months. The dot-com bust was a different animal. The index fell 49.1% from its March 2000 peak and didn't get back to that high until May 30, 2007, more than seven years later. (Related: Latest Update: How to generate text: using different de...)

Both make Plus500's list of the biggest stock market declines since 1950. Over that stretch, Plus500 counts about 38 corrections of 10% or more and 14 bear markets, drops of 20% or more. In other words, corrections are common, and full-blown bear markets are much rarer.

Sources indicate that the 10 declines here run from 1957 to 2022, from the post-war boom and the inflation-wracked 1970s to Black Monday, the financial crisis, the pandemic crash and the 2022 bear market. Their causes repeat: stretched valuations, rising interest rates, inflation, financial stress and the occasional outside shock.

Industry observers note that the list isn't every bear market since 1950. A few near-misses didn't make the cut, including the 19.9% drop tied to the 1990 Gulf War and the 19.3% fall in 1998. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

Recent findings suggest that each slide covers the size of the drop, what caused it and how long the index took to recover. Recovery times are measured either from the market's peak or from its bottom, and each slide states which. That choice matters: The same rebound looks a lot shorter counted from the low.

According to recent reports, the S&P 500 fell 56.8% from Oct. 9, 2007, to March 9, 2009, as the housing bust turned into a global financial crisis. Counted from that March 2009 low, the index took about four years to reach its old high, on March 28, 2013.

The rebound started fast: The index rose 68% in the first 12 months after the bottom, according to Plus500. The rest of the climb took much longer.

For some investors, it was the second blow of the decade. Anyone who bought at the March 2000 peak had only just gotten back to even in 2007 when the financial crisis knocked them under water again. They didn't get back above their purchase price for good until 2013, 13 years after buying.

And that's in nominal terms. Getting back to the same dollar amount isn't the same as getting back the same buying power, since inflation had chipped away at those dollars along the way.

According to recent reports, the S&P 500 lost 49.1% from March 24, 2000, to Oct. 9, 2002, falling from 1,527.46 to 776.76. At 929 days, about two and a half years, Plus500 calls it the longest S&P 500 bear market since 1950, measured from peak to bottom.

Sources indicate that the crash grew out of the late-1990s frenzy for tech and internet stocks, many of them valued on the growth investors hoped for rather than profits they actually made. When those hopes faded, tech stocks collapsed. As Plus500 puts it, rate cuts couldn't undo a decade of overvaluation.

The climb back took far longer than the fall. The index didn't regain its March 2000 high until May 30, 2007, more than seven years later. From the 2002 bottom alone, that took about four years and seven months.

The math indicates why. A 49.1% drop isn't erased by a 49.1% gain; that would only get the index back to about 1,158. Returning to 1,527.46 took a gain of about 97%. Put another way, $100 invested at the March 2000 peak was worth about $50.85 at the October 2002 low, before dividends.

The S&P 500 fell 48.2% from Jan. 11, 1973, to Oct. 3, 1974. Getting back to the old high took 90 months from the peak, or 69 months from the bottom. The index didn't return to its 1973 level until July 1980.

The math tells you where the time went: The slide lasted about 21 months. The other five-plus years were spent climbing back.

It happened in the middle of the decade defined by stagflation, stubborn inflation combined with weak growth. Consumer prices more than doubled over the 1970s, according to Plus500, citing Robert Shiller's data.

It has been revealed that that makes even the 90-month figure misleadingly rosy. It tracks only the index's price, not what it could buy. By the time the S&P 500 got back to its 1973 level, those dollars bought far less than they had seven years earlier. In real terms, the wait was longer still.

It has been revealed that the S&P 500 fell 36.1% from Nov. 29, 1968, to May 26, 1970, as the excesses of the 1960s Go-Go bull market ran into inflation, Fed tightening and a recession.

Industry observers note that the Go-Go years had pushed stock prices to stretched levels, leaving the market exposed once the economy turned. Spending on the Vietnam War fueled inflation, the Fed tightened in 1969 and the economy slipped into recession late that year. Stocks turned first, peaking before either the Fed's moves or the downturn.

Sources indicate that then corporate profits shrank. When earnings fall but prices don't, stocks suddenly look even more expensive, and share prices got squeezed.

The decline lasted about 18 months. The index regained its 1968 high on March 6, 1972, 21 months after the bottom and 39 months after the peak. After a 36.1% drop, getting back to even took a gain of about 56.5%.

The S&P 500 lost 33.5% from Aug. 25 to Dec. 4, 1987. The decline is best known for Black Monday, Oct. 19, when the Dow fell 22.6% in a single day, still the largest one-day percentage drop in U.S. stock market history.

The crash came in the middle of the decline, 55 days after the August peak, and the index kept sliding for another 46 days before bottoming.

Plus500 points to four causes: overvaluation, rising interest rates, program trading and portfolio insurance. Program trading used computers to fire off huge batches of orders at once. Portfolio insurance, meant to limit losses, automatically sold stock futures as prices fell, which pushed prices lower and triggered even more selling. There were no circuit breakers to stop the spiral; those didn't exist yet.

Recent findings suggest that the Fed moved promptly. Alan Greenspan, just two months into the job as Fed chair, put out a statement promising to keep money flowing through the financial system, and the Fed cut rates.

In a recent development, unlike most declines on this list, no recession followed. The economy kept growing through 1987 and 1988, and corporate earnings held up. The S&P 500 regained its August high on July 26, 1989, 23 months after the peak and just under 20 months after the bottom.

The S&P 500 fell 33.9% in just 33 days, from a record close on Feb. 19, 2020, to a low on March 23. Plus500 calls it the fastest bear market on record. Most big declines since 1950 took months or years to play out. This one happened in a few weeks.

According to recent reports, the speed came down to the trigger. The selloff was driven by an outside shock, the pandemic, not a breakdown inside the financial system. And the response was enormous.

The Fed cut interest rates to 0%-0.25% on March 15, and on March 23, the day the market bottomed, it announced open-ended bond buying, known as quantitative easing, with no cap on its purchases. Days later, Congress passed the $2.2 trillion CARES Act.

The recovery was just as fast. The index climbed back to its February peak in August 2020, 126 trading days after that high, the fastest bear market recovery in at least 50 years at the time, according to Plus500. Over the 12 months after the bottom, the S&P 500 gained 74%.

All told, 2020 squeezed a full bear market cycle, from record high to bottom and back to a newly introduced record, into about six months.

In a recent development, the S&P 500 fell 27.9% from Dec. 12, 1961, to June 26, 1962, and got back to its old high on Sept. 3, 1963.

Years of post-war optimism had pushed valuations high, especially in hot technology stocks, then nicknamed "tronics.".

The slide is named for President John F. Kennedy, whose administration picked a public fight with the steel industry in April 1962 over price increases. It went well beyond harsh words: The Justice Department opened a grand jury investigation into possible price-fixing, and FBI agents highlighted up at steel companies with subpoenas, according to Time. The clash rattled corporate confidence and hit industrial stocks.

Cold War tensions were rising too, building toward the Cuban Missile Crisis that fall, though the market had already bottomed by then.

Shortly after the low, in July 1962, the Fed cut margin requirements from 70% to 50%, meaning investors could buy stock with less of their own cash. The index took 14 months from the bottom to recover, and 21 months from the peak.

It has been revealed that the S&P 500 fell 25.4% from Jan. 3 to Oct. 12, 2022, as inflation surged and the Federal Reserve raised interest rates aggressively. It then took 15 months from the bottom to reach a latest high, which it finally did on Jan. 19, 2024.

Counted from the January 2022 peak, the full round trip took about two years.

It's the most recent decline on the list, and one of two from the 2020s, along with the COVID crash two years earlier.

In a recent development, for context, Plus500 puts the S&P 500's average gain in the 12 months after a bear market bottom at 47%. That's an average of past recoveries, not a forecast for any single one.

Industry observers note that the S&P 500 fell 27.1% from November 1980 to August 1982. Then it bounced back fast, regaining its prior high in November 1982, just three months after the bottom.

The decline itself ran 622 days, roughly 20 months, much longer than the typical bear market since 1950, which lasted about 13 months on average.

The backdrop was Fed Chair Paul Volcker's war on inflation. To break double-digit price increases, the Fed held interest rates at punishing levels, helping push the economy into a deep recession in 1981 and 1982. Newly elected President Ronald Reagan backed Volcker, giving the Fed the political cover to keep rates high as long as it took.

Counted from the November 1980 peak, the round trip took about two years, almost all of it spent falling. That three-month rebound was far quicker than the 14 months it took after the 1962 Kennedy Slide or the 15 months after the 2022 bear market.

In a recent development, the S&P 500 fell 20.7% from July 15 to Oct. 22, 1957, a quick, 99-day drop that's the only 1950s entry on the list.

Its short length was typical of the era. Declines in the 1950s and 1960s averaged about 178 days, and every one of them recovered within seven to 14 months. The 1957 slide fit that pattern.

It was also the shortest of the three pre-1970 declines on the list, about half as long as the 1962 Kennedy Slide and less than a fifth as long as the 1968-1970 bear market.

Later crashes played out very differently. The dot-com bust and the 1980-1982 bear market each dragged on for well over a year from peak to bottom.
