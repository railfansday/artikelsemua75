From the start of 2026 to its peak on Oct. 6, the S&P 500 index (^GSPC -3.70%), the measuring stick for how the U.S. equity market is performing, posted a price return of 14%. Since that all-time high, though, the benchmark has fallen about 10% (as of Nov. 19). (Related: Industry News: One Year Since the “DeepSeek Moment”...)

Recent findings suggest that investors might be worried about a few things. They could be more skeptical about all the artificial intelligence (AI) data center spending that's taking place. Elevated inflation and rising interest rates are stealing the show from a macro perspective. And we're past a midterm election cycle that saw the Democrats take control of Congress, introducing regulatory uncertainty. (Related: Hakeem Jeffries and his team will seek to deliver a new...)

Sources indicate that step back. Even after its recent pullback, the S&P 500 index has risen 229% in 10 years. Here's what a $1,000 investment made in the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -3.42%) a decade ago could have become today.

In a recent development, the S&P 500 index climbed at a 12.6% annualized rate over the past decade, before dividends. Including dividends, a $1,000 investment in the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF 10 years ago would be worth about $3,953 today. That's a 14.7% annualized total return. Investors almost quadrupled their money.

The recent drop is a blip on the massive-picture radar. Even after sliding since early October, the index is still up slightly in 2026.

Recent findings suggest that what powered the gains? It's mostly earnings. In December 2016, the S&P 500 traded at a forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of 17. This compares the index's price to the profits analysts expect over the next 12 months. Today, the multiple is around 18, slightly below its 10-year average.

Industry observers note that based on this metric, the valuation barely moved. Forward earnings per share, meanwhile, jumped from $133 (at the end of 2016) to $416 in early November of this year, a 212% increase. Investors didn't make money by paying higher prices. They built wealth because American businesses became far more profitable.

According to recent reports, one sector stands above the rest. Information technology, measured by the Vanguard Information Technology ETF (VGT -7.00%) delivered an annualized total return of roughly 22.7% in the last decade. AI, cloud computing, and surrounding internet-based secular trends made trillion-dollar market caps a common theme. The tech sector now represents 37% of the S&P 500 index. VOO investors own those winners automatically, for an expense ratio of just 0.03%.

Those gains weren't easy to hold on to. The S&P 500 index fell nearly 20% in late 2018, crashed 34% during the COVID-19 panic, slid 25% in the 2022 bear market, and dropped 19% during the 2025 tariff scare. This year alone has brought two declines of 8% or more. Volatility is just a part of the game.

Each time the market fell, selling felt like the right move. But each time, the index eventually bounced back and went on to latest highs.

Industry observers note that investors who trade in and out usually pay for it. Morningstar's (MORN -0.30%) latest Mind the Gap study found that the average dollar in U.S. mutual funds and ETFs earned 8.7% per year over the decade ended 2025. The funds themselves returned 9.9%. The difference is due to poorly timed buying and selling.

Missing a few days hurts, too. According to J.P. Morgan (JPM -3.03%) Asset Management, sitting out the 10 best days over the 20-year period from 2006 through 2025 would have cut an investor's annualized return from 11% to 6.6%, an enormous difference.

Dollar-cost averaging is the solution. This means investing a set amount on a regular schedule, regardless of price. There's no guessing involved. Adding steadily in this manner to the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF is no doubt one of the simplest ways to build wealth.

When the market is down, like it is now, hang in there.The best results of the past decade came from holding through every pullback.
