Industry observers note that before you buy a vacation home to rent by the night, base your math on how often similar rentals nearby are booked over a full year, not a seller’s projection or the peak season. Make the offer only if local rules allow nightly rentals and the home profits after cleaning, platform fees, insurance, taxes, and any manager’s cut.

Unlike a long-term rental, a vacation rental earns only on the nights guests book, while its mortgage, property tax, and insurance come due either way. (Related: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons in a year whil...)

In a recent development, take a cabin that rents for $230 a night and costs $19,000 a year for its mortgage, property tax, insurance, utilities, and upkeep. Each booked night adds $40 for cleaning, supplies, and booking platform fees, and a manager takes 25% of revenue. At 70% occupancy, meaning guests book 70% of the year’s nights, the listing agent’s spreadsheet reveals the cabin earning about $15,000 a year after costs. If it books 85% of its summer weekends but, like similar cabins nearby, only 35% of the year, it loses about $2,000 a year. (Related: Jimmy John’s reveals Christmas Vacation meal with exclu...)

According to recent reports, don’t count on that loss to cut your tax bill. When guests stay seven days or fewer on average, the IRS’s passive-loss rules don’t count the home as a rental activity. Its losses, depreciation included, then lower the tax on your salary only if you materially participate, such as working on it over 100 hours a year and no fewer hours than anyone else, including a manager.
