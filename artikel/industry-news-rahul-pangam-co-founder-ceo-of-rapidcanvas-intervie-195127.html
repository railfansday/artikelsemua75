Rahul Pangam is Co-Founder and CEO of RapidCanvas, where he focuses on helping enterprises move AI applications from concept into production. Before RapidCanvas, he co-founded Simility, which was acquired by PayPal. His work centers on building AI systems that fit real business workflows and remain useful as data, requirements, and operating conditions change.

RapidCanvas is a managed AI execution firm that combines an agentic AI platform with field AI engineers to develop and maintain enterprise applications. Its approach brings together business context, reusable production infrastructure, and ongoing evaluation, with customers retaining ownership of their context, solution IP, and readable code. Before co-founding RapidCanvas, you and Uttam Phalnikar built Simility, which was acquired by PayPal. What did that experience reveal about the challenges of deploying enterprise AI, and how did it shape your decision to start RapidCanvas?

Fraudsters change tactics the moment you catch them, so a model that was right last quarter can be wrong today. That’s the lesson Simility taught us about enterprise AI. Every customer had different data, risk tolerances and processes, and a wrong call cost real money. Most of our effort went into fitting the system to each customer’s workflow, capturing how their best analysts decided and giving the business enough visibility and control to trust it. It has been revealed that A platform alone doesn’t make a market. You pick an urgent problem, prove value in production and expand from there. PayPal highlighted how much more valuable the technology became once it plugged into existing distribution and infrastructure.

It has been revealed that generative AI made prototypes cheap. Dependable business systems are still hard to build. RapidCanvas covers the full path from business problem to governed production application, so companies don’t have to choose between a tool they staff themselves and a consulting project that never repeats.

Gartner predicts that 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built by vendors’ forward-deployed engineers by 2028 because of rising costs and difficulty evolving those systems independently. RapidCanvas also pairs a platform with field AI engineers. What makes your approach different, and where do you agree with Gartner’s concerns?

According to recent reports, gartner has the failure mode right. When the field engineer becomes the product, the customer has bought dependency. The context and code sit with a few people outside the firm, and every change turns into a latest engagement.

According to recent reports, our engineers have two jobs: capture the business context a generic platform can’t see, and turn repeated work into product. Each deployment should make the next one less custom. They start from reusable production infrastructure and domain patterns, and the customer’s definitions, rules, workflows and decision history live in an Enterprise Context Engine the customer owns.

Customers also own their solution IP and get readable code. They can run in their own cloud, a managed environment or SaaS, and nothing ties them to one model provider. We stay accountable after go-live without making the customer dependent on us. The test is whether the customer can see what the system knows, how it’s evaluated, what it’s allowed to do and how to change it. If the answer is no, Gartner’s concern is justified.

In a recent development, you emphasize that your engineers remain accountable after applications go live. What does that accountability mean in practice when an application’s accuracy declines, business requirements change, or the expected financial return fails to materialize?

Recent findings suggest that too many vendors declare victory at go-live. That’s when our job really starts, because running software and valuable software are two different things.

In a recent development, before launch, we agree with the customer on the business outcome and the technical bar. We test on their own data, set benchmarks with known answers and turn those criteria into a regression suite. After launch, we track accuracy, groundedness, human acceptance, errors, latency, cost and adoption. When performance slips, we diagnose the whole system before blaming the model. The cause could be data drift, a schema change, a upcoming business rule, a prompt, a model patch or a workflow we never captured correctly. Every fix has to clear the same bar before consumers see it.

According to recent reports, when the financial return doesn’t show up, the model, the workflow or the business hypothesis is wrong, and each calls for a different response. We might recalibrate, narrow the application’s authority, redesign the process, swap in conventional automation or stop the use case. Keeping a project alive because everyone is invested in it is the worst outcome. AI has to earn its place in the operation.

It has been revealed that rapidCanvas offers custom AI applications through subscription pricing. How do you keep costs predictable as customers add integrations, increase usage and request changes, and what are the limits of what the subscription covers?

Predictable pricing comes from productizing the repeatable work and being upfront about what varies.

In a recent development, customers pay a flat monthly subscription based on their exact needs. It covers customized solutions built with our experts, full platform access, user licenses, integrations, monitoring, maintenance, support and training. They can add or drop solutions and build newly introduced ones on the platform without opening a services project every time the business changes. That works because we reuse tested infrastructure and domain patterns, use deterministic software where it’s the better tool and route each task to the model with the right balance of accuracy, latency and cost.

Industry observers note that the price does assume a defined user population, deployment model, infrastructure footprint and support level. If a customer materially expands any of those, or moves into a upcoming data environment, we agree on the change before the economics shift. Normal evolution of an agreed solution stays in the subscription, while a materially new footprint is a commercial conversation. Customers should never see a change order for the routine work of keeping production AI useful.

Your Enterprise Context Engine captures business processes, rules and decision history. How do you turn undocumented employee knowledge into context an AI system can reliably use, particularly when people disagree about how a process should work?

Recent findings suggest that tribal knowledge only becomes usable context once it has a source, an owner and a decision rule. Dumping transcripts and documents into a large context window just creates a bigger pile of unstructured data.

Sources indicate that we start with what the company already has: documents, system records, schemas, procedures and decision history. Then our engineers sit down with process owners and frontline employees to capture what those systems miss, including definitions, exceptions, approval paths and the reasons behind judgment calls. The Enterprise Context Engine turns all of it into structured, versioned context. Every fact traces back to a source, and every rule carries an owner and a change history.

When people disagree, that tells you the process was never standardized. The system surfaces the conflict, and we work out whether it reflects a legitimate variation by region, product or business unit or whether the firm needs one approved rule. A named process owner decides, and we record the decision, exceptions, effective date and approval. Until the business resolves it, the AI shouldn’t pretend it has. That call stays with a person.

Sources indicate that how do you decide whether a customer’s problem needs an autonomous agent, a predictive model or conventional automation? Can you share an example where a simpler approach proved more effective than an agentic system?

Industry observers note that the most mature AI architecture usually has less AI than people expect. Each approach should do the job it’s best at.

Conventional software or rules should carry most of the load when the logic is stable, repeatable and explainable. A predictive model may be enough when the business needs a forecast, score or probability. Agents make sense for workflows that involve multiple steps, changing information, tool use and judgment within clear limits. Most serious applications combine all three, with people keeping authority over the highest-stakes decisions.

Invoice reconciliation shows why. One customer had to reconcile invoices across 15 entities and eight currencies. Most of that work called for normalization, matching logic and consistent rules. AI added value on the ambiguous cases, and people reviewed the exceptions. The application went live in three months and now matches more than 90% of invoices automatically. A fully autonomous agent would have added variance to a process that depends on exactness. The goal is the best business outcome with the least unnecessary complexity.

Recent findings suggest that reusing components across deployments can improve economics, but each business has different requirements. Which parts of an AI solution can you safely standardize, and how do you validate that a component proven for one customer will work for another?

Standardize the scaffolding and leave the judgment to the customer. Every enterprise application needs production engineering, and very little of it is unique to any one manufacturer.

We standardize authentication, role-based access control, data connectors, deployment, audit logging, security scanning, monitoring, testing, error handling and the evaluation harness. We also reuse domain patterns for recurring problems like invoice reconciliation, document extraction and forecasting. That gives every engineer a real head start.

Recent findings suggest that what stays customer-specific is the business context: terminology, data shape, thresholds, approval hierarchy, exceptions, risk tolerance and outcome measures. We treat reuse as a hypothesis. A component that worked for one customer gets us to validation faster but never lets us skip it. Every deployment is tested on the customer’s own data against acceptance criteria for that workflow, including edge cases, access controls, security, load and end-to-end behavior. Reuse shortens the time it takes to produce evidence, and the evidence bar stays where it was.

What testing and monitoring should an enterprise require before allowing an AI application to take consequential actions? How do you handle model updates, unexpected behavior and decisions that need human approval?

It has been revealed that autonomy is a delegation decision before it’s a model decision. Companies already have rules for delegating authority to people, and AI needs the same discipline.

It has been revealed that before an application takes a consequential action, the company should define what the system can see, what it can recommend, what it can do on its own and what needs approval. Each task type needs an acceptance threshold, examples from the customer’s own data and a trace from every output back to its source, rules and model. The application also needs access controls, end-to-end and load testing, a named owner, an audit trail and a rollback path.

Recent findings suggest that in high-stakes workflows, recommending and acting should be separate steps. Payments, claims, regulated decisions, material financial commitments and unusual exceptions go to a person until the evidence supports more autonomy. Model updates should be handled like software releases, with any newly introduced model, prompt or schema change clearing the same regression suite first. If behavior turns unexpected, the system should stop or narrow its actions, route the case to review and roll back if needed. Autonomy gets earned one workflow at a time and stays revocable.

RapidCanvas claims more than 40% of its customers now run multiple AI use cases in production. Could you walk us through a customer’s progression from its first deployment to broader adoption, including measurable results and anything that did not work as expected?

That 40% is the number I watch most closely. A successful pilot is good evidence, but the real proof is a customer asking for a second and third use case because the first one changed how the business runs. Here’s what that looks like in practice.

Industry observers note that one North American channel incentive management platform started with claims processing. We captured the rules behind the workflow and built controlled paths to approve, hold, adjust or reject each claim. The decision engine now applies 499 business rules and has processed more than 11,000 claims. In one recent quarter it adjudicated 494 claims, approving 297, declining 77 invalid ones and holding 84 for review.

Recent findings suggest that once that workflow was stable and the customer could see how decisions were made, the relationship grew to four use cases across claims adjudication, program intake and fraud mitigation. Each fresh one started with the context, controls and infrastructure the first had already established.

According to recent reports, not everything moved at the same pace. The fraud work stayed in design longer because the outcome definition and telemetry weren’t ready, and we had no results we were comfortable claiming. I’d rather delay a use case than automate something we can’t yet evaluate. A good first deployment doesn’t make every neighboring process ready for AI.

As AI automates more application development, how will the role of field AI engineers change? What expertise should enterprises retain internally so they can understand, govern and eventually modify their systems without becoming dependent on a vendor?

Industry observers note that code is getting cheaper, which makes judgment worth more. Field AI engineers will write less of the undifferentiated foundation and spend more time understanding the business, choosing the architecture, defining evaluations, designing exception paths and setting controls.

The role moves toward architect, adviser and validator, and the technical work becomes more consequential. The key skill is knowing where AI belongs, where deterministic software does better, what evidence to require and how the system should behave when it’s uncertain. The strongest engineers will translate between domain experts, data, software and governance, with no single model at the center.

Industry observers note that enterprises don’t need to build an AI lab, but they should keep the expertise a model can’t replace. That means a business or product owner accountable for the outcome, domain owners who know the exceptions, data and architecture leadership, security, risk and compliance expertise and enough engineering skill to inspect the code, integrations, configuration, evaluations and change history. The customer should own the durable assets: its context, solution IP, readable code, evaluation suite and change-control record. A good partner leaves the enterprise more capable after every deployment and never becomes indispensable.
