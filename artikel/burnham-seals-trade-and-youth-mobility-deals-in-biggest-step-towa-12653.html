Burnham seals trade and youth mobility deals in biggest step towards EU since Brexit

Agreements reached on food and drink, emissions and to allow young people to move around continent, but may be derailed over student fees.

Andy Burnham has taken the biggest step towards deeper ties with the EU since Brexit, sealing deals to ease trade and create a mobility scheme for young people as he declared a “new world” of cooperation between the two sides. (Related: Hawaiian Interisland Awards — Hop Between Islands...)

At a major summit in Brussels on Friday the EU and UK revealed three deals, on food and drink, emissions trading and a youth mobility scheme, which will allow 100,000 young Britons to live, work, study and travel in the EU, with reciprocal access for Europeans in the UK.

But the trio of deals could be derailed in six months, as the EU threatened to block them unless the UK allows European students to pay domestic-level fees to study at British universities. (Related: British Airways Avios — Short Nonstop Domestic Flights...)

Burnham rejected suggestions there was any confusion over the deals coming into force: “The deal is done. It is there in black and white in front of you,” he said, adding that the legislation would enter parliament next week.

The European Commission president, Ursula von der Leyen, played down the disagreement, saying she was “confident we are going to find a solution”.

Hailing the “historic” agreement Burnham said: “This is a good day for Britain and this is a good day for Europe,” as he argued that the agreements answer “the biggest challenges of our times and the biggest worries that people have”, citing the cost of living, lack of economic growth, illegal immigration and challenges facing young people.

The prime minister remarked he would “brainstorm” with the EU over future ties, while also reiterating that he would not get ahead of public opinion.

Sources indicate that speaking to reporters, Burnham stressed that nothing would happen in the current parliament that went much beyond this deal. The so-called brainstorming process was, he said, “just looking at options in a new world, and having an open discussion about what those might be. It will be to inform the next parliament.”.

According to recent reports, burnham also challenged political opponents, notably Nigel Farage, who was in Brussels on Friday to hold a retaliatory press conference, to justify why they would reject a deal which, he stated, would boost growth and lead to cheaper food and electricity. “Why would anybody turn their back on that?”.

The prime minister noted the deals, which were initiated by Keir Starmer, would add 0.5% to British GDP, a £17bn boost to the British economy.

Farage noted Burnham had been naive and the EU had “used this moment to strongarm the British prime minister over tuition fee cuts for hundreds of thousands of EU students”.

EU leaders sought to play down differences over tuition fees. “We are heading in the same direction and that is what matters,” the European Council president, António Costa, said of the broad relationship. He described the UK as “our closest neighbour, a reliable ally and a natural partner”.

Von der Leyen noted it was up to the UK to decide where the journey was going, adding: “We welcome this brainstorming, so count on us.”.

In a sign of tension in the negotiations, the EU agreed a declaration that the three deals would not come into force without equal treatment on fees for EU students at British universities.

Sources indicate that “The [EU] Council and [European] Commission consider that this agreement is to provide for equal treatment with nationals as regards tuition fees for all students,” stated the confidential statement seen by the Guardian. The EU would not adopt the three agreements before “the conclusions of the negotiations … providing for equal treatment on tuition fees”, it added.

Sources indicate that another potential flashpoint could be the UK’s status as a rule-taker. The food and drink and emissions trading deals require the UK to align with EU rules. British experts will be invited to relevant EU meetings, including dealing with potential animal health emergencies. But Britain will not have voting rights. Burnham rejected suggestions this was a fig leaf, saying it would be “quite a moment” to have British experts back in EU meeting rooms.

France was said to be instrumental in shaping Britain’s exclusion, although Paris argues its position was widely supported and reflects the reality that a non-member state cannot be a decision-maker.

Following the talks, Burnham was invited to a lunch of pea and mint soup, battered North Sea cod and chips, followed by Brussels waffle with fruit.

The summit began with a highly choreographed welcome ceremony in the Europa “space egg” European Council building, where a quintet played the British and EU anthems.

The UK hailed a joint decision to create a “partnership for economic resilience”, as an answer to its concerns over the EU’s Made in Europe prepares. The EU is still finalising legislation that will give EU companies preference when public money is spent on the key technologies, such as renewables and electric cars. The UK has been arguing for Britain to be included in Made in Europe to ensure UK firms are not locked out of European supply chains.

Recent findings suggest that in response, the two sides agreed to set up “a common prosperity and economic security space”, essentially an answer to shared concerns about artificially cheap Chinese goods that are depressing prices across Europe. But questions over whether the UK needs to align with EU trade defences remain open. Burnham mentioned the two sides had reached an agreement “to work together where we have common threats”.

In an echo of the Brexit years, the final deal emerged after late-night talks went to the wire.

The German Social Democrat MEP Bernd Lange called on both sides to resolve the tuition fees glitch to make studying affordable in a way that works for universities: “We can’t open the door to young people and then price them out of walking through it.”.

The EU has welcomed Burnham’s breaking of taboos over rejoining, but also voiced frustration at British negotiating tactics. “The UK has a tendency to make even small questions so difficult,” an EU diplomat stated. “A British prime minister talks about a change of approach … Then in negotiations it seems like British negotiators act the same way they always had.”.
