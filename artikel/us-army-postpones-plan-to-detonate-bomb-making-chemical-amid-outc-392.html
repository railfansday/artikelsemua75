US army postpones plan to detonate bomb-making chemical amid outcry

The controlled detonation can lead to a plume containing lead particles, worrying residents and state officials.

The US army has delayed its strategy to detonate a stash of bomb-making chemicals in south-eastern Iowa as questions swirl about the public health risks.

The army was set to blow up about 5 tons of lead azide in Middletown, Iowa, at noon on Friday, a move it described as the safest way to dispose of the highly sensitive compound.

During the controlled explosion, barrels of the material would be stored inside concrete structures and surrounded by sand-filled Hesco barriers reaching up two stories high.

Army officials advised certain residents of Middletown to evacuate between 8am and 2pm local time, due to the potential for window damage. The vibrations from the two blasts would not last long but could break old windows in town, and the resulting dust was expected to linger for no more than one hour, depending on wind speed, officials stated.

The detonation is also expected to produce a plume containing lead particles.

Officials have released maps showing the potential for lead dust to drift north. Updated maps indicated the plume’s shift, and the army will continue to update the map based on weather. While the army recommends cleaning cars, windowsills and outdoor surfaces after the detonation, residents have taken to social media to voice concern and question whether that will be enough.

Iowa’s top leaders appeared to learn about the plan along with the public. The office of the Iowa governor, Kim Reynolds, was first briefed on Monday about the public advisory, her office said.

Sources indicate that “An unacceptable lack of communication and clarity has made Iowans understandably concerned and angry about this situation,” governor-elect Rob Sand wrote on X on Thursday. “The public deserves to know, if this is the safest option, why other options were worse.”.

Adam Telle, the acting secretary of the US army, alluded to the criticism in his Thursday announcement about the delayed detonation.

“Public communication is essential to public trust,” he wrote on X, adding that he was engaging state officials.

The plan is now expected to proceed on Monday. According to Telle, it’s the “latest possible moment”, before temperatures drop, which could risk destabilizing the material.

Middletown residents learned in March that the army needed to dispose of several dozen barrels containing lead azide, after the ammunition plant paused operations as part of a review and the degraded material was discovered.

“The safety of our people and our neighbors is non‑negotiable,” Lt Col Daniel Nosse, the commander of the plant, said at the time, acknowledging the volatility of the chemical compound used in munitions production.

According to recent reports, the army shared more details during a public meeting in Middletown in October, advising that a detonation was planned for an unspecified date in November. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) on 13 November approved an emergency permit, paving the way for Friday’s detonation.

That permit required the army to track specific rates of potential air and dust dispersion and notify the public five days in advance, prompting Sunday’s original advisory to communities as far as 100 miles (160km) away.

Recent findings suggest that just a few miles north-west, the Danville community school district will close on Friday, the superintendent mentioned in an announcement on Tuesday. Pat Wallace said his initial intention was to keep schools open, but there were enough uncertainties that made canceling “the most responsible decision”.

Industry observers note that “I am not suggesting that the detonation will result in unsafe conditions,” he wrote. “The reality is that we simply do not know exactly what conditions will look like Friday afternoon.”.

In a recent development, in Mediapolis, less than 20 miles (32km) north-east of the ammunition plant, city officials were approached about allowing soil, water and air testing late last month. But they only learned of their potential exposure on Sunday, when the initial map put them dead-center in the dust exposure area. Schools and the public library will close on Friday and the local fire department planned to hose down playgrounds that could be covered in lead particles.

The city of nearly 2,000 residents was no longer in the plume in an updated map.

Industry observers note that “This is probably a first for me. I believe it’s a first for a lot of our citizens,” the deputy city clerk, Amy Stucker, said on Wednesday. “The biggest problem we’re having is the lack of information.”.

Patrick O’Shaughnessy, a professor at the University of Iowa’s department of occupational and environmental health, noted it was encouraging to see that the army and the EPA were doing all they could to minimize the dust, but it was still unknown how much would end up in the atmosphere and on surfaces.

Lead is a neurological toxicant that can decrease brain activity and development, especially in young children, O’Shaughnessy said. He is particularly concerned for toddlers who are at the age of putting everything in their mouth.

According to EPA documents, alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the contents of the drums using other chemicals were considered. But officials noted any proposal that involved handling the drums would increase the probability of accidental, fatal detonation.
