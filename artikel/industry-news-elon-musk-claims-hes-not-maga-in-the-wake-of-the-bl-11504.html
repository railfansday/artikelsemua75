Since President Donald Trump’s reelection in 2024, Elon Musk has been one of his biggest allies in Silicon Valley. The Tesla CEO has served as both Trump’s employee and his friend: He spearheaded the Trump administration’s Department of Government Efficiency, aka DOGE, and wasn’t shy to tell the press that he came to “love” Trump over the course of their work together. But in an interview at this week’s annual DealBook Summit, Musk suddenly sang a different tune. “I’m not MAGA,” Musk remarked. “I’m not sure what MAGA means, but if that means sort of blind adherence to a set of policies by the Republican Party, then I certainly don’t have that.” Musk may claim not to know the full extent of what “MAGA” means, but in the lead-up to the 2024 presidential election, he was happy to embrace the term. Wearing an infamous black MAGA hat to one of Trump’s rallies at Madison Square Garden, Musk told the crowd that he’s “not just MAGA,” but “dark gothic MAGA.” Now, though, after the country’s historic shift to the left in this month’s midterm elections, Musk is positioning himself as bipartisan. “I think there are positives and negatives in both parties,” he said. Musk’s historic midterm spending Musk’s recent actions make it hard to believe he doesn’t consider himself bound to the Republican Party. Ahead of the midterms, the world’s richest man became the largest individual political donor in U.S. history, giving $446 million to conservative PACs including his own America PAC, which he founded in 2024 to support Trump’s presidential campaign. But according to Musk, that record-breaking spending wasn’t in direct support of Republicans, but in support of a politically balanced Congress. His goal—“which failed, by the way,” Musk joked—was for Republicans to hold 51 seats in the Senate in 2029. “I think we want to have at least one of either the presidency, the House, or the Senate be of the other party,” Musk said. But the blue tsunami proves that Musk’s investment didn’t pay off. He states he feels “pretty foolish about having spent so much money and not seeing much for it.” He added: “Probably, if I could rewind the clock, I would have spent less money.” Musk’s donations included hefty sums in his home base of Texas, where two Republican candidates he backed, Gov. Greg Abbott and Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, lost to Democrats Gina Hinojosa and James Talarico, respectively. But Musk is looking on the bright side, saying he believes that Hinojosa “is going to be quite moderate” as governor. “Based on everything I’ve heard about her, I think things will be fine in Texas with Gina as governor,” he said. “I don’t think she’s a far-left governor.” A blue wave in tech? Musk isn’t the only tech titan to downplay their GOP affiliations in the wake of the midterms. Last week, Palantir CEO Alex Karp claimed the software company’s products are “essentially progressive” despite its contracts with the Trump administration, including creating surveillance technology for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). “I’ve been a Democrat my whole life. I grew up in a far-left environment,” Karp said. “I know more about being a progressive than almost anybody on the planet, and I absolutely believe Palantir—all of our products are essentially progressive.” Like Musk, Carp said his support of Republicans has been in service of political moderation, not a right-wing takeover. “My giving is equal Democrat, Republican,” Karp said. “I can tell you, without going into things I’m not gonna disclose, I’m very balanced, and everyone in the political arena knows it. And I’ll tell you what—I’m also not the village idiot.”.
