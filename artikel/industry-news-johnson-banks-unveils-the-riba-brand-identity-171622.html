Johnson Banks rebuilds RIBA for its 200th birthday: the RIBA brand identity turns negative space into a bold typographic mark.

The first thing you see is the space between the letters. RIBA arrives as four solid capitals on a pale wall, each letter built from the gaps around it, the R split into a bowl and a leg, the B into two stacked bowls, the A into a triangle over a flat base. It is a mark about architecture made from architecture itself, walls and openings rather than a drawn symbol, and it holds at every size, from a tote bag to a building banner. Recent findings suggest that the type does the work. Johnson Banks started from a Philip Johnson line, that all great architecture is the design of space that contains, cuddles, exalts, or stimulates the people in that space, and let the negative space inside the four letters carry the idea. The mark can sit on top of a photograph, cut into it, or anchor a corner or an edge, and the same logic runs through a set of drop capitals, letters drawn to open a page like a doorway.

Industry observers note that the old identity, letters drawn by Eric Gill beside a highly detailed symbol, had drifted over twenty-five years into hundreds of sub-brands with no house style holding them together. The new system pulls them back into line, with related identities for RSAW, the Royal Society of Architects in Wales, and RIBAJ, the institute journal, each sitting inside the same approach, and clear guidelines built on accessible fonts so the brand stays useful and durable. Industry observers note that colour went back to the beginning. The historic red, the shade the public knows from RIBA signboards, returns front and centre, running across the tote bag, the drawing stencil and the vertical banner on a brick facade. Against it, the palette stays quiet, stone grey, pale blue, black, so the red reads as a signal rather than a decoration, and the crest, the two lions, survives in a simplified, softened form as a quiet sign-off, debossed in black on black.

Recent findings suggest that the system proves itself on the objects architects use. Book spines carry RIBA, RSAW and RIBAJ in red, white and blue on a wooden shelf. A red stencil ruler cuts the letters into a drawing tool. A poster for the Domestic Building Contract pairs a giant yellow D with a red architectural photograph. And the illustration style, flat geometric shapes in red, blue and green with small cut-out figures, gives the brand a way to talk about people without a photograph.
