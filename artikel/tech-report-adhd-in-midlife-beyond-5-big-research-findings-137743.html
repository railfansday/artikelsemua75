“What helped someone with ADHD in their 20s is necessarily going to help them in their 60s, 70s, and 80s.”.

The experience of ADHD in midlife and older age is largely undocumented but not unimportant.

An estimated 66% of adults with ADHD ages 45 to 54 have no recorded diagnosis, according to an analysis of UK health records. Missed diagnoses and misdiagnoses make research on this population difficult — but not impossible.

Amber John, Ph.D., a researcher at the University of Liverpool, reviewed studies on how the experience of ADHD changes with age during her November 2026 ADDitude webinar, “Aging with ADHD: Newly introduced Research Findings in Midlife and Beyond.”. Recent findings suggest that in clinical interviews, adults with ADHD recalled feeling different growing up but being unable to explain why. A diagnosis helped them see those experiences in a different light.

It has been revealed that “These experiences that previously might have seemed disconnected, for many, started to form this more understandable pattern,” John said. “And that could really change the meaning that someone gives to those past experiences, particularly where they had just been viewed as evidence of personal failings.”.

Recent findings suggest that many people with ADHD develop painstaking routines and coping strategies to manage symptoms. The effort it takes to keep those routines going often goes unseen and can fuel burnout.

“This raises a really vital question about what it is that we actually mean when we say someone is functioning well,” John said. “Understanding someone’s support needs really means that we need to ask about the cost of managing.”.

In a recent development, the experience of ADHD changes as life circumstances change. Menopause, caring for aging parents, and retirement can affect how symptoms play out in daily life and disrupt routines that have helped people cope for decades.

“A way that somebody is using to manage or to cope that’s worked in one period of life might become less useful when health, or demands, or available support changes,” John stated.

In a recent development, in doctors’ offices and workplaces, people with ADHD often shoulder the burden of explaining their needs and advocating for basic support. They can put considerable effort into managing their symptoms and still face barriers to getting care or participating at work.

“There is a limit to focusing on individual-level coping because a person can put really substantial effort into managing whilst the environment actually continues to make participation difficult,” John said.

John’s research highlights the need for ADHD support that adapts as people’s circumstances change. We shouldn’t assume that “what helped someone with ADHD in their 20s is necessarily going to help them in their 60s, 70s, and 80s,” she mentioned.
