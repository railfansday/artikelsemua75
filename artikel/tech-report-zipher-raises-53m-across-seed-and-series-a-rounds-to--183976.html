Zipher has raised $53 million across its seed and Series A rounds to automate how enterprise data workloads run in the cloud. The company stated TLV Partners, Maor Investments and Glilot Capital backed the rounds. It did not disclose how much each round contributed or the valuation.

In a recent development, the funding will go toward research and development and expanding sales, including a larger U.S. enterprise sales operation and growth outside the United States. Founded in 2024 by CEO Dan Coster and CTO Yoav Gill Ben Ephraim, Zipher targets the infrastructure underneath analytics and AI projects: the computing resources that move, transform and process data before people or applications use it.

Industry observers note that data teams have traditionally chosen cluster configurations, job schedules and execution engines ahead of time. That works when a job’s input volume and deadline are reasonably predictable. But a pipeline that processes steady traffic on most days might need considerably more memory during a holiday rush or when a backlog lands. Reserving enough capacity for the largest run can waste money on quiet days; sizing every run for the average can make an unexpectedly large job miss its deadline. Zipher’s product documentation describes an execution layer that learns from earlier runs, looks for recurring changes in demand and adjusts resources accordingly. Customers define targets such as how rapidly a job must finish and how much it should cost. Zipher states its software can then adjust a cluster, route a workload to another engine or rewrite it for another framework. The company lists support for workloads on AWS, Azure and Google Cloud and integrations with tools including Apache Airflow, dbt and Databricks Workflows. (Related: Should I Quit My Job? A Decision Framework...)

One example is deciding when a job can use spare cloud capacity. Amazon EC2 Spot Instances use capacity that AWS can reclaim. That can make them a fit for work that can recover from an interruption, but a poor fit when interruption would put a deadline at risk. Zipher says it monitors spot availability and can adjust resources if capacity is reclaimed or a job runs out of memory. The point is not to use the cheapest infrastructure on every run. It is to weigh the cost of that run against the cost of an interruption or missed deadline.

According to recent reports, hertz uses Zipher for production data workloads, according to the announcement. Gaurav Rastogi, Hertz’s senior director of enterprise data analytics and BI platforms, remarked in a statement distributed by Zipher that the rental tech giant saw compute costs fall by 25% to 40%, depending on the workload, while maintaining consistent performance during seasonal peaks. Zipher does not disclose a common baseline across customers or separate the effect of its various optimizations, so the Hertz figures illustrate one deployment rather than a savings target for every buyer.

Sources indicate that the organization has also published case studies on how customers such as SciPlay and Yotpo use its execution layer. Those examples give potential buyers places to start evaluating fit, but they do not replace a trial that accounts for their own data volumes, cloud contracts and recovery requirements.

Sources indicate that the rise of agent-generated workloads adds a latest dimension to the operational demands of agentic AI infrastructure. Creating another pipeline can be easy; keeping the expanding collection on time and within budget remains an infrastructure problem. Zipher’s approach places those execution decisions in an autonomous layer, with performance targets and budgets defining the outcome it is meant to deliver.

That makes the full cost and reliability of a completed workload more crucial than the price of a single compute instance. Retries, data transfers and missed deadlines all affect the result. Zipher’s funding will support its effort to bring that operating model to more enterprise environments.
