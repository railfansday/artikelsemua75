UK politics live – latest updates.

Theresa May has mentioned she feels “aghast” at Andy Burnham’s initiative for potentially rejoining the EU, arguing in a rare interview that the prime minister should “accept Brexit and move on”.

Speaking to the BBC after Friday’s UK-EU summit, at which Burnham secured the biggest step towards renewed ties with Brussels since Brexit, May stated that a further move towards membership, as talked about by the PM, would dominate the government’s time.

“What I saw was that the extent of those negotiations, the hours that had to be put in,” said May, who took over in No 10 from David Cameron shortly after the 2016 referendum and shaped the basics of the very hard departure deal that Boris Johnson eventually sealed. “It’s across the whole of government. It’s not just prime ministerial time, [it’s] cabinet ministers’ time, officials’ time, to-ing and fro-ing on those negotiations, because you have to negotiate hard in order to make sure that you’re getting out what you want. “That’s one of the reasons why I looked slightly aghast at what Andy Burnham was talking about – further negotiations of going into the EU. As a country, we voted for Brexit.

In a recent development, “When I was prime minister, I was very clear: we should deliver on that vote. So I’m afraid my message to him really is: people voted for Brexit. Brexit is done. Just accept Brexit and move on.”.

Recent findings suggest that may said it was damaging for governments not to trust the results of referendums: “I almost think this is what Andy Burnham is saying to the British people … He wants to pat them on the head and say: ‘You know what? Remember that vote? Well, you got it wrong, didn’t you? So have another go and come out with the right answer’ – what he thinks is the right answer.”.

May’s intervention is an unusual step from the former prime minister, who has typically reserved her public statements since leaving office for areas of cross-party consensus. On taking office, she opted to push for minimal residual ties with the EU as a way to maintain unity in the Conservatives. But she struggled with the Brussels negotiations amid dissent among her MPs, and was ousted in 2019.

Pressed on whether Brexit had been a success, May refused to say, arguing that with global shocks such as Covid and Russia’s full invasion of Ukraine, it was impossible to know.

Asked outright if she believed the UK was in a better or worse state as a result of the decision to leave the EU, she replied: “I don’t think Britain’s state today is about whether or not we left the European Union.”.

The summit in Brussels on Friday saw the announcement of three deals, on food and drink, emissions trading and a youth mobility scheme which will allow 100,000 young Britons to live, work, study and travel in the EU, with reciprocal access for Europeans in the UK.

Burnham, who has condemned the May-Johnson departure deal for hampering growth and gumming up trade, has raised the possibility of even closer ties and that the UK could rejoin the EU in the future.

None of this would happen until after another election, with Burnham committed until then to the Brexit “red lines” of Labour’s 2024 manifesto, which rules out being part of the EU’s customs union or single market.

Recent findings suggest that while the changes in the new deal will be presented to parliament as a bill next week, at the summit, Burnham slightly slowed down the pace of any further moves. He noted he would “brainstorm” with the EU over future ties, with the timetable talked about in terms of months, while reiterating that he would not get ahead of public opinion.

The Conservatives have been pushing back against the deal made on Friday, briefing newspapers on Sunday that signing up to the emissions trading system would add costs to domestic energy bills because of EU carbon taxes.

Downing Street argues the deal will bring down energy and food costs, with Burnham saying on Friday it could add as much as 0.5% to GDP in the long term.
