No Emotional Distress Damages in Contract Suit Over Placenta

From Staton v. Jefferson Health-Abington, decided by Judge Harvey Bartle (E.D. Pa.) Oct. 15, but just noted a few days ago in the Westlaw Bulletin:.

It has been revealed that [Staton] alleges that she contracted with defendant to return her placenta to her after the birth of her son and that Jefferson failed to do so. She relies on a document entitled "Patient Specimen Release Form" which she signed on August 22, 2021. In her prayer for relief, plaintiff seeks the award of "compensatory and consequential damages against [Jefferson] … in an amount to be determined at trial, based on the unique personal, cultural and spiritual value of Ms. Staton's placenta." In addition, she asks the court to "[g]rant such other and further relief as the Court deems just, equitable, and proper." …. (Related: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

Sources indicate that in Pennsylvania, it is the general rule that emotional distress damages are not recoverable for breach of contract. The parties have cited no case and this court has found none where the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania has ever held that emotional distress damages are recoverable in a breach of contract action. However, in D'Ambrosio, v. Pennsylvania National Mutual Casualty Insurance Co. (1981) the Court left the door open….:.

The possibility cannot be ruled out that emotional distress damages may be recoverable on a contract where, for example, "the breach is of such a kind that serious emotional disturbance was a particularly likely result." ….

[Restatement (Second) of Contracts §] 353, which the Pennsylvania Supreme Court referenced by a different number in D'Ambrosio, precludes emotional damages except in very limited circumstances:. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Recovery for emotional disturbance will be excluded unless the breach also caused bodily harm or the contract or the breach is of such a kind that serious emotional disturbance was a particularly likely result.

The Restatement gives examples in Comment a. of cases which fit into the second exception. They are contracts for the proper disposition of a dead body, contracts for the carriage of messages concerning death, and contracts of carriers and innkeepers with passengers and guests….

Sources indicate that in this court's view, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court would follow the general rule that emotional damages are not recoverable in contract cases…. While contracts for the proper disposition of dead bodies allow for distress damages under § 353, plaintiff concedes that she "does not equate a placenta with a decedent's remains.".

It has been revealed that in opposing to the pending motion, plaintiff stresses her allegations that her placenta had personal, cultural, and spiritual value to her and that she suffered emotional distress when it was not returned to her. Even if true, her subjective reaction to the alleged breach of contract is not relevant to the issue before the court. In Pennsylvania, emotional distress damages are generally not recoverable in a breach of contract action. Whether emotional distress damages would ever be recoverable would depend on the type of contract or breach alleged, not on the plaintiff's emotional or mental state.

The standard set forth in § 353 is an objective one. No case suggests that the application of § 353 or the allowance of emotional distress damages would be decided based on the effect of the breach on the particular plaintiff without regard to the type of contract or breach involved. Otherwise, the floodgates would be open to emotional distress damages in virtually every breach of contract action….

The result might in principle have been different in a tort lawsuit for conversion, since emotional distress damages are generally available in such torts; but it's not clear that ejected parts of the body such as the placenta would be treated as property under Pennsylvania law for conversion purposes.

Joseph Hoynoski and Jeffrey Chomco (Marshall Dennehey, P.C.) represent defendants.
