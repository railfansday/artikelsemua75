Gartner’s 2026 Hype Cycle for Agentic AI places AI agents at the Peak of Inflated Expectations. Yet, the firm also predicted more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, due to escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. Put together, those two findings describe a market where buyers are under pressure to adopt agents before they can tell which ones will hold up.

Nearly every planning vendor now calls its product AI-powered, and many of the demos look alike: a chat window, a question about inventory, a quick answer. A planner executive evaluating platforms has to work out which products will truly unlock fresh capability and which just put a flashy chatbot on top of a legacy SaaS. In a recent development, take a common scenario: a shipment delay. In a traditional platform, a planner finds out about the delay via an exception report. They export data, upgrade formulas, rebuild the affected scenarios and present their recommendation in a cross-functional meeting. An AI assistant layered onto that process can proactively summarize the delay. The planner still has to rebuild the plan by hand to see what that suggestion does to other customers, material orders and warehouses. (Related: Latest Update: How we sped up transformer inference 100...)

Agents change the planning process by working from a shared, verifiable model of the business: supplier lead times, inventory targets, production constraints and allocation rules. When the delay comes in, an agent can immediately recalculate the plan, flags which SKUs and customers are now at risk, generates alternatives and highlights the downstream effect of each one. The planner’s work moves from assembling analysis to choosing between options. This enables a real transition from reactive to proactive planning.

This gap shows up fastest under pressure. Tesla faced a cash crunch with 400,000 pre-orders to deliver during the 2018 Model 3 production ramp, and planning had to change faster than the organization’s spreadsheets could keep up. The planning team built a 50-person planning engineering organization and its own software to connect sales and operations planning with day-to-day supply decisions, so a change in one part of the proposal flowed through everything it touched. Over the ramp, the team cut finished-goods inventory from 75 days to 15. Few companies can justify building a planning engineering team of that size. Well-built AI planning tools can give a lean planning team that same ability to replan the whole network when something shifts.

Sources indicate that most of that hesitation comes down to a few practical problems. Planning data is scattered. Demand forecasts sit in one system, purchase orders in another, and supplier commitments often live in email threads and shared drives. An AI agent can only reason about the constraints it can see, so a tool connected to a partial picture will produce confident recommendations that miss the binding constraint.

According to recent reports, much of the most vital knowledge was never written down. Experienced planners know which suppliers run late in Q4, which customers get priority when inventory is tight, and which minimum order quantities can actually be negotiated. Until those rules are captured in the planning model, AI has nothing to work from but historical data.

According to recent reports, many teams also hand workflows to unsupervised agents too early. Edge cases that an experienced planner handles by instinct will break an agent that was never told about them. The projects that stick start with the planner who already does the work. That person defines the workflow, monitors the agent and approves what it produces at set checkpoints. Once planners build a daily habit with the tool, it stays in use, and the knowledge they’ve encoded becomes something the company owns.

It has been revealed that many planning teams will choose next year’s tools in the coming weeks, and vendor demos won’t make that choice easier. Planners are a skeptical lot by nature, and some vendor claims deserve extra skepticism.

According to recent reports, the clearest test is whether a tool can model your process, not the vendor’s. AI-native systems are composable by nature – they offer as much flexibility as a spreadsheet, with the power of an enterprise planning platform.

According to recent reports, here’s how to test it. Bring one workflow your team runs today, with its real quirks: how you allocate scarce inventory across customers, which suppliers get extra buffer, when a planner overrides the forecast. Ask the vendor to set it up on your data and walk through it. An AI-native platform can flex to fit how your team works and let you add steps as the process changes. If the answer is that your team will need to adapt to the system’s standard workflow, you are looking at a legacy platform with a chat window on top.
