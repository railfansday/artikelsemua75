Smart home infrastructure belongs in the original home design

🌐 Source: Housingwire • 📅 13 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (706 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • A connected home is only as dependable as the infrastructure behind it.
  • Recent findings suggest that security technology has become a practical home feature rather than a luxury add-on.
  • Recent findings suggest that expectations have also changed.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A connected home is only as dependable as the infrastructure behind it. Buyers may see cameras, smart locks, thermostats and mobile apps, but the performance of those systems depends on decisions that are largely invisible after construction: cable routes, network capacity, power, equipment locations, ventilation and camera sightlines. When those decisions are postponed until the final stages of a project, the builder and homeowner inherit constraints that are expensive to correct.

Recent findings suggest that security technology has become a practical home feature rather than a luxury add-on. The National Association of Home Builders has identified security cameras, wireless security systems and video doorbells among the leading technology features buyers want.

Industry observers note that technology planning should begin while the architect, builder, electrical team and integrator can still coordinate. The team should identify exterior and interior camera locations, network access-point locations, cable pathways, power requirements, equipment-rack space and ventilation before framing and finishes limit the options. This work does not require a homeowner to choose every device in advance. It creates the pathways and capacity that allow the final system to be designed well. In a recent development, CEDIA’s smart-home wiring guidance recommends a wired backbone, centralized and labeled cabling, adequate conduit and room-by-room planning. Those measures matter because wireless products still depend on a reliable network, and cameras or access-control devices often need both data and dependable power. Installing an extra pathway during construction is usually simpler than opening a finished wall to create one later.

Recent findings suggest that an integrator called after construction may have to work around blocked sightlines, inadequate cable routes, limited power and poorly located network equipment. The resulting installation may require visible cabling, patching finished surfaces or placing devices where installation is possible rather than where performance is best. A camera mounted under an architectural feature may technically cover an entrance while still producing a weak image because of angle, glare or nighttime lighting.

Sources indicate that existing homes can still be improved. The first step should be an assessment of the network, camera placement, available wiring and equipment that can remain in service. Open standards and compatible systems can help preserve prior investments, but compatibility should be confirmed for each project. The lesson for upcoming construction is straightforward: design flexibility while the building is still flexible.

Security systems are moving beyond recording toward software that can classify events, search video and help a homeowner find the moments that matter. That adds fresh planning questions. Where will processing occur? How much bandwidth is required? What happens if the internet connection is interrupted? Who controls the video, how long is it retained and how are updates delivered? These are infrastructure and ownership questions, not simply product specifications.

Cybersecurity also belongs in the design conversation. The National Institute of Standards and Technology IoT baseline highlights capabilities such as device identification, secure configuration, data protection, access control and software updates. Builders do not need to become cybersecurity engineers, but they should ask who will maintain connected systems, how updates are handled and what support the homeowner receives after closing.

Industry observers note that the most durable specification is not a list of today’s gadgets. It is a proposal for the experiences the home must support and the infrastructure required to support them. A project team should decide how residents will monitor entrances, manage access, receive alerts and expand the system over time. It should also document cable paths, network design and equipment locations so a future integrator can understand the home without starting over.

According to recent reports, builders do not need to install every possible technology at closing. They do need to avoid making future choices unnecessarily difficult. Early coordination protects the design, reduces disruptive retrofit work and gives the buyer a home that can adapt as security and smart-home technology changes.
