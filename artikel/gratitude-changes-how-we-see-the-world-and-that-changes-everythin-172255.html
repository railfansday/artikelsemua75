Gratitude changes how we see the world. And that changes everything, including your team’s performance

In a recent development, in 1863, as the United States was at risk of splitting apart in a bloody civil war, President Abraham Lincoln called on Americans to give thanks. The war was in its third year, and the Battle of Gettysburg had left more than 50,000 soldiers wounded, missing, or dead. The country’s future was entirely uncertain, people in Northern and Southern states were deeply divided—and yet on October 3, Lincoln issued a proclamation designating the last Thursday of November as a national day of Thanksgiving. Sarah Josepha Hale, the writer and activist, had been urging U.S. presidents to do this for decades without success, until that moment. Considering the physical, emotional, and psychological toll the war was having on the American people, it’s remarkable that Lincoln believed the nation still had blessings worth counting, even when people had every reason to focus on what was going wrong. That distinction gets to the heart of why gratitude matters, not just at Thanksgiving, but throughout our daily lives. Gratitude Changes What We See Feeling grateful comes easily in the massive events of life—when we get a promotion, buy our first home, or receive a clean bill of health after a harrowing illness. But as Lincoln seemed to understand, gratitude may be most valuable when our lives are off track or not going in the direction we’d hoped. When we acknowledge the good around us, we begin to see our circumstances differently. Rather than becoming consumed by what’s missing or going wrong, we recognize that there is always much in our lives worth appreciating. Human beings are naturally drawn to outliers, things that interfere with our happiness: our problems, challenges and anything requiring fixing. That attention can serve a useful motivational purpose, but when it dominates our thinking, we can lose sight of what is working; all we’ve achieved and the people who enrich and enable our lives. Gratitude doesn’t require us to ignore what’s wrong. It opens us up to seeing the bigger picture. In doing so, it can shift us from negativity and disappointment toward a more positive, hopeful and optimistic view of our lives. More than a century and a half later, psychology would confirm what Lincoln seemed to know intuitively: Directing our attention toward what’s good in our lives doesn’t deny hardship, but it can meaningfully shape how we experience it. Research has found that people who practice gratitude tend to be more optimistic, more resilient, and better able to maintain a sense of well-being during difficult times. Being Grateful Doesn’t Mean Everything Is Good Oliver Sacks, the British neurologist and best-selling author, offered a striking illustration of this distinction near the end of his life. In his short collection of essays, Gratitude, he reflected on his terminal cancer diagnosis and the knowledge that his time on the planet was running out. Even facing death, he wrote, “I cannot pretend I am without fear. But my predominant feeling is one of gratitude.” That admission is stunning because Sacks wasn’t denying the reality of his circumstances or suggesting he should be thankful for dying. Instead, he was expressing gratitude for the life he’d led, including the books he’d written, his scientific work, his relationships, and all the experiences over the years that had brought him happiness. David Steindl-Rast, an author and Benedictine monk who is globally recognized as the “grandfather of gratitude,” makes a similar distinction. We cannot reasonably be grateful for everything that happens to us, but we can find opportunities for gratitude amid our hardships and clearly see all that remains valuable. Gratitude simply allows us to recognize both realities without letting either one erase the other. What Gratitude Means for Leaders As workplace leaders are generally responsible for meeting goals and completing projects, they’re naturally motivated to identify where their teams are falling short so they can turn things around in time. Those obligations demand attention, but they can also build a habit of zeroing in exclusively on performance gaps. When leaders lose sight of their teams’ wins, they risk discouraging future successes by leaving employees feeling their boss can never be satisfied. Consistent with this, research from Gallup and Workhuman found in 2024 that only 22% of employees say they receive the right amount of appreciation for their work. And this directly affects more than morale; it impacts productivity. Compelling organizational psychology studies also show that gratitude acts as a powerful catalyst as more appreciated employees demonstrate higher resilience, greater intrinsic motivation, and significantly greater results. Expressing gratitude provides a counterbalance. A leader who openly and frequently recognizes their employees’ abilities, appreciates the effort behind their accomplishments, and acknowledges the team for overcoming challenges demonstrates that they not only see but also appreciate the people and relationships that make their successes possible. Research published in the Journal of Applied Psychology in 2026 found that gratitude expressed by leaders often prompted employees to notice and express appreciation toward their coworkers, strengthening relationships within the organization. That finding suggests that gratitude can influence more than the relationship between a leader and employee; it can help shape how people treat one another, fostering a greater sense of belonging and well-being within a team. A Practice, Not a Holiday Lincoln’s Thanksgiving proclamation came in the midst of the Civil War, not after the strife was over. And while gratitude didn’t erase the division or bring the nation back together overnight, it offered Americans a reason to appreciate all that remained worth preserving. In our own lives, we face great losses, disappointments and frustrations—and there will always be things we wish were different. But by dwelling only on what’s missing, we risk overlooking the people who enrich us, the daily experiences that bring us joy, and all the progress we’ve made. Gratitude widens our aperture and gives us a richer interpretation of things. Thanksgiving Day provides an annual reminder to be grateful, but the greater opportunity is to make gratitude part of how we see the world every day of the year and, in doing so, make our lives far more expansive.
