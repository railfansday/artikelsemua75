Reuters reported on November 22, 2026 that Anthropic limited deal teams to four senior bankers at some of its 22 IPO underwriters and asked some bank officers to accept six-month restrictions on work with AI rivals, as part of a tightly controlled listing process.

Sources indicate that several people close to the offering described a compressed timeline, late changes, delays in providing financial projections and rushed document approvals. Known internally as Project Lantern, the IPO generated more than $500 million in bank fees, underscoring the financial incentive for banks to participate despite the friction. One person familiar with the matter interpreted the team cap as an attempt to prevent leaks and said banks found ways to work around it.

The requested legal documents covered core transaction-team members and named OpenAI, Cursor, DeepSeek and Hugging Face. Bankers and lawyers said banks routinely manage conflicts and protect client confidentiality, but such broad exclusivity is rare in an IPO, particularly when it binds individual bankers. It has been revealed that anthropic shared materials with most underwriting banks through NetRoadshow rather than ordinary PDFs. The platform recorded who opened documents and which pages they viewed, applied individual bankers’ names as watermarks and prevented consumers from saving copies. One person said the system complicated bankers’ work, while leaks continued to appear in the press.

The report ties that caution partly to Reuters’ earlier disclosure of details about Anthropic’s finances, risk factors and dealings with other technology companies, nearly four weeks before the IPO registration statement became public.

Document controls coincided with a late approval cycle. Before the planned November 10, 2026 marketing launch, a law firm sent banks a PDF about Canadian share sales, then told them to disregard it because it contained an incorrect price range and share information, according to an email reviewed by Reuters. The corrected version of the updated registration statement arrived through NetRoadshow at about 2 a.m. Newly introduced York time, leaving banks several hours to approve it before filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

People close to that process remarked Elon Musk’s team directed how banks marketed the stock and what types of investors to bring, told some banks the order sizes they needed to fill, sometimes amounting to a few billion dollars, and had them agree to underwrite without knowing what they would be paid.

Orders for both offerings exceeded the available shares many times over, making the securities easy to market despite the procedural friction.

In a recent development, jay Ritter, a University of Florida professor emeritus who researches IPOs, said both companies negotiated to pay underwriters nothing on additional shares. On the broader balance of power, he said: “These companies certainly did have some market power to tell underwriters that it’s not going to be business as usual.”.

According to recent reports, anthropic, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, the two banks that led both IPOs, declined to comment. SpaceX and OpenAI did not respond to Reuters’ requests for comment.

The practical consequence for banks was a trade-off between large fees and reduced control over staffing, documents and parts of their compensation. Ritter’s assessment limits the precedent: companies without comparable investor demand are unlikely to obtain the same terms, so the episode does not establish a general model for AI offerings.
