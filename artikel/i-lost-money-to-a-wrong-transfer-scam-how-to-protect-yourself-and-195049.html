According to recent reports, imagine you work at a shipping and customs-clearance company. An email arrives from your boss at head office asking for an urgent transfer before closing time. Or a message from a courier says your parcel is on hold until you pay a fee with your bank card, or that you've won a prize. Or a notice from a software or media service you use says your account will be closed in three hours.

If you've received a message like that, you've met phishing. It is one of the most widespread attack methods, because victims are often relaxed and act on impulse, because it costs the attacker almost nothing, and because it works often enough. I fell for one myself, and I'll tell you the story below. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

In this guide I'll cover what phishing is, why it works even on smart people, how to protect yourself and your family first, then your company and your team, and what to do if the worst happens. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Phishing is pretending to be a well-known, senior, or trusted party (a bank, a coworker, a vendor, a popular platform) to trick the victim into giving up a secret or taking a harmful action: typing a password into a fake page, opening a malicious attachment, or sending money to the attacker's account.

Sources indicate that the attacker isn't breaking into your machine. They're breaking into you, through deception. That's why phishing stays effective no matter how good firewalls and antivirus get.

Attackers play on simple human feelings:.

When we're busy or tired we skim, and that's exactly the trap the attacker is betting on.

In a recent development, in 2022 I was in my office, busy preparing a major event. A WhatsApp message arrived looking like a bank notification of a transfer into my account. Then my phone rang. The caller was skilled, experienced in fraud, and spoke with complete confidence. He greeted me politely, then stated: "Sorry, we got the number wrong, money reached you by mistake." I looked at the notification, and it indicated exactly the amount he had just mentioned.

Industry observers note that it was a screenshot of an old transfer that the scammer reuses on his victims. I was talking to him on speaker while my mind was on the event, so I thanked him and, without hesitation, sent that amount from my own account and forwarded him the transfer receipt. He ended the call shortly after.

Sources indicate that then my bank's message arrived: the amount had been deducted from my balance. I opened my account on my phone and found that no extra money had come in all day. I went back to the scammer's "notification" and saw a different date and a completely different name and destination. I called him again. His phone was off, and it still is today.

Afterwards I reported it to the police, who told me they receive many reports like this. I told my staff and the trainers who work with me, then ran a training course about this kind of fraud. As for the money, I accepted the loss and moved on.

Stop before you click or transfer. Feeling rushed or scared is your cue to slow down, not to act.

Never refund money based on a receipt image. Open your bank app or statement yourself and confirm the money actually arrived. If it's real, ask the bank to return it officially.

Industry observers note that verify through a second channel. If the "bank" or "boss" writes, contact them using a number or app you already had, especially if the message came as a voice note or video, since both can be faked. Never use the contact details inside the message itself.

Recent findings suggest that turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) everywhere. It's the single most important control. Even if your password leaks, the attacker can't get in with it alone, unless you approve the prompt or hand over the code. Prefer an authenticator app or a hardware security key over SMS codes.

Industry observers note that use a password manager. A hidden security benefit: it won't autofill your password on a fake lookalike site, which is an early warning.

It has been revealed that keep everything updated. Many attacks exploit known bugs that were fixed long ago. Here's an analogy: if you keep using the same antibiotic for long stretches, it may stop working, because bacteria adapt and you have to change the drug. Systems you don't upgrade are the same: attackers learn their weaknesses and your defenses lose their effect.

Recent findings suggest that individual awareness isn't enough. We're human, and we'll slip someday. You need a guided system that becomes habit, so mistakes are less likely and less damaging.

According to recent reports, start with email, work tools, hosting consoles, and finance accounts. Use passkeys or security keys where you can: they're tied to the real domain, so they simply don't work on a fake site.

According to recent reports, write it down: no upcoming payee, no refund, and no change to a supplier's bank details is executed without confirming against the real account statement, a phone call-back to a number already on file, and a second approver. This single rule would have stopped what happened to me, and it defeats most BEC attempts.

In a recent development, the fewer permissions an account has, the less an attacker can do with it if it's stolen. Give admin rights only to people who truly need them.

Recent findings suggest that A long yearly session is forgotten fast. Short monthly examples work better, along with simple internal phishing simulations done within legal and policy limits, to show staff what a malicious message looks like. The goal is learning, not embarrassment.

In a recent development, give people a clear button or address to report and suggest improvements about any suspicious message, and make it known that anyone who clicks the wrong link and reports it immediately is thanked, not punished. An employee who fears punishment hides mistakes, and hiding is what turns a small incident into a disaster.

Turn on your mail provider's filters and take regular backups kept separate from the main network, so a ransomware attachment doesn't mean the end of the company.

Recent findings suggest that you can't stop phishing messages from arriving, but you can make them fail. Verify through a second channel, turn on 2FA, set clear rules for money and permissions, and build a culture where everyone reports without fear. Start with one step today: turn on 2FA for your email, then share this guide with your team.
