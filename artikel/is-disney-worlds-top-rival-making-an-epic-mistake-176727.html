Is Disney World's Top Rival Making an "Epic" Mistake?

Sources indicate that "Some people say, ‘Give the customers what they want.’ But that's not my approach," Steve Jobs once noted. "Our job is to figure out what they're going to want before they do.". (Related: How to Budget for a Wedding Without Going Broke...)

Will Comcast (CMCSA +2.79%) fail to heed Jobs' advice? The entertainment and connectivity giant is trying to give some of its biggest fans what they want. It's been 18 months since Epic Universe at its Universal Orlando resort in Florida became the first major U.S. theme park to open in the last two dozen years. (Related: Racehorse named IShowSpeed wins major in Australia...)

In a recent development, the park has only been available to those purchasing single- or multi-day admissions, but that's about to change. Late last week, Comcast declared that Epic Universe will be available to add to existing annual passes by early next year.

Comcast is giving its customers what they want. Don't be surprised if things don't go as planned for the country's second-largest theme park operator.

I have been to Epic Universe nine times since it opened in the springtime of last year, buying a single-day ticket every time. My first seven visits took place within April and September of last year. My family took a 14-month break before returning to visit last Monday and Tuesday. Two days later, Comcast would deliver the news that delighted my fellow passholders clamoring for more than just a la carte access to the ambitious theme park.

The gated attraction is gorgeous, unique, and immersive. It's also unforgiving when it's hot or rainy in Florida, something that shouldn't surprise anyone to learn happens often in this peninsular state.

Sources indicate that the biggest problem -- even when it's not scorching hot or a thunderous deluge that shuts down all but four rides -- is capacity. Some of the bar-raising attractions still have pockets of downtime. Even the more basic experiences that don't have reliability issues have low hourly throughputs. The subtraction adds up.

Epic Universe lacks the ability to serve as many guests as its nearby sister parks, Universal Studios Florida and Islands of Adventure, or Disney's (DIS +1.30%) four rival tourist magnets. What will happen when annual passes come into the mix starting Jan. 4 of next year?

There are already some rumblings of dissatisfaction, and this is before those floodgates open. Folks generally love Central Florida's hotbed of attractions. The seven other major theme parks within a 20-minute drive of Epic Universe -- Disney World, Universal, and United Parks' SeaWorld Orlando -- have respectable scores on the visitor review site Tripadvisor. They all score between 4.3 and 4.6 out of 5 stars. Epic Universe right now clocks in at a mere 2.5.

There is still a lot to like, if not love, at Epic Universe. I haven't paid for nine single-day admissions because I'm a glutton for punishment. The rich theming is mesmerizing. Outside of Disney World's Epcot, no other domestic park can match its breadth of quality food offerings. The two stage shows are top-notch. There are three exclusive rides that should be on every enthusiast's bucket list. However, if several guests are leaving unhappy now, just imagine what will happen next year, once Universal Orlando passholders -- soon to be called pass members -- pay $120 to $430 more per year than their existing passes for a year of Epic Universe access.

Recent findings suggest that there are three ways that this plays out, and two of them won't be pretty for Comcast stock investors like me. The first scenario is that annual pass members receive less than they expected. Universal will roll out the program in phases, but initially, guests will be limited to two daily reservations. Use one up, then grab another. Disney World has a system like this in place, but availability is plentiful, with tens of thousands of daily slots across its four parks. It should be far more limited and restrictive here. It has to be. Die-hard fans, who thought they were paying for a year-long buffet of thrills, could be in for a rude awakening.

The second grim scenario is that day guests walk away even more dissatisfied. More people mean longer lines under the current capacity constraints. With admissions revenue per capacity dipping into the double digits -- like other parks with a healthy annual pass member base -- the math will prove even more challenging. This may curb the sophomore slump Epic Universe is experiencing in turnstile clicks, but it may come at the cost of burning its highest per-capita revenue segment of visitors on one-day tickets.

The third, and most optimistic, scenario is that the handoff goes smoothly. The ideal fix would have been to boost capacity by fast-tracking high-capacity attractions before Epic Universe even opened. Instead, the first project to break ground was an event center that will open next year. It recently revealed its first newly introduced ride, but that roller coaster is "years" away from opening, according to the announcement.

Industry observers note that there is still hope that Comcast can strike the right balance, satisfying day guests and pass members simultaneously. It won't be easy, but after seeing Comcast shares halved over the past five years, you can't blame the media stock bellwether for taking on an epic challenge to turn around its Epic Universe challenge.
