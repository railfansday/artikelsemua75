Although these reactions may seem extreme, they're not unusual after a sudden split or an ugly breakup. Normal, that is, in the beginning.

In a recent development, divorce is one of the toughest transitions anyone will go through. Even under the best circumstances, it can destabilize your home, finances, parenting, friendships, and sense of identity.

Looking at divorce through Maslow's hierarchy of needs, many folks feel knocked down to the two lowest levels: basic survival and safety. The higher-functioning levels of love, esteem, and self-actualization take a back seat.

When you're dividing assets, potentially doubling your expenses, and perhaps splitting time with your kids, you're understandably more focused on getting through today than on what you might gain tomorrow.

And this is where your brain comes in.

Sources indicate that the amygdala is a small structure deep within the brain that helps detect threats and trigger emotional responses. It's part of a larger system involved in our fight, flight, or freeze reactions.

Industry observers note that jared's rage, Nan's fear, and Sean's shutdown illustrate different responses to overwhelming stress. Clients have told me they feel like their massive emotions mean they're "going crazy." I tell them they're probably not going crazy. They're being stretched emotionally, sometimes beyond anything they've experienced before.

Ideally, as the initial shock wears off, rage softens into anger, terror becomes manageable fear, and numbness begins to thaw. But not everyone moves out of survival mode easily—especially if the loss triggers previous unresolved losses or traumas.

Fortunately, another part of the brain can help: the prefrontal cortex.

Located just behind your forehead, it helps with reasoning, planning, impulse control, and thinking through actions and their consequences. It’s the part of the brain that asks, "Is this really a good idea?" “Should I say exactly what’s on my mind right now?”.

Industry observers note that under intense stress, these skills can become compromised. You're more likely to react impulsively, see things in black-and-white terms, and seek immediate relief, rather than consider long-term consequences.

The amygdala and prefrontal cortex work together, but when our threat-response system is on high alert, thoughtful decisions become harder.

That's why people sometimes do things during divorce they'd never dream of doing otherwise. They send vicious texts, threaten to take the kids away, spend thousands fighting over possessions worth far less, or make financial decisions that hurt them more than their spouse.

It has been revealed that these actions may feel justified in the moment. But there's a difference between reacting to pain and making decisions that serve you.

Just because your spouse sends an inflammatory text doesn't mean you have to answer immediately—or at all.

Your first impulse may be to defend yourself or fire back. Don't. Unless there's an emergency, give yourself time to settle down. Sometimes an hour is enough. Sometimes you need a day.

There can be something satisfying about telling your spouse exactly what you think of them or making sure they don't "get away with" what they've done.

Ask yourself: "Will this help me build the life I want after divorce, or am I just trying to feel better right now?".

3. Consider your children's needs without disregarding your own.

Sources indicate that some parents become so consumed with their hurt that they lose sight of what their children are experiencing. Others sacrifice their own well-being because they believe putting the kids first means putting themselves last.

According to recent reports, neither extreme is helpful. Children need stability and emotional safety. They also benefit from parents who take care of themselves.

I've seen people spend more on attorney fees fighting over furniture than the furniture was worth. I know of one wealthier couple who spent thousands of dollars fighting over cleaning products (I couldn't make that up)!

At that point, it's clearly not about the item being fought over as much as it is about feeling wronged, wanting control, or needing to win.

Before digging in your heels, ask: "Will this matter five years from now?".

In a recent development, some things absolutely are worth fighting for, including safety, your children's well-being, and financial security. But not every disagreement deserves a battle.

Recent findings suggest that 5. Be willing to compromise without abandoning yourself.

Recent findings suggest that one of the hardest things about divorce is accepting that you probably won't get everything you want. In some cases, you'll feel like your spouse is getting more than they deserve. It can perpetuate, or even exacerbate, your pain.

It has been revealed that ask yourself if you really want to keep fighting, or do you want to be done so you can move on? That's not to suggest that you should surrender or let your spouse take advantage of you. It means recognizing that a workable resolution may be better for your long-term mental health than winning every argument.

Just a note about safety: When abuse, coercion, or intimidation is involved, safety and legal protection come first.

According to recent reports, you're not going to feel calm and rational every minute. Divorce hurts. Sometimes terribly. You're allowed to be angry, frightened, or devastated. The problem isn't having these feelings. It's what you do with them.

Here's how to engage your higher brain: Before taking action, ask yourself:.

"Am I trying to solve a problem, protect myself, or punish someone?".

According to recent reports, sometimes you're protecting yourself, and that's appropriate. Other times, you may realize you want your spouse to hurt as much as you do. That's human, too. But acting on that impulse rarely gets you where you want to go.

Industry observers note that you may not control your spouse's behavior or how the divorce unfolds. But you can recognize when you're operating from fear, anger, or overwhelm.

The goal isn't to divorce without feeling deeply. It's to make sure temporary emotions don't create permanent consequences.

Industry observers note that after all, divorce isn't the last chapter of your life. You still have to live with your decisions long after it's over. The intense feelings you feel are in your body, not theirs. How long do you want to live with those emotions hijacking your pleasure and taking away your power? Practice letting go daily. Join a support group. Get into individual therapy. Read helpful books. Meditate. Your power is in where you are headed, not where you've been. Keep moving forward.
