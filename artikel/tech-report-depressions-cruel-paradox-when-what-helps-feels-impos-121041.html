There is a cruel paradox of depression: The very activities that might help you feel better are the very ones you feel least capable of doing.

When my depression was at its worst, which meant my motivation was at its lowest, that was precisely when I needed to find ways to become active. I knew what helped. But knowing and doing are two very separate things. Two things separated—in the case of depression—by a great distance.

I should go for a walk, but all I wanted was to hide under my covers. I should eat a salad, but all I wanted was to scarf down sweet potato fries. I should reach out to my friend Alana, but Netflix seemed like a better companion.

Making matters more challenging, if I did manage to set goals, they were usually unrealistic, which only served to reinforce the depressive cycle: setting a goal, not accomplishing it, feeling worse about myself, and withdrawing further. The wash, rinse, and repeat cycle ad nauseam.

In a recent development, I’ve discovered after many depressive episodes that it’s not about pushing through or pushing harder. It's about finding balance between compassionate acceptance of where I’m at, and identifying gentle, achievable action that will benefit me most. Emma Morton, a psychologist at Monash University in Melbourne, and I were interviewed about strategies that interrupt the depressive cycle.1.

Morton explained that one evidence-based strategy is called behavioural activation. It aims “to break the cycle of lethargy and negative thoughts and behaviors that maintain depression.”. (Related: Tech Report: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

She noted, "When we’re depressed, symptoms such as the loss of interest and enjoyment in your usual activities, combined with fatigue and lack of energy, can make our worlds become very small.".

Recent findings suggest that I know this to be true. My bedroom, my TV, and the thoughts in my head—very negative thoughts at that—become base camp.

It has been revealed that morton acknowledged, “When we’re overcome by exhaustion, resting makes sense. When everything feels stressful, withdrawing can feel protective … Unfortunately, this kind of behavior maintains, rather than helps, a depressed mood. The more isolated from our environment we are, the fewer opportunities we have for connection and positive experiences that can help improve our mood. Instead, we can end up with more time for negative self-talk and rumination.”.

According to recent reports, I learned that, at its core, behavioural activation is about gradually increasing two types of activities: those that bring me—or used to bring me—some kind of enjoyment, and those that give me a sense of accomplishment. Or even better, activities that do both.

Activities that bring me a sense of mastery—a sense of self-efficacy—have great potential to lift my mood. They offer an opportunity to feel good about something or simply provide a distraction from my caustic inner thoughts and help pass the time.

It has been revealed that I remember one particularly ugly depression, one loaded with all the bells and whistles: lack of motivation, self-hatred, self-sabotage, no self-esteem, no joy, unrelenting fatigue, and perpetual anxiety. You know the type: a nice combustible, masochistic mixture of all things unwanted.

Recent findings suggest that I began to practice behavioural activation and set goals. Early on, I saw the importance of letting go of the expectation that these small activities would make me feel better right away. This alone gave me motivation to carry on when I didn’t see the point.

It was the accumulation of small positive actions that began to improve my mood. Slowly, I would notice little blips of joy—ok, maybe not joy—but relief. Moments when the world felt a little brighter—if only briefly.

The activities distracted me, helped pass the time, and helped me put one foot in front of the other. At first, a goal that made me feel good about myself might simply be putting my jeans and shoes on, instead of wearing sweatpants and slippers. My goal wasn’t even a walk—it was to change into something I might wear when and if I went for a walk. That’s how manageable I needed them to be.

Morton acknowledged this approach can sound boring and overly simplistic. “But it’s not,” she explained. “When paired with other evidence-based techniques, it’s a powerful tool that can disrupt the cycle.”.

According to recent reports, I easily judged myself when I thought about these goals. They were embarrassingly small and meaningless, weren’t they?

This inner hurdle had the potential to prevent me from even trying. In order to have a chance at any success, I needed to find a way to address it.

In a recent development, self-compassion and gentleness were the antidotes. Another cruel paradox—these weren’t exactly in abundance when I was in the depths of darkness. It was true, in the past I was capable of more. But depression, like other illnesses, affects our ability to function. Maybe, at the time, those activity goals were the perfect size for me.

In a recent development, when I couldn’t find compassion for myself—read most of the time—I borrowed it. I turned to my therapist, a friend, or read excerpts from helpful books like Pema Chodron’s When Things Fall Apart. Or, if I was lucky, a cuddle from a loving pup helped. They all reminded me that I'm worthwhile, just because I am. Period.

I would make a list. Monday: Have a shower. Tuesday: Talk with Petrina. Wednesday: Walk around the block with Lea. Thursday: Eat a homemade healthy dinner. Friday: Watch two hours of Grey’s Anatomy, not four.

Slowly, very slowly, over a period of weeks or months (yes, months—yowch—I know), I started to feel better, and the depression began to fade. Climbing out of my depression, though, wasn’t entirely due to the accumulation of small achievements. I was also taking my medication, seeing my psychiatrist and counselor, keeping my sleep on track, and meditating.

We can’t force ourselves to feel better. That’s not possible—even if it were—it wouldn’t be helpful. But setting small, achievable goals that give our mood a chance to shift is. And lo and behold, those tiny actions add up and can gradually bring relief and eventually help us feel like ourselves again.
