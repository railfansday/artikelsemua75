Older muscles recover more slowly, and a lingering repair signal may explain why

Researchers found that low-dose ibuprofen improved muscle strength recovery in older mice, but benefits remain untested in humans.

It has been revealed that two weeks in bed or in a leg brace can weaken muscles. Older adults can have a harder time regaining that strength.

For the study, the hind legs of old mice were kept off the ground for two weeks before recovery began.

Ibuprofen blocks the production of prostaglandins. Old mice were given a low dose for the first two days of recovery. They ended up with a stronger grip than old mice that received no drug. A higher dose did not help.

A repair signal that lingers too long, the team concluded, may be part of why old muscle bounces back poorly.

According to recent reports, the study was led by Paul-Emile Bourrant while he was a graduate researcher at University of Utah Health. He worked with Micah Drummond, a professor of pathology.

It has been revealed that bourrant remarked the research is aimed at older adults who are recovering from inactivity. Bed rest after hip surgery is one example. If their muscle does not return, these adults can lose their independence.

Recent findings suggest that most of the experiments were carried out in male mice or in cells grown in dishes. The human part involved 11 volunteers, and none of them took ibuprofen.

Muscle loses size and strength quickly when it goes unused after an injury, surgery, or a hospital stay. In aging muscle, that loss is harder to reverse.

In a recent development, to reproduce this disuse, the team kept the hind legs of mice off the ground for 14 days. The animals were then allowed to walk normally for up to a week. The young mice were about 4 months old, and the old mice were about 25 months old.

In a recent development, strength was measured with a grip test. Each mouse held a metal grid with all four paws. A researcher then gently pulled the animal back by the base of its tail. The peak force was recorded by a meter.

In a recent development, both age groups had lost grip strength after 14 days of disuse. After a week of normal walking, the young mice had regained it.

The old mice still had not returned to their starting strength. Muscle mass in the calf was likewise not fully restored.

In a recent development, fat levels in calf muscle were measured by the team at each stage of the experiment. The signaling molecules produced from those fats were measured as well. Six mice from each age group were examined at every stage.

Arachidonic acid is an omega-6 fat stored in cell membranes. During recovery, greater amounts were released from the membranes of old muscle, and the level remained high. In young muscle, it was kept at relatively low levels throughout.

Arachidonic acid serves as the starting material from which prostaglandins are made. That conversion relies on COX enzymes, which happen to be the target of ibuprofen.

It has been revealed that during recovery, several prostaglandins were higher in old mice than in young mice. Young animals revealed little change over the same period.

The authors described that pattern as notable. Signals like these usually fade as muscle repairs itself. Such fading is seen after a session of resistance training, for example.

According to recent reports, drummond said muscle heals with the help of prostaglandins. But when the levels stay high for too long, he said, inflammation can turn chronic. Cells can also become stressed.

The team then turned to people. Five young adults and six healthy older adults took part. Each wore a knee brace on one leg for 14 days. Weight was kept off that leg with crutches or a walker.

The young adults averaged 23 years of age, and the older adults averaged 68. All of them came from the Salt Lake City area and were part of a larger registered trial.

Industry observers note that small samples of thigh muscle were taken from each person at four points. The first was taken before the brace went on, and the second when it came off. Two further samples were collected on days two and seven of recovery.

The human data were less striking than the mouse data. Total levels of the fat-derived signals were not clearly altered during recovery.

After seven days of recovery, two prostaglandins were higher in the older adults than in the young adults. By then, earlier rises in several signals had partly subsided in the younger group.

A slower and smaller response was seen in humans than in mice, the authors wrote. Differences between the two experiments were also noted, including how the muscle was rested and which muscle was sampled.

Sources indicate that aging tissue gradually collects cells that no longer divide but have not died. Such cells are known as senescent cells and are often nicknamed zombie cells. They release inflammatory signals into nearby tissue.

Industry observers note that in old mice, senescent cells in the calf muscle rose by day four of recovery. Their numbers rose further by day seven. A comparable increase was not observed in young muscle.

In the old animals, a greater number of senescent cells was associated with a lower calf muscle mass. A positive association with prostaglandin levels was also observed at day four of recovery.

Senescence was then induced in three types of cultured mouse cells by means of a chemotherapy drug.

No increase in a major prostaglandin was detected in the muscle cells or the connective tissue cells. An increase was detected only in the immune cells known as macrophages.

Cultured muscle fibers were exposed to fluid that had been collected from the senescent macrophages. The fibers were reduced in size as a result. This reduction was partly prevented if a COX enzyme had been blocked in the macrophages.

The question of whether lower prostaglandin levels would improve recovery was then addressed in old mice. After the same 14 days of disuse the animals were injected twice daily during the first two days of walking.

Industry observers note that the mice were divided into three groups of 7 to 12 animals each. Ibuprofen was given at 30 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day to the first group. The second group received 60 milligrams per kilogram and the third group was injected without any drug.

It has been revealed that grip strength adjusted for body weight was assessed on day seven of recovery. A higher grip strength was recorded in the low-dose group than in the untreated group.

No difference in calf or lower-leg muscle mass was found between these groups. However, more force for its size was produced by the soleus, a small lower-leg muscle, in laboratory tests.

Less collagen, the fibrous material of scar tissue, was seen in stained sections of the calf. Two of the three markers of senescent cells were also reduced.

Industry observers note that these benefits were not observed in the high-dose group. In the paper, the authors suggested that complete COX suppression might disrupt the remodeling of muscle.

Dose and timing may also account for the mixed results of earlier research on these drugs and muscle, they added.

In one trial, older adults who took COX-blocking drugs during resistance training gained more muscle and strength.

“We don’t have the full picture yet,” Bourrant stated.

Industry observers note that ibuprofen was not given to any of the human volunteers. Its effects were tested only in old male mice, at two doses over two days.

The human comparison was small, with 11 people, and female mice were left out. Because muscle ages differently in males and females, testing in females is still needed, the authors wrote.

Recent findings suggest that it also remains unclear which cells produce the extra prostaglandins inside living muscle. In dishes, senescent macrophages made more of one prostaglandin. The authors called for animal experiments to pin down the cells responsible.

For any treatment that targets prostaglandins in aging muscle, the authors wrote, the dose and the timing will matter.

Future studies could test whether a short, low dose during recovery helps older people the way it helped old mice.

The full study was published in the journal Aging Cell.
