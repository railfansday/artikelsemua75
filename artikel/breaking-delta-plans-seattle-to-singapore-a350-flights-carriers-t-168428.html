Breaking: Delta Plans Seattle To Singapore A350 Flights, Carrier’s Third Longest Route

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

We know that Delta has huge growth aspirations across the Pacific, particularly to Asia. It’s an area where Delta has increasingly fallen behind United in recent years, but Delta now hopes to not only catch up, but even beat United. We’ll see what comes of that, and in particular, find out just how costly that growth is.

Sources indicate that A few months ago, Delta executives publicly confirmed intends to return to Singapore, a destination that hasn’t been served by the airline since 2019, when that was an intra-Asia tag flight from Japan. Executives also confirmed out of which airport the service would operate. There’s now an additional enhancement, as an announcement about the fresh route is imminent, which almost certainly points to a fall 2027 launch for the service. (Related: Course Launch Community Event...)

As Delta executives view it, the airline is pretty maxed out in terms of its European potential, and in the long run, they see the most long haul growth potential in Asia, the Middle East, and Africa. So, what kind of expansion should we expect?

Back in August 2026, Delta Chief Commercial Officer Joe Esposito explicitly confirmed that the airline would return to Singapore. What was perhaps most interesting is that he also indirectly confirmed the hub out of which the service would operate — Esposito ruled out that the service could operate out of Los Angeles, so it’s pretty clear that the airline will operate this service out of Seattle (SEA).

That’s also what JonNYC reported in the past, and now we can take that a step further — Delta is holding an event in Seattle in early December 2026, and that’s when the airline is expected to announce the service.

My guess is that we’re looking at a fall 2027 service start, at best. At 8,070 miles, this will be Delta’s third longest flight, after its routes to South Africa, which are from Atlanta (ATL) to Cape Town (CPT) and Johannesburg (JNB).

One certainly wonders if this Singapore route could be operated by Delta’s newly introduced Airbus A350-1000s, which will feature an all-new passenger experience. While the plane has great range and per seat economics, the only thing I’d say is that it’s “a lot of plane” for a new market, in terms of the overall capacity (even with the very premium layout). (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

Industry observers note that in both the Seattle and Los Angeles to Singapore market, Singapore Airlines is the only other carrier operating. So competitively there’s not a huge difference in that regard, though Los Angeles is a significantly larger market in terms of demand for Singapore, and for that matter, Singapore Airlines also has more service out of the airport. But perhaps Delta is also looking to beat Alaska to the punch in the Seattle market.

It’s fantastic to see Delta looking to grow its presence in Asia. Point-to-point flying to Asia is a market that United has basically had cornered in recent years, and competition is good for consumers.

It has been revealed that it’s amazing to think for how many years Delta basically squandered its Asia advantage. Keep in mind that back in the day, Northwest had a robust Asia network, and after the merger with Delta, we saw that network decreased slowly but steadily, as Delta instead focused on routing everything through Seoul Incheon, and into the Korean Air network. So I’m happy to see a reversal of that strategy.

I also have to say that given Delta’s hubris, it’s kind of fun to see the airline having to increasingly play “catch up,” rather than coming in as the dominant carrier. And I think that very much sums up what’s going on in Asia.

Sources indicate that the most basic structural disadvantage that Delta has is that United has a fortress hub in San Francisco (SFO), which is where a large part of its Asia network flies from. That’s fantastic for connectivity, not to mention San Francisco is a very strong market for Asia traffic.

Recent findings suggest that delta, meanwhile, is taking a split hub approach, flying out of both Los Angeles and Seattle. Obviously Delta is trying to make the best of its situation, but there’s no denying that this is an uphill battle:.

It also has to be emphasized that in terms of actually transporting passengers profitably (or at less of a loss), United does a better job at that than Delta, despite its lower overall profitability. Comparing passenger revenue per air seat mile to cost per air seat mile, United is at 16.18 cents vs. 16.46 cents, while Delta is at 17.37 cents vs. 19.31 cents.

Delta is growing quite a bit in Asia at the moment. The airline has already launched flights to Hong Kong, with flights now on sale to Manila and Tokyo Narita. On top of that, Delta is expected to announce a fresh Seattle to Singapore route within the next two weeks, with a launch likely planned for the fall of 2027.

It’s great for passengers to see more competition across the Pacific, as Delta sure is making up for a lack of effort for years. However, the airline will also have quite the disadvantage compared to United for a long time….
