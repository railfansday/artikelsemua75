AI has rapidly moved to the center of the enterprise agenda, but results haven’t followed the investments. Our analysis of more than 6,600 client inputs across 900+ Fortune 500 and private equity-backed companies found that AI being cited as a top priority increased eightfold over 12 months. More than 52% of client discussion topics now involve AI, yet many organizations remain stuck between identifying AI opportunities and implementing solutions that improve business outcomes.

It has been revealed that in the past, these companies would seek counsel from a big consulting firm to help them implement technology and get “unstuck.” That tactic is no longer working. Businesses across industries have spent two years and millions of dollars on AI strategy decks from major consulting firms with little to show for it. I’ve talked to many who were handed an AI strategy or pilot but can’t turn it into meaningful business impact. In fact, nearly 44% of companies in our dataset sought additional support after working with at least one major consulting firm on AI initiatives. And MIT research found that just 5% of integrated generative AI pilots were delivering substantial measurable value. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

In a recent development, why? Companies are approaching AI as a technology problem, when the technology isn’t the hard part anymore. The hard part is changing the business to make it work. Successful enterprise AI adoption requires true organizational change, and true organizational change comes when experienced operators with domain expertise are a core part of crafting AI solutions. Recent findings suggest that global spending on AI is forecast to total $2.7 trillion in 2026, a 49.5% increase from the previous year. The ambition is there, the money is there, and the access to technology is there, but there are several barriers preventing companies from turning those investments into measurable value and ROI.

In my experience, this disconnect typically takes two forms.

The first is strategy without execution. Our data found that the most common barrier to realizing AI’s value, reported by 52% of companies, isn’t technical; it’s the lack of an AI roadmap aligned with business strategy and grounded in the organization’s ability to execute.

Companies engage major consulting firms to assess AI opportunities, develop a comprehensive framework, and recommend where to invest. These strategies can provide valuable direction, but too often, organizations are left with detailed presentations and ambitious plans and without a clear understanding of how to put them into practice.

Industry observers note that the second barrier to success is the ability to measure outcomes. With today’s AI capabilities, technical teams can develop sophisticated pilots in a matter of weeks. But companies need to answer two critical questions before investing: Are we applying AI to the right business problems, and how will we know if it’s working?

First, organizations need to identify applications that can meaningfully improve business performance, rather than simply demonstrate what the technology can do or drive efficiency without reallocating freed-up resources purposefully. Research from RAND found that AI projects often fail because business leaders and technical teams aren’t aligned on the problems they’re trying to solve.

In a recent development, second, companies need to establish how they’ll accurately measure the business impact of AI from the outset. That means defining clear and measurable KPIs, establishing a baseline, and determining the extent to which improvements can actually be attributed to the technology.

Sources indicate that so what’s missing? What’s the solution to removing barriers and finally seeing results? Operational expertise, deployed strategically.

Recent findings suggest that the forward-deployed engineering (FDE) push has been making headlines over the past six months. Technology companies and consulting firms are increasingly investing in FDE teams that work directly with businesses to develop and implement AI solutions, with Google/Accenture being the latest announcement.

Though this is an vital step forward and will be valuable to businesses without in-house technical experts to build AI solutions, FDE initiatives still miss a critical element: domain expertise and operational experience. Engineering know-how alone isn’t enough to ensure solutions deliver meaningful business results. An engineer may know how to develop a sophisticated AI application, but that doesn’t necessarily mean they understand the intricacies of managing a supply chain, overseeing finance operations, or improving a tech giant’s pricing strategy.

That’s where experienced business operators come in. Professionals who have spent years leading these functions they’re helping to transform; people who understand the workflows, challenges, and performance metrics that actually create value. Working alongside AI engineers as an integrated team, these operators can identify where AI will have the greatest impact, anticipate operational constraints, and fundamentally rethink how work gets done rather than simply adding technology to existing processes. This way, the AI deployments fully align with key business metrics and organizational strategy, shortening the time to meaningful impact and ROI.

Sources indicate that for example, a finance organization may be looking to use AI to improve reporting and forecasting. Rather than simply automating reports, an experienced finance operator working alongside AI engineers could redesign how financial data is collected and analyzed, enabling faster forecasting, better decision-making, and more time for strategic work.

Bringing together the people who understand how the business operates with those who understand what the technology can do is key, but the expertise needs to be applied in the right order.

Rather than starting with the technology and looking for places to apply it, businesses should take a more deliberate approach: starting with the business problem, identifying where AI can make a measurable difference, and building a clear path from implementation to results.

The organizations that know where to apply that technology will see the greatest returns. Pairing experienced operators with AI engineers could be the difference between another wave of expensive experiments and AI finally delivering on its promise to transform business.
