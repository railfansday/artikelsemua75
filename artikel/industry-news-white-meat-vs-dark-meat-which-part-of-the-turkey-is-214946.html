In a recent development, every Thanksgiving, the table splits into two camps. There's Team White Meat, politely asking for "just a slice of breast, please." Then there's Team Dark Meat, already eyeing the drumstick like it's the last Pop-Tart in the box. It's basically Team Edward vs. Team Jacob, but with gravy.

Somewhere along the way, dark meat picked up a reputation as the "less healthy" choice. As a dietitian, I get asked about this a lot, especially the week of Thanksgiving. The short answer is that dark meat isn't the villain it's made out to be. The long answer is way more interesting, so grab a fork. Cranberry sauce optional.

Sources indicate that the color comes down to a protein called myoglobin. Myoglobin stores oxygen inside muscle cells, and muscles that work steadily over long periods need more of it. On a turkey, that means the legs and thighs, which the bird uses to stand and walk around all day.

Industry observers note that domestic turkeys use their breast muscles far less, so they carry less myoglobin and stay pale. Myoglobin contains heme iron, which matters for nutrition. More on that in a minute. (Related: Industry News: Train a Sentence Embedding Model with 1B...)

So when you choose dark meat, you're eating hardworking muscle. That changes its nutritional profile in a few notable ways.

Recent findings suggest that both cuts are excellent sources of protein. A 3-ounce serving of roasted, skinless turkey provides roughly 24 to 25 grams of protein, whether it's white or dark meat. White meat edges ahead by a gram or two, but in practical terms, both will do the job.

It has been revealed that if protein is a priority, either cut gets you there. No need to stage a dramatic family intervention over the drumsticks.

In a recent development, skinless dark meat does have more fat than skinless white meat, typically a few extra grams per 3-ounce serving. Some of that is saturated fat.

Sources indicate that context matters, though. The difference amounts to a few grams on a single plate, not a massive gap. Keeping the skin on also adds more fat to either cut. A slice of breast with crispy skin can end up in a similar ballpark to skinless thigh meat.

Saturated fat and heart health remain an active area of research. A Cochrane systematic review found that reducing dietary saturated fat was linked to a modest reduction in cardiovascular events over time. That finding reflects overall eating patterns across months and years, though, not what you eat at one holiday meal. A few extra grams of fat from turkey thighs won't make or break anyone's health.

Fat also brings flavor and moisture, which is why dark meat rarely turns into the dry, sawdust situation that can happen to an overcooked breast.

In a recent development, remember myoglobin? Thanks in part to its heme iron, dark meat generally contains more iron than white meat. Heme iron, the type found in animal foods, is generally absorbed more efficiently than the non-heme iron found in plants.

That's meaningful, because iron deficiency is the most common cause of anemia worldwide. It's especially common among people who menstruate, pregnant people, and young children. Turkey thighs won't fix a deficiency on their own, but they can absolutely contribute to your overall iron intake. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Dark meat also delivers noticeably more zinc than white meat. Zinc plays a key role in immune function, wound healing, and taste perception. Not bad for the part of the bird some people skip.

Neither cut offers a complete B vitamin package on its own. Together, though? They make a pretty great team.

Sources indicate that not really. It's just different. White meat offers slightly more protein and less fat, plus more niacin and B6. Dark meat has more iron, zinc, and riboflavin, plus richer flavor and a juicier texture. Both provide vitamin B12.

Industry observers note that neither one deserves a "good" or "bad" label. Health is shaped by your eating patterns over time, not by which slice you pick from the platter on one Thursday in November.

If you have a specific health condition, a registered dietitian can help you figure out what works best for you. For most people, though, the best turkey is the one you actually enjoy eating.

Recent findings suggest that pick the cut you love. Better yet, take a little of both. Dark meat isn't a nutritional compromise, and white meat isn't a punishment. They're two parts of the same bird, each with its own strengths.

In a recent development, so go ahead and claim that drumstick. Or the breast. Or both. Your plate, your call. Just save some gravy for the rest of us.
