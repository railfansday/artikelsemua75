Sadio Mane was named best player at the 2025 Africa Cup of Nations.

Everyone knows a football match should take 90 minutes, but the final of the 2025 Africa Cup of Nations has been playing out for 10 months.

In a recent development, now, finally, Morocco or Senegal will be crowned winners. However, that moment of glory will come in a courtroom, not on the pitch.

On Tuesday afternoon sport's highest legal authority, the Court of Arbitration for Sport (Cas), will render its judgement on Senegal's appeal against the decision to strip them of the title they thought they won in January against the hosts in Rabat.

The case was heard in early October, and the wait is almost over.

With 66,000 fans creating an electric atmosphere in the Prince Moulay Abdellah Stadium, Morocco were chasing their first Afcon title since 1976.

But the final descended into chaos deep into second-half stoppage time.

With the match goalless, Senegal thought Ismaila Sarr had scored a dramatic winner, only to discover that referee Jean Jacques Ndala had blown for a Morocco free-kick.

The controversy deepened in the 98th minute when, after a video assistant referee (VAR) review, Ndala awarded Morocco a penalty.

Recent findings suggest that tempers boiled over on the touchline and in the stands. Senegal coach Pape Thiaw led some of his players and staff down the tunnel, while supporters clashed with security as order briefly collapsed.

Play eventually resumed and, in the 124th minute, Morocco missed a golden chance to end their 50-year wait for continental glory when Brahim Diaz failed to convert a penalty, chipping it 'Panenka' style into the arms of goalkeeper Edouard Mendy.

Pape Gueye's extra-time strike then secured a 1-0 win for Senegal, who lifted the trophy for a second time.

The Royal Moroccan Football Federation (FMRF) quickly lodged a complaint with the Confederation of African Football (Caf), arguing that Senegal had breached competition regulations.

In a recent development, article 82, contained within a chapter titled 'Withdrawals', states: "If, for any reason whatsoever, a team... refuses to play or leaves the ground before the regular end of the match without the authorisation of the referee", they shall be "considered [the loser] and shall be eliminated from the current competition".

In March a Caf appeals board agreed, ruling that Senegal had forfeited the match and awarding Morocco a 3-0 victory.

While there is precedent for Caf overturning results during Afcon and World Cup qualifying, some people, including one former senior Caf official, argued that only the referee has the authority to abandon a match if a walk-off occurs. The FMRF noted it was seeking the proper application of tournament regulations, but Senegal's federation (FSF) described the ruling as "unfair, unprecedented and unacceptable" and appealed to Cas.

Some Senegal supporters were initially convicted of hooliganism offences after clashes during the final but eventually returned home after receiving a royal pardon.

Founded in 1984 and based in the Swiss city of Lausanne, Cas was set up as an independent legal body designed to settle disputes as global sport became more lucrative and, as a result, more litigious. (Related: Latest Update: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

For governing bodies which recognise Cas' authority, it is deemed to be the supreme arbiter, meaning its ruling is final unless, in rare cases, Switzerland's highest court decides to set it aside based on a limited number of technicalities.

More than 900 cases are filed every year, from more than 50 different sports, covering issues such as employment contracts and transfers, doping, corruption, TV rights and, in this case, the organisation of sporting events.

The FSF's appeal was heard in private on 8 October by a panel of three arbitrators chosen by Cas for their expertise in sports law.

The panel was led by Frenchman Gerald Simon, founder of the Sports Law Laboratory at the University of Burgundy and a Cas member for 23 years.

Although Caf's appeals board is independent, a reversal of its judgement would deliver another blow to the reputation of African football's governing body, which has been heavily criticised for the retrospective decision to hand Morocco the title.

Caf president Patrice Motsepe stated in March that it will "adhere to" and "respect" whatever Cas decides, so, if things go in Senegal's favour, it could mean someone having to tinker with the Afcon rulebook.

Thiaw lost his job following Senegal's last-32 World Cup exit this summer, while Walid Regragui, the man in the Morocco dugout in January, was dismissed in March.

Whatever the outcome of the case, animosity is likely to linger between the two federations.

The FMRF's senior hierarchy was furious with Senegal's behaviour, believing the pictures beamed around the world irrevocably tarnished the image of the first Afcon hosted by the kingdom since 1988 - a tournament into which a huge amount of time and money had been invested.

The FSF and the Senegalese government have leaned into a feeling prevalent across the continent that Morocco holds too much influence within African football.

Other incidents also irked Senegal, such as laser pens being directed into players' eyes and ball boys moving goalkeeper Mendy's towel, something which led to the barely believable sight of reserve keeper Yehvann Diouf being tackled to the ground by three ball boys when he tried to intervene.

With the next Afcon scheduled to take place in June and July next year, the chances of a high-profile rematch between Africa's best two teams is a tantalising thought.

While the chaos of this year's final may have given birth to a newly introduced rivalry for fans to revel in, there is unanimous agreement that such encounters should be settled on the pitch and not in a courtroom.
