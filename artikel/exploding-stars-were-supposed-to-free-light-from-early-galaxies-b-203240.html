Exploding stars were supposed to free light from early galaxies, but they may have done the opposite

Hubble spectra of six young galaxies suggest winds from exploding stars trapped the light that drove the reionization of the Universe, rather than freeing it.

For most of its first billion years, the Universe was wrapped in a fog of hydrogen gas that blocked ultraviolet light. Something cleared that fog. (Related: Industry News: huggingface_hub v1.0: Five Years of Buil...)

Astronomers have long assumed that exploding stars helped, by blasting open channels for light to get out of young galaxies.

New Hubble observations of six nearby galaxies point the other way: the galaxies leaking the most light were the ones whose stars hadn’t started exploding yet.

Cody Carr led the study as a postdoctoral scholar at Zhejiang University in China and is now a postdoctoral researcher in astronomy at the University of Michigan.

Renyue Cen of Zhejiang and Stephan McCandliss of Johns Hopkins University were the senior authors, and nearly 30 researchers from more than a dozen institutions took part.

“Basically, how these galaxies were leaking ionizing radiation isn’t what we thought,” Carr noted.

In the early Universe, hydrogen atoms were whole, each a proton with an electron attached. Gas like that soaks up ultraviolet light.

High-energy light from the first galaxies eventually knocked the electrons off, and gas in that state is transparent. Astronomers call the change reionization, and Carr described it as the last major phase transition of the Universe.

The puzzle has been how the light got out, because a galaxy forming stars at a furious rate is also full of gas and dust that absorb that light.

In a recent development, one long-standing answer involved supernovae, the explosions that end the lives of the biggest stars. Their winds, the idea went, pushed the gas aside and opened the way. (Related: Tech Report: AI for Game Development: Creating a Farmin...)

The alternative is that the light escaped earlier, while the biggest stars were still alive. Their own radiation and the fast winds blowing off their surfaces would have done the work, before any star had exploded.

Galaxies from the early Universe are too faint and too far away to study in this detail, so the team picked six nearby galaxies that resemble them.

All six are compact and forming stars at a high rate, and all six have similar mass and chemical makeup. What differs is how much of their high-energy light escapes – from less than 0.4 percent to about 21 percent.

The team used the Hubble Space Telescope to take deep spectra of each one, spreading the ultraviolet light into its component wavelengths.

Gas moving toward us absorbs light at slightly shifted wavelengths, so the dips in a spectrum show how fast a galaxy’s wind is blowing and how much gas it carries.

Industry observers note that the spectra also show how old each galaxy’s current burst of star formation is. The biggest stars live only a few million years before they explode, so a burst younger than about three million years has no supernovae yet.

The three galaxies leaking the most light were the youngest, with bursts under about four million years old.

Industry observers note that in the youngest of all, the absorption came mostly from the fast winds blowing off massive stars, at around 6.7 million miles per hour (3,000 kilometers per second). The galaxy-wide wind was slow.

According to recent reports, the three weakest leakers were older, and their spectra looked different. The stellar-wind signature had faded, and a galaxy-wide wind had taken over, carrying more neutral hydrogen and more dust.

In the youngest galaxy that wind moved at roughly 335,000 miles per hour (150 kilometers per second). The oldest galaxies drove winds about five times faster.

The faster the wind, the less light got out. The authors read that as the launch of a wind, driven by exploding stars, that sweeps gas and dust up into the outflow, where it blocks the light that had been escaping.

“It appears that energy input from supernovae has stirred up the surroundings and increased, rather than decreased, the coverage of neutral gas and dust that suppresses ionizing radiation escape,” McCandliss mentioned.

The team checked whether the pattern holds beyond six galaxies. They stacked the spectra of 89 galaxies from a larger Hubble survey into three groups by how much light escapes.

The strongest leakers revealed narrower hydrogen absorption than the rest, in line with slower winds, and the non-leakers hosted the fastest winds.

The timing changes the arithmetic of reionization. A galaxy’s contribution depends on how much high-energy light it makes and what fraction gets out, and those two things can peak at different times.

If the light escapes while the biggest stars are still alive and shining, galaxies like these could have done more of the work than models that wait for supernovae allow.

Simulations that explain reionization lean heavily on supernovae. Cen noted the results point to a need to examine how radiation and winds from young stars shape the surrounding gas before the first supernovae explode.

Six galaxies is a small sample. The ages come from fitting models to the starlight, so the timeline is an average rather than a clock. Hubble’s spectra also can’t see the hottest wind gas, which only shows up in X-rays.

The team looked for X-rays from the strongest leaker with a separate telescope and found none. That fits a galaxy whose supernova-driven wind hasn’t started.

The six galaxies are also heavier and richer in heavy elements than the galaxies of the first billion years.

In lighter, more primitive galaxies, the authors expect radiation to matter even more. Massive stars there are likelier to collapse into black holes without exploding, which would delay the supernova winds further.

Whether compact galaxies like these were enough to clear the fog on their own is still an open question. Carr and his colleagues are seeking more Hubble time to observe more galaxies.

The 36-year-old telescope is near the end of its working life, the authors wrote. The proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory is one instrument that could take over this kind of measurement.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Astronomy.
