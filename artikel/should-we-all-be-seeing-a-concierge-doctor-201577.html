Should We All Be Seeing a Concierge Doctor?

It has been revealed that for as long as I can remember, going to my primary care doctor has been simple: I’d schedule an appointment a few weeks (or months) in advance, commute across town for an in-person appointment, and hope that my insurance covered whatever labs were ordered. But lately, many people have been opting for a membership-based system where they pay a monthly or annual fee for on-demand care. This concept is called concierge medicine. Over the past few years, the number of concierge medicine services like Tia and One Medical has skyrocketed. Between 2018 and 2023, the number of direct primary care and concierge practice sites grew by roughly 83 percent, per a 2025 study in Health Affairs. (But there are also concierge practices specific to gynecology, longevity medicine, and even emergency medicine.). (Related: Industry News: huggingface_hub v1.0: Five Years of Buil...)

“Over the course of my career, I've seen a lot of frustrations that patients have had in terms of access to care, long wait times, lack of continuity of care,” indicates Jane Zhu, MD, a primary care provider and associate professor of medicine at Oregon Health and Science University, and author of the Health Affairs study. There’s a real need for faster, more on-demand medical care—and concierge medicine is one way that patients are getting that.

Membership prices vary, with some costing as little as $9 a month for on-demand urgent and primary care, and others costing as much as $45,000 a year for top-tier care (at-home doctor visits for urgent care, X-ray imaging from your bed, and even private jets on standby for medical evacuation while you’re traveling). If you don’t live in a major city, many of these programs offer telehealth services all over the country.

In a recent development, so, what’s the catch? Ahead, read up on everything you need to know about concierge medicine, including the pros and cons, and who it’s right for.

The premise is simple: paying a membership fee unlocks extra attention and access to care from your doctor.

Sources indicate that at most traditional clinics and hospitals, the goal is for doctors to see as many patients a day as possible. (More visits equals more insurance claims and/or copays, which in turn makes money for the practice.) With concierge medicine, doctors don’t operate within this same construct. Instead, they make money from membership fees, which means they can still turn a profit with a much smaller pool of patients. When you schedule an appointment with a concierge doctor, you’re not competing with hundreds of other patients for a time slot in the same way you would for a traditional doctor.

Industry observers note that when Kristen Harvey, MD, MPH, a concierge family medicine physician at Mount Sinai, worked in traditional medicine, she only got about 15 to 20 minutes for each patient visit. Now, she has the freedom to extend her visits to 45 to 60 minutes and form closer relationships with her patients. “I'm able to go into deeper discussions from a more holistic perspective and have a different type of conversation,” she says. Talking more in depth about sleep, nutrition, and lifestyle habits in general is key to helping her patients prevent illnesses in the first place—not just treat them when they come up.

In a recent development, additionally, getting lab work done through concierge medicine may be a smoother process. While some traditional practices may have you drop by LabCorp to get your blood drawn, for example, many concierge practices will just do it on site, and even go beyond your average complete blood count for in-depth lipid, hormone, and genetic panels. But everything isn't always included in the membership, so check before they draw.

When you need a specialist outside of your concierge practice, they’ll often have health navigators—people whose job it is to schedule your appointments for you, facilitate communication between your providers, coordinate prescriptions, and run point on any insurance claims. Because the health care system can feel so fragmented and hard to understand, the health navigation piece is a major highlight for many patients, states Scott Braunstein, MD, an internal and emergency medicine physician and the chief medical officer at Sollis Health, a concierge clinic for urgent and emergency care.

Regarding costs outside of the membership fee and bloodwork, every concierge practice is different. At Tia, for example, preventative care services like yearly physicals or vaccinations are usually covered by insurance, but diagnostic appointments often come with additional out-of-pocket fees. That means that if you come in for a UTI, sick visit, or complaint about a new health problem, it’ll often result in an additional charge on the patient’s end. The average copay for these kinds of visits at Tia is between $15 and $75—not too different from a traditional doctor’s appointment. However, at a high-end program where you pay entirely out of pocket, the care is pretty all-inclusive.

As of now, there isn’t enough data on concierge medicine to determine whether this model provides superior health outcomes for patients—like fewer hospitalizations or better chronic disease management—compared to traditional systems. That being said, there is one undeniable factor that contributes to good health: regularly going to the doctor. A 2026 report by the health policy initiative Milbank Memorial Fund found that people with chronic illnesses who regularly see a primary care provider have lower rates of hospitalization and emergency room visits—and lower health care costs on the whole.

In a recent development, however, in the U.S., about one-third of adults don’t have a usual source of primary care, per the report. Concierge medicine is one way to bridge this gap of primary care access, and give people an easy, on-demand way to take care of their health. Appointments are easier to schedule, can often be taken from your phone or computer, and the experience is designed to just be more enjoyable and convenient. “When we remove barriers for people accessing care, people are more likely to engage in their health. And when people are more likely to engage in their health, they have better health outcomes,” says Natasha Bhuyan, MD, is a primary care provider and the vice president of In-Office Care at One Medical.

According to recent reports, if you can get into these models of care, they seem to be beneficial for both the clinician and the patient. But the question remains: “What happens if you can't get into those models of care?” claims Dr. Zhu.

According to recent reports, the number of clinicians practicing in membership-based clinics grew by 78 percent between 2018 and 2023, per the Health Affairs study. That means that more clinicians are leaving traditional medicine for this model—which could put pressure on an already-stretched-thin primary care system. If more clinicians leave the traditional system, it could become harder for the people who can’t pay for concierge medicine to access the traditional care they need. So while concierge medicine may help some people live healthier lives, it doesn’t yet fix the broader need for more primary care access in the United States, indicates Dr. Zhu.

The study also found that while concierge medicine providers are growing, the share of physicians actually declined, and the number of advanced practice providers—like nurses, nurse practitioners, and physician assistants—increased. These advanced practice providers were more likely to be upcoming entrants to the workforce with no prior employer. So while there are still concierge providers with a career’s worth of experience, you might be more likely to get a newer provider with less experience if you opt for concierge medicine.

Sources indicate that if you’re frustrated with a fragmented health system where you have to be the one to connect the dots between all of your doctors, concierge medicine could be a welcome reprieve. For instance, Dr. Harvey expects to see more and more patients with chronic illnesses opting for concierge models in the next couple of years. If you have a condition like PMOS, for example, you might be seeing five different specialists to deal with the symptoms: a dermatologist, endocrinologist, gynecologist, nutritionist, and a primary care provider.

With a concierge practice that specializes in convenient, wraparound care, you may be able to see multiple of these specialists all in one place, and have a health navigator or coach to help you coordinate appointments, indicates Dr. Bhuyan. When you’re dealing with complex medical conditions, or just finding yourself going to the doctor a lot for illnesses or infections, paying for extra time and access to your doctor might just make for a better experience.

It has been revealed that the term “concierge” can make this form of care seem cost-prohibitive—and some services certainly are. That said, there are a few options with low membership fees that take insurance, including One Medical (starting at $9/month), Sesame Plus ($10.99/month), Tia Signature ($25-a-month membership, only available in some states) and Folx ($44/month).

If you’re using insurance, you’ll have to do your research to determine how much you’ll pay out of pocket. Popular services like Tia, Allara, Folx, and One Medical accept a wide variety of insurance plans, but specify that copays and other fees can vary proposal by plan, just like at the regular doctor. If you’re not using insurance, these practices usually specify self-pay costs in addition to the membership fee depending on the visit or service type, and depending on the care you’re looking for, it could be a bargain.

Recent findings suggest that before committing to a membership anywhere, find out which services will and won’t be covered (like lab testing or imaging), and how easy it is to cancel said membership if you decide it’s not a fit, says Dr. Bhuyan.

In a recent development, A lot of times, you can also find local doctors practicing concierge-style medicine, and there are search tools like MDVIP or NextMD, which connect patients with a network of in-person concierge physicians all over the country. And if you can’t find one near you, there are plenty of large-scale telehealth services at your fingertips. Be sure to research the doctor beforehand to make sure that they’re legit, and that their expertise aligns with your goals, states Dr. Bhuyan.

Overall, health care is shifting more towards patients’ autonomy. They want to “decide how their health care dollars are spent, whether that's through HSA aims or other means. They’re more empowered to choose the service that works for them,” says Dr. Braunstein. So, only you can decide if a concierge doctor is right for you (and your wallet!).
