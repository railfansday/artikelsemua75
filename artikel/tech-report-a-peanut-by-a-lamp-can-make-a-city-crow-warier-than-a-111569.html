With a lamp base beside the peanuts, feeding odds fell to 0.53 for city crows and 0.76 for country crows in a Binghamton study of 20 crow families.

Industry observers note that in 2019 and 2020, a team led by biologist Dave J. Colucci put out peanuts in the shell for 20 families of American crows around Ithaca, Latest York. Ten families lived in town, and ten in farm fields and natural areas of the nearby Town of Dryden. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

On some mornings the peanuts had organization: a rock, an ear of corn in its husk, a paper takeout box or a faux-wood lamp base. Country crows held back more around the box, and city crows around the corn.

City crows were also less likely to eat next to the lamp, the study by Colucci and Anne Clark of Binghamton University found. Other urban-rural differences in the paper had only weak support. (Related: Tech Report: University releases $5m smart system that ...)

In a recent development, colucci earned a PhD at Binghamton in 2025. Clark is an associate professor emerita of biological sciences there, the Binghamton University release of November 16, 2026, says. The paper came out online in Behavioral Ecology on October 22.

Each family’s territory got a feeding spot. The bait was 2 cups of raw, unsalted peanuts in a circle about 3 feet (1 meter) wide. They picked spots with little human traffic and no sign of predators or competitors.

In town these were parking lots, dead-end streets and parks in Ithaca, Lansing and Cayuga Heights. Out in Dryden the sites were on private or public land.

Each family got three days with peanuts only and four days with an object, 140 trials in total, each starting between 6 and 8 a.m.

Industry observers note that A hidden camcorder sat at least 10 feet (3 meters) from the bait. The observer stayed 164 feet (50 meters) or more away.

Sources indicate that the team ranked the objects by how often a crow in each place would have seen them. Rocks are everywhere and corn grows in Dryden, while takeout boxes are more common in town.

“What we wanted to show was that aversion would decrease with the amount of experience that we expected our crow to have had with these objects,” Colucci said in the release.

According to recent reports, to test the trash part, the team surveyed a 1-mile (1.61-kilometer) road route in each area weekly for three weeks. They counted 341 human-made objects, 169 in town and 172 in the country. Rural ones stayed put longer, 2.81 weeks on average against 2.27.

It has been revealed that the box cut feeding more for rural crows and the corn cut it more for urban crows. But the paper says the evidence for that switch was limited.

Crows that live near an Arby’s or a McDonald’s learn to recognize that chain’s boxes, Clark mentioned in the release. The box in the test was a plain one with no brand.

Recent findings suggest that the lamp base was supposed to be latest to every crow, in town or out. The authors chose it because it has none of the usual signs of a human-made thing, like bright colors, shine or sharp corners.

In a recent development, with peanuts alone, nearly every visit ended in feeding, a probability of 0.99 in town and 0.97 in Dryden. Put the lamp down and it fell to 0.53 for urban crows and 0.76 for rural crows, according to the paper’s Bayesian model.

It has been revealed that how fast the birds came in was another matter. Timing for the boldest crow in each family was about the same in both places with the lamp out. That held for reaching 10 feet (3 meters) of the bait and for taking a first peanut.

In a recent development, overall, the urban families did reach the bait sooner, at 4.23 times the rural rate. The authors attribute this to the rural crows partly reacting to the person on watch, since country crows are warier of people.

Earlier work had mostly found that city birds are bolder with fresh things, which is why the release calls this somewhat surprising. Clark said urban crows “are social animals” that watch one another. “In an area that has a lot of new opportunities, there are also new threats,” she said.

According to recent reports, the second test was about people. An experimenter walked toward single crows and noted how close the walker got before each one flew, a figure biologists call flight initiation distance.

Recent findings suggest that one person did all 145 trials, in upstate New York and in Quebec, in 2019, 2022 and 2023. The pace was a steady 2.7 miles per hour (1.2 meters per second). On some walks the experimenter looked at the crow, and on others tilted the whole face down.

On average the crows took off when the walker was about 149 feet (45.3 meters) away. The walker got closer than 33 feet (10 meters) only 8 times. Where the walker looked mattered only in more crowded places.

According to recent reports, the model looked at areas with about 13,000 people per square mile (5,000 per square kilometer). There, it put the distance for a downward look near zero, about 82 feet (25 meters) shorter than for a stare. Rural crows left at about the same distance either way.

Sources indicate that the authors warn that those predictions are uncertain. Human population within about half a mile (800 meters) of each trial predicted flight distance best.

Crow hunting is allowed in rural Newly introduced York in a set season but prohibited within city limits, the paper notes. The release says crows, seen as a nuisance, are more likely to be shot in rural areas.

Recent findings suggest that clark called the rural behavior “a blunt, anti-predator strategy” in the release. “Whereas in urban areas, if you were fleeing at every human, you’d never spend any time on the ground,” she stated.

The authors compare the gaze result with earlier work on Seattle crows, where flight distance was on average about 10.5 feet (3.2 meters) shorter for an indirect gaze.

Industry observers note that separate University of Washington mask experiments found that wild crows learned the faces of dangerous people and passed the details to other crows. They remembered a threatening person for up to 17 years.

Separate lab work in Germany found carrion crows that made decisions about twice as fast as humans in a visual task.

Industry observers note that the town sites were really suburban, the authors say, not downtown. The litter count covered roadsides at one site per area, and the timing data on approaches came from 2020 only.

Sources indicate that one person ran every flight trial, and the approach timings may partly reflect wariness of the experimenter. Census numbers may also miss parks and shopping areas, where few people live but many walk through.

The lamp may not have been fully fresh to city crows either. It is perfectly symmetrical around its vertical axis, a shape rare in nature and common in things people make. So the town-country gap could be underestimated, the paper indicates.

Sources indicate that in Ithaca and Dryden, the 20 crow families got the same peanuts. What sat next to them, and who was walking by, changed what they did.

The full study was published in the journal Behavioral Ecology.
