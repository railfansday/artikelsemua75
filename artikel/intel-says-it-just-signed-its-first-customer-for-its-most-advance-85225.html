Intel Says It Just Signed Its First Customer for Its Most Advanced Chipmaking Process. The Stock Is Still More Than 60% Below Its June Record.

"And then 18A, I'm involved in production and we just signed the first customer," Intel (INTC -7.97%) CEO Lip-Bu Tan said on Wednesday, Nov. 18, at the TomorrowX Summit in Austin, Texas.

In a recent development, he didn't name the customer. Intel's tech chief, Pushkar Ranade, later clarified on X that it had signed up for 18A-P, an upgraded version of 18A, the manufacturer's most advanced manufacturing process. CNBC, which reported Tan's remarks, called it the first outside customer for Intel's 18A chips. (Related: Tech Report: Animal control called to rescue alligator ...)

For a organization still working to build a manufacturing business for other chip designers, the signing is a milestone. Shares haven't gotten a lift from it, though. Intel stock closed at around $55 on Tuesday, Nov. 17, and closed near $51 on Friday, Nov. 20 -- more than 60% below its record close of $140.94 on June 22. (Related: Build A Racetrack codes (November 2026)...)

In a recent development, I think that response makes sense. A signed customer starts a foundry relationship, but it isn't revenue yet. And Intel's own numbers show how small its outside manufacturing business still is.

Intel might never tell investors who the customer is.

Recent findings suggest that "As is standard practice in the foundry business, I have consistently maintained that we will not disclose customer details," Tan said in prepared remarks for Intel's Oct. 29 earnings call.

Sources indicate that A name could still come from the customer's side. That same Wednesday, Intel mentioned Microsoft is the first manufacturer to announce use of its EMIB chip packaging tech, for chips Microsoft designs for its Azure cloud.

Sure, outside companies have lined up behind Intel's latest technology before. Microsoft stated in February 2024 that it planned to produce a chip design on Intel 18A. That September, Intel said it would build an artificial intelligence (AI) networking chip for Amazon's Amazon Web Services on 18A too. And in August, Japanese chip designer Socionext (OTC:SOCNF) said it would use 18A-P to develop custom chips.

Still, Tan described this customer as signed, and Ranade linked it to 18A-P, so it's arguably the first customer signed for that process. Investors still don't know how many chips the customer intends to buy and when Intel gets paid.

Intel's third-quarter foundry revenue grew 58% year over year to $6.7 billion, but most of it came from Intel's own product groups. Revenue from outside customers was $411 million, compared to $293 million in the second quarter and just $32 million a year before. Intel mentioned the third-quarter jump came mostly from a non-recurring customer program. And in its quarterly filing, the firm said substantially all of its foundry business still supports manufacturing for Intel's own products.

So, outside revenue is growing, but it's still around 6% of Intel's foundry sales, and the latest quarter relied on a one-time contract.

Industry observers note that the losses are shrinking, at least. The foundry's third-quarter operating loss was $1.9 billion, around $240 million less than in the second quarter and almost $470 million less than a year before.

"These results represent meaningful progress, although we still have considerable work ahead of us to deliver a sustainably profitable Foundry business," CFO David Zinsner remarked in Intel's prepared remarks for the Oct. 29 call.

For the signing to matter to shareholders, I think the customer would have to come forward, since Intel won't name it. Intel's 18A-P process also has to move from risk production, which began in June, to volume.

Industry observers note that intel states the process is on track to launch across its own products by year-end, but it hasn't said when an outside customer's chips will follow. The deal also needs to show up as recurring outside revenue instead of another one-time program.

Sources indicate that more is at stake, too. Intel noted in its last quarterly filing that committed demand for 14A, its next process, will dictate how fast and how far it expands manufacturing. That includes demand from potential significant outside customers. A signed 18A-P customer could help Intel get those commitments. But a deal on 18A-P isn't a commitment to 14A.

In a recent development, meanwhile, investors are already paying plenty for Intel's future profits. At around $51, the stock trades at a price-to-earnings ratio of about 29 on next year's expected earnings. And Intel expects non-GAAP (adjusted) earnings per share to fall to $0.25 in the fourth quarter from $0.52 in the third.

Tan's announcement is progress for a foundry that's mainly made Intel's own chips. But until the customer is named and its chips show up in Intel's outside revenue, I don't think it changes much for the stock.
