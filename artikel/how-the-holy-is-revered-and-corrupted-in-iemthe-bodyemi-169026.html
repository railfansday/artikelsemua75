How the Holy Is Revered and Corrupted in The Body

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

I wanted nothing more than to see the Holy Spirit. One day after church, my grandfather described the moment he first believed was witnessing the Holy Spirit: a soothing, cleansing sensation in which God, in some way, possessed and reassured your mind. Divine intervention felt attainable for a moment. At first, I, too, hoped to discover serenity, an especially enticing thought since my parents were far less religious. But over time, that belief twisted in my preteen mind, instilling instead a fear that every misstep might damn me. To be viewed unfavorably by God—as a kid—is a terrifying thought. And it lingers.

I soon let go of the institutionalized beliefs. Yet, a longing to believe in something greater—especially when facing the unknown—never fully left me. I still desired to find spiritual ground, one free from this all-consuming, stress-fueling shame. This friction between spiritual surrender and self-actualization is the beating heart of Quinn Shephard’s The Body. Beneath a relentless downpour of religious guilt and an insatiable appetite for forgiveness, the students at St. Mark’s Catholic High School battle their inner demons as they discern what they want for themselves versus what the world expects of them.

Industry observers note that shephard and co-showrunner Gina Gammell thrust the audience into an age of Razor flip phones, AOL instant messaging, and Juicy Couture tracksuits, all set to a 2000s soundtrack featuring Kylie Minogue and No Doubt. Its barbed-edged storytelling draws comparisons to Cruel Intentions and Pretty Little Liars, with a bewitching touch of Jennifer’s Body or The Crucible. Here, girl teenagedom traverses suppressed sapphic love and the systemic weaponization of religious guilt—an incisive critique biting enough to solicit a petition with over 120,000 people pushing to cancel it. This coming-of-age series emerges from the dual nature of what we hold sacred: its capacity for reverence and its vulnerability to corruption. Catholic school scares me. Teenagers scare me more, especially Lisbeth (Geena Meszaros), the hyper-religious, manipulative student at the heart of the story. At first, The Body introduces us to Lisbeth as the quintessential “weird kid,” socially ostracized because of her eyebrow-less face and her freakishly religious family. (She evokes Carrie in Carrie.) But as The Body unfolds, Lisbeth pulls—or rather, tugs—at the web of guilt ensnaring the school’s dance team, the Lions, concerning the near-death of their newly introduced teammate, Grace (Sofia Wylie). Grace fell into a coma shortly after the girls pretended to sacrifice her at a Halloween party, popping ecstasy pills they referred to as the “body of Satan.” This hazing ritual is all in good fun until Grace’s heart actually stops … if only briefly. Industry observers note that the aftermath casts a shadow of guilt over everything—especially for Anya (Kristina Bogic), queen bee and dance team captain, who moved Grace’s unconscious body back to her bedroom with Lisbeth’s help. Soon after, Anya begins to see visions of Grace in her dreams. The moment she confesses this to the group, the other girls start confirming visions of their comatose friend. This uncanny groupthink preys on the girls with something to hide, who carry shame with them wherever they go. Anya, for instance, feels deep contrition for her romantic feelings for her best friend Elise (Sarah Boustany), whereas Elise more boldly embraces her queer sexuality. Elise faces the world with a steady confidence, shielding her from spiritual panic—and its subsequent manipulation.

The same isn’t true for most of her teammates, all of whom seek solace in the intangible, in God and the Holy Spirit, in the saintly visions of Grace. Grace then becomes a symbol of the sacred, a story that inspires people and therefore can be corrupted to control others. The girls’ desperate need for forgiveness makes them vulnerable to Lisbeth’s manipulation. When the team goes on a getaway together, they take hallucinogenic mushrooms before deciding that they must purge evil spirits from Grace by way of an exorcism (led by Lisbeth, of course). Lisbeth controls this ritual, forcing each girl to confess their sins. (She already knows them all after reading all their private messages.) Her feigned omniscience incites hysteria among the girls.

Industry observers note that shame clouds nearly all the girls’ minds. Maddie (Shirley Chen) wrestles with an eating disorder, some girls admit to premarital sex or impure thoughts, and Lisbeth attempts to squeeze out a confession from Elise about her feelings for Anya. The Body runs the gamut of teenage girlhood, but Shephard distills these insecurities into a single critique of control: how the holy—the fear of God—can exploit people, corrupt what we see, and shape what we believe. In episode 6, “Star Quality,” mass hysteria breaks out in the town, with people associated with the church now believing to see visions and revere Grace as a saintly figure. It’s driven by an innate desire to be seen—to see something—and a desperate desire to be part of something larger.

Industry observers note that this same desire, to witness something collectively transcendent, forged the foundation of my spiritual beliefs. It also scared me enough to run from them. A connection to God came with fear of hellfire and the shame of sin. In this way, forgiveness felt like something I received out of desperation, rather than salvation. The Body studies the dangers of this unmitigated belief, of a doctrine designed to parse out the unknown. But by whose authority? Shephard’s story reminds us that the storyteller—the pastor or the manipulative teenage girl—controls the things we see and the things we may believe.
