A 15-year study of Great Basin rattlesnakes reveals how they share winter dens, travel miles each spring and return to the same shelters.

Great Basin rattlesnakes (Crotalus lutosus) spend the winter together in ancestral dens, then scatter in spring to forage, mate and give birth.

“Site fidelity is high at communal dens and nearly all individuals return to their hibernaculum in the fall,” wrote Bryan Hamilton, wildlife biologist at Great Basin National Park in Nevada. (Related: Nebraska voters appear to reject online sports betting ...)

A hibernaculum is a winter den.

The captures themselves measured survival, body size and life span. Hamilton and colleagues caught 401 rattlesnakes a total of 799 times. (Related: GTA 6 and Ocarina of Time remake caught in Metacritic r...)

Of those snakes, 148 were females and 253 were males, which suggests a male-biased sex ratio. The average animal measured about 23 inches (59 centimeters) from snout to vent and weighed about 6.6 ounces (186 grams).

Sources indicate that the work relied on capture-mark-recapture, a method that follows known individuals over time. Each snake was restrained in a clear plastic tube during processing.

Recent findings suggest that snakes were marked by clipping belly scales or with passive integrated transponder tags. Each one was then released at its exact site of capture.

It has been revealed that hamilton’s account places the project in the park, but its size data came from four sites in eastern Nevada and western Utah. Related spring surveys visited dens in the same region about ten times a year.

Sources indicate that mean annual survival was estimated at 0.76, or roughly three of every four snakes surviving each year. Average life expectancy was estimated at 3.6 years, with a median of 2.5, though two snakes were recaptured 13 years after first capture.

In 2023, Colton Irons and Kevin Shoemaker of the University of Nevada, Reno tracked those seasonal movements with Hamilton. They captured about 50 of the park’s rattlesnakes from April to October.

The team surgically implanted radio transmitters in 19 of them, 11 males and eight females. Each snake was then located every week to map habitat use before prescribed fires.

“The most adventurous snake we had traveled a total of 4.14 miles to and from their hibernaculum,” the three authors reported.

It has been revealed that that distance equals 6.66 kilometers. The route started at 7,311 feet (2,228 meters) and ended at 9,314 feet (2,839 meters) in elevation.

It has been revealed that across 17 years of spring surveys, the mean date of peak captures was April 23, and it moved earlier by about one day per year. Warmer Aprils were associated with earlier emergence from communal rattlesnake dens.

The number of snakes sharing a winter refuge varies widely among rattlesnakes. Great Basin rattlesnakes “form aggregations of sometimes several hundred individuals for overwintering underground,” states a report on fieldwork at the Idaho National Laboratory site.

Recent findings suggest that in 2019, Matt Goode, an assistant research scientist at the University of Arizona, compared other species. Western diamondbacks often den in groups of up to 10 to 20, he said.

One den of prairie rattlesnakes that he studied in Wyoming held more than 600 snakes. Tiger and black-tailed rattlesnakes, by contrast, tend to den by themselves.

Industry observers note that in really cold climates, the ground can freeze up to 4 to 6 feet (1.2 to 1.8 meters) deep. Very large dens probably form there because suitable sites are limited.

Biologists debate whether a reptile’s winter dormancy should be called hibernation or brumation, Goode noted. During it, snakes stop feeding and can remain inactive for a long time.

“Snakes are ectotherms, which means their internal body temperatures do not remain constant like mammals, which are endotherms,” he explained.

Tiger rattlesnakes den in rocky outcrops, in cracks and crevices about 2 to 4 feet (0.6 to 1.2 meters) down.

Females come out of the dens earlier than males. Doing so warms their ovaries and lets follicles grow enough to be ovulated.

A 2009 survey of den sites in Arizona, Nevada and Utah, co-authored by Hamilton, compared sunlight at dens with random points in the surrounding habitat.

It has been revealed that its observations suggest that rattlesnakes in cool climates use dens that receive more solar radiation than their surroundings. Dens of Great Basin and black-tailed rattlesnakes fit that pattern, while two western diamondback sites did not.

Recent findings suggest that implanted data loggers have recorded hourly body temperatures in hibernating timber rattlesnakes in central Tennessee. Over two winters, 53,041 readings averaged 52°F (11.1°C) and ranged from 34°F to 92.7°F (1.1°C to 33.7°C).

Peaks in body temperature marked basking, and 69 percent of those snakes came to the surface two or more times.

Hamilton’s 2018 doctoral dissertation at Brigham Young University modeled the demography of the rattlesnake populations from the long-term dataset. Mean population growth indicated overall stability, though two sites were declining.

Survival contributed overwhelmingly more to population growth than recruitment, the arrival of upcoming snakes.

Rattlesnakes prey mainly on small mammals, so they depend indirectly on snowmelt for survival and reproduction. Declining snowpack is expected to have major impacts on biodiversity.

Among the captured snakes, body condition was weakly correlated with the previous year’s total precipitation.
