According to recent reports, verra announced a $5 million seed round led by Kearny Jackson, with participation from Petra, a16z Speedrun and angel investors. The New York startup says it will use the funding to develop its product, hire and expand integrations with enterprise knowledge systems. Its initial market is consulting, where teams are paid for expertise but can spend considerable time finding, checking and repackaging work the firm has already done.

Verra’s approach is to give an AI agent access to relevant precedents and the methods a particular practice uses, rather than asking it to start from a blank prompt. The result can be a sourced first draft in a format the consultant already works in, such as a spreadsheet, report or slide deck. That makes this a test of how AI fits into professional services workflows, not just of how well a model can summarize documents. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

Consulting deliverables are rarely interchangeable. A previous engagement may contain a useful model or method, while its assumptions, client data and conclusions are unsuitable for the next assignment. A good handoff needs to distinguish what can be reused from what has to be checked again. One advantage of working inside a familiar template is that the consultant can review and change the result without reconstructing a generated answer in another tool. Industry observers note that verra’s institutional memory documentation describes connecting existing data sources and using projects and skills to carry working methods from one engagement to the next. Its sourced analysis tools cite original material and can extract information from a collection of documents into a table. That distinction matters: a search result points to a file, while a cited output gives reviewers a way to inspect the particular evidence behind a statement. A citation still does not establish that the underlying evidence is applicable, accurate or current.

In a recent development, the firm says Berkeley Research Group expanded its deployment to about 1,000 people within two months of a proof of concept, and more than 87% of onboarded consultants used it weekly. Those figures are from Verra’s announcement; the percentage is for onboarded consultants, not the entire firm or the consulting industry. The Keystone Group also deployed the platform firmwide, according to Verra. These deployments indicate that some firms are moving past small pilots, but the release does not independently measure the effect on client outcomes or profitability.

For firms evaluating this approach, time to first draft is only one part of the calculation. The more useful comparison is the time to a client-ready deliverable, including review, corrections and approval. A fast spreadsheet draft loses much of its value if a reviewer has to rebuild the logic. Conversely, a draft that makes assumptions visible and points back to the right sources may be useful even when it needs substantive edits.

A consulting firm’s archive mixes reusable frameworks with client-specific and sometimes confidential material. Verra’s governance documentation states connected files retain source permissions, and firms can use their existing single sign-on and directory. Verra also describes both a hosted option and single-tenant deployment in the customer’s own cloud. Buyers still need to validate how permissions behave when people switch practices, when a source is withdrawn, or when an output combines material drawn from several engagements.

The broader opportunity is to make an organization’s accumulated work useful without turning every past deliverable into reusable training material. Whether that works at scale will depend as much on the quality of the source archive and the firm’s review practices as on the agent itself.
