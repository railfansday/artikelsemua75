What Happens When You Come Home From Your Thru-Hike?

Industry observers note that there are endless articles, Youtube videos, books, and discussions about preparing for trail. But what happens when we come back home? (Related: Industry News: Train a Sentence Embedding Model with 1B...)

Sources indicate that this question receives far less guidance. But for many hikers, “coming home” is one of the hardest parts.

I thru-hiked the Appalachian Trail and hiked 1600 miles of the Continental Divide Trail, and I have felt the challenge of spending months on trail and then coming home to a “society” that feels so at odds with the trail experience.

How might we be able to carry the gifts of the trail with us and create a life and a world that is more deeply alive?

Industry observers note that for months, we sleep beneath the stars, watch the Milky Way move across the sky, catch the sunrise, and feel the cool breeze of dawn, brimming over with possibility as birds greet the day. As we walk, carrying everything we need on our backs, demands and expectations fall away.

There is no one left to impress, and no one left to be, other than our raw, unfiltered, beautiful selves. In this rawness and vulnerability, we meet each other. We walk, we talk, we sleep under the stars. We bear witness to one another’s stories, we hold one another up. We watch as the first flowers open in the spring, and then as the first leaves turn crimson, set on fire in the fall.

Somewhere along the way, we find deep connection with ourselves, with others, and with nature. We find more space, more color, more life. For the first time, perhaps, we have eyes to see.

We return to our jobs and routines, and the demands and expectations return too. Suddenly, how people interact with us is dependent on how we look, how much money we make, or where we’re from. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

We’re expected to work, probably a lot of hours, cooped up indoors, often without a break. With little appreciation, perhaps little financial reward, and little connection to how our work might benefit people or nature. The core of who we are, which had emerged so quietly on trail, sometimes feels buried once again.

I believe the connection and community that we experience on trail open up an incredible possibility. In returning home, we have a chance to carry these gifts with us. We have a chance to create a kinder and more beautiful world: a world with more space, more color, and more life. I believe we can start by rewriting our own lives, step by step, and then by participating in the rewriting of our own small corner of the world. And maybe, this journey is the most key trail of all.

We can begin by considering how we can incorporate small aspects of our experience into our everyday life (and then more, and more, until our lives are more and more aligned).

After all, it’s one step after another that allowed us to hike for hundreds, or thousands, of miles. Are our everyday lives really so different from the trail? Isn’t the natural world all around us? The birds, the flowers, the trees, right outside our windows? The people around us every day, are they not people just like people on trail?

And if people on trail can form supportive, inclusive, beautiful communities, why shouldn’t we also be able to do that in our daily lives?

Industry observers note that we all have the power to act, to respond, to create something in this world. On trail, we persevere in the face of conditions that we have no control over: difficult weather, trail conditions, and the physical limitations of our body. Truly, we are small and weak in the face of the power and beauty of Mother Nature.

In a recent development, but we have the power to respond, participate, and persevere. And as it turns out, that power ends up taking us pretty far. From Georgia to Maine, or Mexico to Canada, or any distance that we so choose.

Similarily, in society, we are all small and weak in the face of many of the powerful forces around us. Our culture, the economy, the govermment, and the people around us are all forces that we have little control over. But just like on trail, we always have the power to respond, to participate, and to persevere. And I believe that power can take us pretty far. To a better world, I venture to imagine.

I think we can get there by leaning into the same things that we leaned into on trail: community, perseverance, connection with nature, trust in ourselves, and other people.

Before hiking, I never thought I could hitchhike, that I could navigate remote wilderness on my own, or that I could literally walk from Georgia to Maine. I never imagined that I could break so many “rules” of our society. But I did, and what I found through that vulnerability was deep connection, deep love, faith, and an abiding confidence.

Might it be that we are playing by unspoken rules in our everyday lives that might be holding us back from true confidence, true connection, and fresh possibilities?

What might happen if we start to question them, and connect anyway?

When we have an urgent need on trail, oftentimes we have no choice but to ask for help. So like it or not, we swallow our pride, get over our fears, and ask some stranger if they can help. Usually, they do, and gladly.

What might happen if we began to do that in our everyday lives? What might happen if we offered that same level of support to others?

What latest possibilities might break open, once we begin to realize that the trail was never separate from society, nor was the end of our hike the end of our journey.

What if it was only the beginning?

What might the next steps look like in practice?

According to recent reports, let’s start with a few questions to consider. Everyone has their own unique experience on trail, and chances are, no two hikers’ “return-home journeys” will be the same. Journaling is one tool that I’ve found helpful in processing my experiences.

What did you experience on trail? How did you feel, and what fresh perspectives did you find? What aspects of your hike contributed to feeling the way you did?

It has been revealed that what perspectives or ways of being did you find on trail that you want to hold onto? What are some small steps you can take to incorporate those perspectives or ways of being into your daily life?

Additionally, below are some of the small practical changes that I’ve found helpful. Have your own practices? Add them to the conversation.

1. Spend the first half hour or more of the day doing something just for you: outside and active is even better. This can set the tone for the entire day. The day belongs to you, to nature, to life itself. Everything else fits into that deeper truth.

3. Eat well (not like on trail, hah!).

4. Get outside regularly. It could be day hikes, weekend backpacking trips, or just taking in the sunlight for 5 minutes from your porch.

According to recent reports, 5. Along the lines of getting outside: connect with God, the universe, nature, or a sense of meaning beyond yourself.

Sources indicate that 6. Stay in touch with trail friends, and find like-minded people at home. Going on the post-trail journey with people “who get it” is immeasurably valuable.

Industry observers note that 7. Find ways to have the authentic relationships like the ones you found on trail. How might you be able to give and receive help more freely, and be yourself without reserve, with the people closest to you?

8. Find joy in the small things. Maybe it’s a fresh bouquet of flowers from the farmers market, the sunlight streaming in through the window, or the birds chirping.

9. Think about how your work can contribute to others, to yourself, and to nature. Start to think about how you might be able to negotiate your work, or your relationship with work, to find better work-life balance, and/or a deeper sense of meaning and contribution. It doesn’t all have to be better all at once. Small steps in the right direction is all that’s needed.

In a recent development, 10. Learn about money. Things like budgeting, the time value of money, how debt works, saving and investing. Like it or not, our world runs on money. Money is one of the major keys to more freedom in our world. Use it as a tool to create more space, joy, abundance, and freedom for yourself, your community, and nature.

Sources indicate that 11. Acceptance. Accepting things we can’t control allows us to have more peace. And we can lean into that which we can control: our response, which is powerful. Just like on trail.
