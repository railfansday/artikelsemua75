Industry observers note that china is "actively working to suppress India's rise," even as the relationship between the two major Asian powers has thawed in the past year, according to a U.S. panel that sees an opening for greater outreach to India.

Industry observers note that on Nov. 18, a U.S. China Economic and Security Review Commission report mentioned Beijing is applying military, economic, maritime, and other forms of pressure to constrain India. (Related: Industry News: Retrieval Augmented Generation with Hugg...)

China has recalled engineers and withheld equipment to slow India's electronics manufacturing, slashed pharmaceutical input prices to undercut upcoming Indian plants, and imposed export controls on battery technology to impede the development of India's clean energy industry, the report said. In a recent development, it added that these tensions offer the U.S. an opportunity to counter growing Chinese influence by building closer ties with India across key sectors such as pharmaceutical and defense.

It has been revealed that ties have improved since a deadly border skirmish in 2020, but experts told CNBC that trust is still thin between Beijing and Fresh Delhi. However, they added that the uneasy peace with China is unlikely to make India align automatically with the U.S. as an ally.

The Indian foreign and commerce ministries and the Chinese foreign ministry did not respond to emails seeking comments.

The thawing in relations between India and China came amid industrial demand, as well as the need for "a whole lot of posturing for the U.S.," remarked Arpit Chaturvedi, South Asia advisor at strategic advisory firm Teneo.

Over the past year, India faced tariff pressures and needed to signal to Washington that its options remain open, including that of renegotiating relations with China, he explained, but added that as the trade deal with the U.S. is "close enough now," there is no pressing need for that signaling.

Further improvement in the relationship is also limited by reports of renewed border tensions between the two sides and Beijing's scant reciprocation on easing access to technology and people movement, he said.

Last month, an Indian military official said that confrontations with Chinese soldiers were ongoing across several locations along the country's northeastern border, even as top leaders from the countries met with fanfare at the BRICS Summit in September.

It has been revealed that another flash point was when Beijing put export controls on tunnel boring machines last month. "Our relations with China remain good, and if there aren't any new hurdles, they can improve further," India's Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal reportedly noted after China's move to tighten TBM exports.

On Monday, a Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson reportedly stated Beijing is ready to work with India to facilitate visa arrangements for business personnel from both sides amid reports that fewer Indian businessmen got visas to attend an annual trade fair in Shanghai.

The Chinese "actively disrupt" areas where India is trying to make progress in terms of moving global supply chains, developing advanced industrial technologies such as EVs, batteries, electronic components, and pharmaceutical ingredients, Pramit Pal Chaudhuri, South Asia practice head at Eurasia Group, told CNBC.

He added China is increasingly focused on containing India economically rather than through military overtures.

Sources indicate that india's manufacturing push still relies on Chinese inputs, from electronics components and solar materials to rare earth magnets and specialized machinery. Between April and October, India imported goods worth $96.6 billion from China, up more than 30% from a year ago, as per commerce ministry data released earlier this month.

Its major imports from China include electronic goods, machinery, chemicals, transport equipment, iron and steel, and pharmaceutical products.

While India needs inputs for its manufacturing ambitions from Beijing and China values access to Indian markets, trust between the two sides is "still thin," according to Reema Bhattacharya, head of Asia research at risk intelligence company Verisk Maplecroft, citing a large trade imbalance and overlapping interests in South Asia and the Indian Ocean as additional problems.
