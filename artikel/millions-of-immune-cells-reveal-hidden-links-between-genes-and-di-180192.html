Millions of immune cells reveal hidden links between genes and disease

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

By measuring gene activity in 5.4 million immune cells, researchers uncovered genetic clues to disease that whole-blood studies had missed.

A blood sample is usually read as one mixture, and for years the studies linking genes to disease have read it that way too.

Yet a gene’s tie to a disease can be found in one kind of immune cell and nowhere else, so a whole-blood reading misses it.

Researchers sequenced each participant’s genome and measured gene activity in more than 5 million immune cells, one cell at a time.

About half of the effects linked to common genetic variants showed up in only one or a few cell types.

That is the main result of the first phase of a project called TenK10K, which aims to build the same map for 10,000 people. (Related: Breaking: Future Value Calculator: How to Project Your ...)

The study’s senior authors included Joseph Powell of the Garvan Institute of Medical Research and UNSW Sydney and Daniel MacArthur of the Centre for Population Genomics.

The center is a joint initiative of Garvan and the Murdoch Children’s Research Institute.

“Most of the biological mechanisms we uncovered were invisible to prior studies,” Powell said.

The team took blood from 1,925 Australian adults who had joined two earlier studies, one of eye health in Tasmania and one of heart disease in Sydney.

They sequenced each person’s whole genome. Then they measured which genes were switched on in each of 5.4 million immune cells and sorted the cells into 28 types.

Comparing people’s DNA with gene activity in each cell type revealed which genetic variants turn a gene up or down, and in which cells.

They found common variants affecting 83% of the 21,404 genes they tested.

Whole-genome sequencing also let them see rare variants, which the DNA chips used in most earlier studies read poorly. Rare variants left signals on 47% of the genes, more than 30,000 effects in all.

About half of the common-variant effects appeared in only one or a few of the 28 cell types.

Almost none appeared in all of them. A whole-blood study averages signals across cell types, so it can miss effects that appear in only one or a few.

The team compared their map with the largest whole-blood study of this kind.

Industry observers note that that study drew on more than 30,000 people and found roughly 17,000 genes whose activity was associated with genetic variants. The single-cell map found about the same number using data from 1,925 people.

About a quarter of the genes in the single-cell map (4,572) were absent from the whole-blood study’s list.

“This is the most detailed map to date of how genetic variation shapes the immune system,” MacArthur said.

The team then matched their map against known genetic risk regions for 16 diseases, among them asthma, autoimmune conditions, cancers and infections, and for 44 blood traits from UK Biobank.

Where a risk variant and a gene-activity variant turned out to be the same one, that gene became a candidate cause.

Nearly 1,500 genes were tied to the diseases this way. Of all the genes tied to a disease or a blood trait, 893 had no reported genetic effect on their activity in the big whole-blood study.

Sources indicate that A gene called OSM is the example the team keeps coming back to. It is switched on in 20 of the 28 cell types.

Its link to inflammatory bowel disease, though, appears only in natural killer cells, where the OSM gene was more active in people carrying the risk variant.

The same matching tied a gene called IGHG4 to Alzheimer’s disease in one cell type only, memory B cells.

Sources indicate that in an email interview with Earth.com, the Garvan team was asked which finding surprised them most. They pointed to “how often the link to disease sits in just one type of immune cell.”.

Sources indicate that A gene can be active across the immune system yet connect to disease in a single cell type, they wrote. “Earlier whole-blood studies simply couldn’t see that.”.

Recent findings suggest that A map like this names candidate genes and the cells they act in. It does not show that changing a gene changes the disease, and no drug was tested.

The authors also wrote that their cutoff for calling an effect cell-specific is conservative, with no second dataset of the same size to check it against.

“This first phase focused on people of European ancestry, and we want our findings to apply to everyone,” the team told Earth.com.

As the project grows, they wrote, more people will let them detect rarer genetic variants and study rarer cells.

“In the next few years, the biggest impact will be on drug discovery rather than in the clinic,” they wrote.

Drugs backed by human genetic evidence are more likely to succeed in trials, they added, and over time the map could help match patients to treatments.

Which of the nearly 1,500 candidate genes actually drive disease is the open question, and this map can’t answer it on its own.

The summary data are free for other researchers to use, and two companion papers add layers on repeated DNA and on which stretches of DNA are open in each cell.

Someone still has to run the experiments that switch a candidate gene off in the right cell type.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
