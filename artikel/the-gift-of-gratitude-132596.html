Certain times of the year or milestone moments can increase the frequency with which we turn to appreciating the good, though this practice actually has value every single day of the year. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

The positive psychology movement has helped individuals focus more on what is strong rather than only on what is wrong. As a therapist who works with individuals at various life stages and in situations of distress, I know and embrace the fact that we can’t ignore the overwhelming symptoms, heartache, trauma, and pain; however, I also believe that every individual has strengths and reserves of resilience that deserve to be incorporated and highlighted.

Some people find this practice of being thankful and looking for gratitude moments to be relatively easy, but others find this much more difficult and unnatural.

Several tips are offered here for consideration to build the gratitude muscle. (Related: How to Build a Healthcare Robot from Simulation to Depl...)

Like the recent movement to incorporate more bloom-scrolling rather than just doom-scrolling on our phones, the notion of being on the lookout for more positivity can turn out to be a welcome shift for many. We know the gloom and doom exist, and we have plenty of opportunities to focus on it, but also actively seeking out more of the positive, the good, and the upbeat can be incredibly beneficial. Rather than dismissing the negative news or ignoring the overwhelming, we are making room for balance in our bodies and brains.

We can be in charge of our media meal plan and how we choose to budget our time and attention.

Sources indicate that the idea of being on the lookout for gratitude moments is sometimes mistaken for waiting for those “big-ticket” items in life, like a windfall of money, a long-awaited vacation, or a promotion at work. While momentous moments are terrific, they tend to be rarer. However, the small moments of appreciating a delicious meal, a story about an everyday hero, a more scenic route home, the feel of a favorite blanket, or a beautiful bird on a branch are available to us on a much more regular basis.

In a recent development, there is a Japanese concept of Ma, which translates to the gap, interval, or space between things. This is often referenced in Japanese architecture, art, or conversation, but the notion of embracing space or pause can also be utilized in this pursuit of the positive. The gap or interval is not empty or useless; instead, it represents a meaningful void full of potential. So even in those moments between hearing difficult news, awaiting dreaded test results, or anticipating the next migraine headache, we can attempt to orient to something small, quiet, amusing, or pleasant for which we can feel grateful.

We can absolutely embrace the significant, but we might as well also be on the lookout for the small because they are much more plentiful, readily available, and repeatable.

Being aware of possible triggers in our environment can have value and usefulness, but orienting toward noticing the glimmers internally and externally is often underutilized.

Glimmers are those small moments of calm or joy that can cue safety and relaxation for the nervous system. The notion of looking for glimmers was introduced by Deb Dana and based on work by Stephen Porges1, and this practice cues the body toward a state of safety and grounding rather than the fight-flight-freeze mode of response that is activated under threat.

Even on really bad days (or maybe even especially on really bad days), we can choose to notice some good or gratifying things.

Glimmers are the opposite of triggers; both exist in our world, and we deserve to be able to know how to be alert to both.

Recent findings suggest that when we are actively looking for more positive, joyful, beautiful, interesting, or unique in our daily world, we begin to see more of it. Sometimes that elegant, sturdy tree, beautifully colored house, smell of our favorite beverage, or sparkling ray of sunlight coming through the window at a certain time of day has gone unnoticed until we take the time to notice. And once we notice, we tend to orient to seeing it again and again—along with that more satisfying sense of appreciation on repeat.

It has been revealed that orienting to some good news, a funny joke, the stuffy nose that finally cleared up, an enthusiastic pet greeting, or a beloved song on the playlist can all be wonderful in the moment, but what really allows the biggest benefit is to allow the positivity to fully soak in. Like attending to a spill on the counter, dipping a sponge into the corner of the spill for just a moment won’t be as effective as slowly, carefully moving the sponge around the spill to fully absorb the liquid.

Recent findings suggest that when we pause to fully notice the benefits, orient the multi-sensorial delights, and stay with it for several moments, we are more wholeheartedly allowing the affirmative and encouraging to register in our systems. And if we choose to later repeat this positive moment in a journal or in a recounting to someone else, the benefits multiply and the positivity is reinforced.

According to recent reports, intentionally, mindfully staying aware of the positive is a happiness hack. And savoring the moment can be a superpower.

Sources indicate that various research studies support the notion that optimistic, grateful people are happier, calmer, more empathetic, less stressed, have better physical health, and even live longer2. Neuroimaging results (MRI and fMRI) also show that practicing gratitude engages key brain regions involved in reward processing and emotional regulation3. These findings support the incredible connection between mind and body.

It has been revealed that studied gratitude interventions include gratitude journals, gratitude letters or texts, and other methods of counting more blessings than burdens. Although there are various self-administered psychological interventions that can improve overall well-being (such as spending time in nature, mindfulness, or acts of kindness), psychological science does consistently show that gratitude practices improve overall mental and physical health.

In a recent development, even if this skill of looking for gratitude, positivity, and thankfulness doesn’t come easily or naturally to you, strive to refrain from self-blame about this. People have a wide variety of circumstances that can influence their ability to orient toward the good, and some of this is influenced by such things as external life circumstances and even genetics. The negativity bias in human brains is also well established and did have an evolutionary benefit4, but times are different now. A broader, more balanced perspective can help. And because brains are neuroplastic, practice in these skills makes for more permanence with these skills.

Be on the lookout for the lovely, and you, too, can optimize your inner optimist.
