Why International First Class Has Failed At US Airlines (But Shouldn’t)

It has been revealed that we’ve just seen American formally retire its Boeing 777 first class, and with that, no airline in the United States has a “true” international first class anymore. It’s the end of an era, because going back many decades, premium products were largely pioneered by US carriers. So I think this raises the question — why can US carriers not make first class work in a way that’s economically viable, especially as they keep talking about how premium they are?

Before we get into this, let me make sure we’re all on the same page when it comes to terms. This post isn’t about the premium cabin on domestic flights, which is often referred to as first class. Instead, it’s about international flights that have a cabin above business class, when it’s marketed as first class. So this would include airlines on my list of the world’s best first class products.

Recent findings suggest that airline executives are divided on the merits of having an international first class cabin. Some insist that there’s no need for such a cabin, as business class has improved so much. Meanwhile others absolutely see merit to such a product.

I mean, just look at how carriers like Air France, Lufthansa, Singapore Airlines, and SWISS, are all investing in first class. I think Air France’s La Premiere first class is the perfect example of the upside such a product has for an airline:.

What’s interesting is that for the airlines that succeed with first class, the single biggest market — and single biggest point of sale — is the United States. So how is it that carriers like Air France, Lufthansa, SWISS, etc., can make money offering first class to the United States (primarily targeting Americans), but US airlines seemingly can’t?

The US has way too many people with tens or hundreds of millions of dollars, or even billions of dollars. There are plenty of people who would pay to fly private, and would pay for Air France La Premiere first class, but would never consider paying the high price for first class on a US carrier, because it’s just not worth it. Industry observers note that at this point, no airline from the United States offers first class on international flights anymore. For airlines that can’t stop talking about how unbelievably premium they are, I find that to be a pretty perplexing reality. I know that many people have just accepted “oh, a good business class is the future,” but I have a different take.

I think no US carrier actually being able to offer a globally competitive product is a failure in strategy and creativity. They have access to the world’s most lucrative customer base, and other airlines can seemingly make money serving US customers with a first class product. The glitch is, US carriers have long not actually given people a reason to fly first class, all while offering too much capacity.

Industry observers note that personally I think there would be a lot of merit to an international first class product that’s boutique, and is operated in the right markets. That’s not to say that I think it’ll happen — I don’t — but I do think it could succeed. The only real hurdle to overcome would be how to ensure good service….

I don't expect US carriers to follow your advice, Ben. Part of the reason is a large number of hubs and not all of them have the same level of demand for very expensive fares. Beyond that, though, business class is much better than most of the now defunct first class cabins whose loss you bemoan. Many of those services were recliner seats, not lie flats, and most (if not all) of them lacked direct aisle access for all seats. Could business class be better? Sure but for most of us, it is very good, especially as compared to the back of the back of the bus. And, most of us will never be able or willing to pony up that kind of money anyway. The bottom line: if carriers can get back the investment required to install first class seats, deal with scheduling the planes only on certain routes, and still make money, we might see it. Otherwise, we won't.

This article is almost entirely from European airlines’ — in particular Air France’s — perspective, perhaps because of the transatlantic market, which alone drives a lot of US-origin premium traffic. There’s almost no discussion of Gulf or Asian airlines, which is surprising, given how Emirates and (among East Asian airlines) JAL, to some extent also SQ, continue to lead the way in offering a world-beating F product. Then again, US airlines see much more competition from the likes of Air France than the likes of JAL when it comes to premium routes, and have to remain competitive across the Atlantic while (except for United) ceding power to the superior Asian airlines across the Pacific.
