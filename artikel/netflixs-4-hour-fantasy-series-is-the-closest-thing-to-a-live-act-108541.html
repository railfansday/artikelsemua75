Netflix's 4-Hour Fantasy Series Is The Closest Thing To A Live-Action D&D Show

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Considering it's such a massive part of pop culture, Dungeons & Dragons hasn't found much success in the live-action realm. The 2000s movies are notorious, although perhaps earn a place in the 'so bad they're good' category, and while the more recent Honor Among Thieves brought joy to all, smiles in theaters didn't quite translate into great chests full of box office gold.

According to recent reports, on the small screen, it's a washout. Animated shows inspired by Dungeons & Dragons have enjoyed great success, especially the Critical Role universe on Prime Video, but as far as live-action is concerned, Stranger Things is as good as it gets. Netflix has announced a proper live-action Dungeons & Dragons project on the horizon in the shape of Ravenloft, which is currently being developed and makes history as the first true Dungeons & Dragons TV series in live-action. Four years ago, however, Netflix did release a short-lived fantasy series that, from a certain angle, is a live-action Dungeons & Dragons adaptation in all but name.

According to recent reports, netflix's primary The Witcher series (the one where Henry Cavill gets possessed by the soul of a Hemsworth brother halfway through) shares very little in common with Dungeons & Dragons. The show, unsurprisingly, feels more like a video game than a tabletop campaign, placing a single protagonist in the spotlight and focusing heavily on visual spectacle alongside a political landscape somewhere between Middle-earth and Westeros.

2022's The Witcher: Blood Origin recalibrates the same fictional world through a Dungeons & Dragons lens. You get the ragtag group of adventurers, all with different cultural backgrounds, skills, and weapons. Compared to its parent series, Blood Origin's streamlined story also flows more like a campaign, as the party works to thwart one central enemy locked in his fortress. Decisions are made that will affect future campaigns to come (i.e. the creation of the first Witcher), and there's an uptick in humor that draws more inspiration from the world of Dungeons & Dragons than The Witcher itself. There's even a charismatic Dungeon Master of sorts, as Jaskier acts as the story's narrator. (Related: Show, Don't Certify: How to Prepare for Your First Data...)

The problem with The Witcher: Blood Origin, of course, is that not many people like it. In fairness, the spinoff has its highlights. Meldof the Dwarf proves an inspired addition, and the four-part story certainly can't be accused of lacking ambition. At the same time, Blood Origin buckles under the weight of propping up The Witcher's future, and Jaskier's inclusion feels jarringly unnecessary.

Sources indicate that the burden of setting up new storylines for the main series, combined with the relatively short season, results in a tale with more exposition than character depth, and Lenny Henry's villain suffers most of all. Blood Origin wants viewers to care about how the prequel will impact Geralt and co. in the future, but that comes at the expense of letting the audience care about the spinoff characters themselves in the present.

Without the kind of substance that would justify a spinoff happening in the first place, even The Witcher: Blood Origin's historic events fall flat. The creation of the very first Witcher and the Conjunction of the Spheres pass by like relatively mundane events. The viewer needs to almost jolt themselves into remembering these are foundational events of The Witcher's universe playing out before their eyes, and not just seven unlucky heroes having a bad day.

Needless to say, the world will expect a little more from Ravenloft, especially with it being TV's first proper live-action Dungeons & Dragons venture. The failure of The Witcher: Blood Origin gives a clear lesson that the new series cannot rely solely on its famous universe and franchise connections, and should instead concoct a story and group of characters that would feel just as compelling if they existed outside Dungeons & Dragons altogether.
