Tech Report: Trump’s USDA changed the rules. Farmers and rural businesses paid the price

It has been revealed that this year, I was forced to lay off all five of my employees. My business wasn’t defeated by a competitor or a declining market. It was devastated by a decision from my own government.

I founded Sun Grants Consulting in 2023 to help farmers and rural small businesses apply for grants through the Rural Energy for America Program (REAP). We grew to five employees and filed almost 200 applications.

In March, USDA announced it would stop processing pending REAP applications and make no further grant awards until latest regulations took effect. Applicants who had followed the existing rules would have to start over. Fifty-seven of my clients’ applications were caught in that decision, representing more than $22 million in rural energy investments and more than $9 million in grant awards. Because my firm is paid only when clients win grants, our anticipated revenue from those applications vanished. (Related: Nebraska voters appear to reject online sports betting ...)

Congress created REAP in the 2002 farm bill to encourage renewable energy and efficiency investments. It has enjoyed bipartisan support, including reauthorization in the 2018 farm bill President Trump signed. REAP is not a blank check: Farmers and business owners put their own capital at risk, hire private contractors and pay costs the grants do not cover.

Then came a second blow. USDA issued a newly introduced rule in October that fundamentally changed how future applicants qualify. Farmers and businesses generally must complete projects, pay the bills and document 12 months of energy production or savings before they can even apply. USDA itself concedes “some uncertainty about whether an applicant will receive an award.”. (Related: Second Circuit denies Kalshi and Coinbase interim relie...)

USDA has turned an investment incentive into a reimbursement lottery. The businesses most in need of assistance are often least able to gamble their capital on a grant decision a year or more away.

Consider Michigan dairy farmer Ron Rusk, whose REAP application I helped prepare. After applying under the previous rules, he invested approximately $86,000 in solar equipment. He sought a grant covering roughly a quarter of the cost. USDA’s March reversal left his pending application in limbo and the October rule terminated his application.

Industry observers note that the damage extends beyond applicants. The National Sustainable Agriculture Coalition estimates that each REAP grant dollar generates $8.72 in total project spending. That means work for electricians, installers, manufacturers and rural contractors. USDA’s own budget documents say REAP projects give farmers and rural small businesses “new and sustainable income streams.”.

Yet USDA’s March announcement vowed to end “taxpayer handouts” for renewable energy, and the president’s executive order targets “market distorting subsidies.” The REAP grant is taxable income, and USDA states a farmer can claim it alongside the federal solar tax credit. In October, USDA revealed $8 billion in assistance to farmers facing higher fuel costs. Emergency relief may be warranted. But why is helping farmers pay for fuel acceptable while helping them invest in permanently lowering their energy bills is condemned?

The administration also invokes farmland protection. USDA research found that solar and wind installations directly occupied less than 0.05% of U.S. farmland in 2020. Protecting productive land matters; making energy grants harder to access for rural businesses is a different matter.

Industry observers note that there is a political irony, too. Brookings found that about two-thirds of REAP grants went to districts now represented by Republicans; 18 of the 20 districts receiving the most are Republican-held. These policies fall hardest on communities the administration promises to defend.

Industry observers note that the legal question is serious. Congress instructed USDA to “promote” renewable energy and efficiency for rural businesses, yet the USDA’s newly introduced rule itself states the policies behind it direct USDA to “disincentivize solar development on farmland.” An agency cannot rewrite a statute by policy.

In a recent development, farmers understand risk. They face weather, commodity prices and uncertain markets every year. They should not also have to guess whether Washington will change the rules after they invest.

A government that claims to champion American business should understand a fundamental business principle: Your word must mean something.
