In Stolen Focus, Johann Hari explores the distress of captive animals through veterinary behaviorist Nicholas Dodman's description of "frustrated biological objectives": innate needs that remain active even when circumstances prevent their expression. Zoochosis describes abnormal behaviors observed in captive animals, including compulsive self-directed activity.

It has been revealed that let’s consider if zoochosis could apply to human beings too: could some of our depression and anxiety be less about defective individuals and more about the increasingly unnatural environments we live in? We recognize something is wrong when a tiger paces endlessly in a concrete enclosure. Yet when human beings experience restlessness, alienation, depression, or anxiety, we are remarkably quick to diagnose them. “I don’t know who discovered water but I doubt it was a fish,” noted Marshall McLuhan. Maybe it’s the water that surrounds us that’s toxic and not something inside of us?

Remote work, online shopping, food delivery, streaming entertainment, and virtual socializing have become increasingly normal since the pandemic. Many of these things are genuinely convenient and some have improved people's quality of life. But maybe our frictionless lives are not necessarily an upgrade to the human experience? Maybe we thrive when we have little victories like trips to the store, restaurants, gyms and other third spaces where we interact with others? (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

We work on one screen, order necessities through another, socialize through messages, exercise in front of a monitor, and unwind by scrolling through videos of other people living lives that appear to be more interesting and exciting than our own. We can spend days on end interacting with two-dimensional representations of the world without actually being face-to-face with other people. Ever heard of pheromones?

We accumulate hundreds of online acquaintances without experiencing the vulnerability and authenticity that we need and crave. We communicate constantly while remaining profoundly lonely.

Humans evolved to move through complex physical terrains, not sit on couches with laptops on our laps. We need touching, exploring, smelling, creating, cooperating, taking risks, and belonging to communities in three dimensions, not two.

It has been revealed that and being constantly available is not our friend. Neither is excessive optimization nor compulsive productivity. Having no time for friends, family, or contemplation should be seen as failure. We can no longer simply take a walk; we need to track our steps, monitor our heart rates, and demonstrate that we have accomplished something in contrast to “just walking.”. So what if some of what we call individual psychopathology is really a response to collective conditions that frustrate fundamental human needs?

Sources indicate that psychology should examine not only what patients are thinking and feeling but also take into account the influences that are causing people to think and feel the way they do. We should ask questions such as, “Do they have true agency? Are they physically engaged with the world? Do they experience meaningful relationships, belonging, creativity, and contribution? Do they have opportunities to experience spontaneity, wonder, and delight?”.

Perhaps we need to reconsider the conditions under which we expect human beings to live, work, love, and flourish. That means examining not only individual choices but also workplaces, economic incentives, social institutions, and the technologies that increasingly mediate our experience of reality.

Helping people adapt to their circumstances is an important part of psychotherapy but our adaptation to COVID-lockdown reclusiveness may be more akin to zoochosis than we have hitherto believed.
