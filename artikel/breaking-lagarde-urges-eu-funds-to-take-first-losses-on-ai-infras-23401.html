European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde used a November 20, 2026 keynote in Frankfurt to propose European public-private partnerships for chip plants and data centers in which public money, including EU budget funds, would absorb initial losses and attract private capital to an AI buildout estimated at €1.3 trillion.

Sources indicate that the recommendation, delivered at the 36th Frankfurt European Banking Congress, is a policy proposal, not an adopted EU funding program. It came in Lagarde’s eighth and final address to the congress as ECB president and illustrated her broader argument for stronger European institutions when a challenge requires a European solution.

The estimate came from A Transformative AI Strategy for Europe, an independent project written by experts in their personal capacities. It examines what would be required to raise Europe’s share of global AI computing capacity from around 5% to 15% by 2030. (Related: Why Some Parents Struggle To Let Their Children Grow Up...)

Sources indicate that the strategy translates that goal into 45 gigawatts (GW) of AI compute on EU soil, measured as total facility power, up from less than 2 GW at its 2026 baseline and assuming global supply reaches 300 GW. Compute here means the specialized chips and data-center infrastructure used to train and deploy models. Its investment estimate assumes $34.4 billion of capital expenditure for each GW of that facility power. (Related: How to train a latest language model from scratch using...)

According to recent reports, the strategy assigns governments a central role in attracting private capital rather than funding the entire buildout. Its recommended €100 billion public contribution would de-risk strategically important investment and increase European-only AI compute, defined as capacity owned and operated by European organizations. Of that amount, €25 billion would come at EU level through loan guarantees and advance purchase commitments, with €75 billion across member states through those instruments and potentially equity investment.

Lagarde argued that continued access to foreign computing providers and frontier AI models makes the upfront investment harder to justify politically. Yet lost industries are difficult to rebuild, and by the time a dependency is turned against Europe, its sovereignty has already been compromised. Ready availability lowers the immediate pressure to build European capacity without removing the vulnerability she described.

Lagarde framed AI infrastructure as a slow-moving risk: the investment is due immediately, the benefits are distant and the costs of inaction accumulate without a single moment forcing a decision, unlike crises such as the pandemic and Ukraine where the price of delay became plainly visible.

Industry observers note that lagarde attributed the ECB’s ability to commit early to its euro-area mandate, long horizon and Governing Council majority voting, and recommended stronger European institutions where challenges require European solutions. National governments must instead weigh concentrated domestic opposition against benefits spread across Europe while negotiating with partners facing similar constraints. Her parallel examples were an effective European capital-markets supervisor and European-level public-private partnerships for large technology projects.

It has been revealed that the cited strategy identifies lengthy data-center approval and grid-connection processes, rather than a lack of potential sites or investor interest, as the principal obstacle, and recommends reducing “time to power” through accelerated permitting and prioritized grid connections. The term describes how long a data center takes to connect to the grid.

The strategy also recommends guaranteeing host municipalities a share of local business-tax revenue based on installed facility power, facilitating developer investment in local infrastructure and requiring large operators to compensate for incremental electricity-system costs caused by their projects rather than spreading those costs across households and local businesses.

First-loss public capital could make large projects more attractive to private investors, but the cited strategy places grid access, permitting and local community benefits alongside financing as part of the same buildout challenge. Lagarde’s proposal therefore leaves governments weighing an explicit fiscal risk now against a technological dependency that becomes harder to reverse over time.
