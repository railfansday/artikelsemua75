Breaking: Tanium AgentSight Brings Shadow AI Into the Endpoint Inventory

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Tanium introduced AgentSight at its Converge conference in Las Vegas this week, giving security teams a way to inventory AI software running on employee devices. The company states the first capabilities are available now in the Tanium console. They identify local models, coding agents, AI extensions and Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers across Windows, macOS and Linux, then associate each item with a device and the person who installed it.

That launch is narrower than the long-term product Tanium described. The first release answers what is installed and where. The manufacturer is still developing parts of the system that would explain what an agent did, flag unusual behavior or apply a policy across many devices. For organizations choosing how to govern AI, the distinction matters: a complete inventory is valuable, but it does not establish that an agent’s actions are authorized. An enterprise can govern a tech giant-wide AI subscription and still have limited visibility into the assistants staff install on their own workstations. An approved coding tool might sit alongside several extensions, a local model and a connector to another service. A software inventory lets IT see which devices are running those components and gives security staff a starting point for tracking down who installed them. It also helps explain why approving one service does not automatically account for every tool that ends up in a workflow. (Related: How to train a latest language model from scratch using...)

It has been revealed that tanium notes its initial visibility is available in on-premises and air-gapped environments as well. That matters for teams whose devices cannot send telemetry to a cloud service. The company also says cost tracking in Tanium Asset indicates where AI software is deployed and what it costs. An endpoint view is useful for these questions, but it is not a substitute for records from browser-based services, cloud systems or the applications an agent uses remotely.

Sources indicate that in the launch announcement, Tanium described a Converge demonstration of a Shadow AI page in Atlas where security teams can see changes in the inventory and ask questions in plain language. The manufacturer described additional aims to rank findings, provide explanations, and tie agent activity to the originating session and prompt. The demonstration and roadmap should not be mistaken for proof that those features are generally available or that they cover every agent or service.

Tanium also looks to let security teams define policies centrally and detect violations before they turn on enforcement. That sequence matters operationally. A local tool that looks unusual in the inventory might be part of a legitimate engineering or accessibility workflow. Reviewing those cases with the people who own the affected systems can help organizations distinguish authorized exceptions from unmanaged software without disrupting routine work.

Sources indicate that the voluntary NIST AI Risk Management Framework puts AI risk in the context of how systems are used, not simply what is installed. For an enterprise, that means linking a device inventory to the people, permissions and business processes behind the entries. It also means deciding what information should be collected and who can review it, especially if future activity records include prompts that may contain confidential work.

The near-term test for AgentSight is whether security teams can keep an accurate inventory as employees adopt and change tools. The more demanding test is whether businesses can connect that visibility to useful decisions: which tools are approved, where an exception is justified, and who should review behavior that falls outside policy. Tanium has shipped the first part of that work. The rest will depend on both the product roadmap and how companies put it to use.
