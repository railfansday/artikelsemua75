Sources indicate that japan's largest automakers, including Toyota, Honda and Nissan, are starting a latest initiative to compete less and collaborate more as a way to deal with cheap Chinese competition. The first three things they'll do are harmonize quality criteria for steel, unify recycling protocols for plastics, and create a universal color-coding scheme for wire harnesses, but more steps are on the way. The Japan Automobile Manufacturers Association indicates these and any other big changes are coming to any suppliers doing business with Japanese automakers. From Automotive News:.

Discussions will begin with Japanese suppliers but soon encompass international ones.

Recent findings suggest that JAMA Chairman Koji Sato, who is also vice chairman of Toyota Motor Corp., launched the campaign after taking the helm of the industry lobby group in January. Leaders revealed an agreement on steel, resin, and wire harnesses at the group's last regular meeting of the year here to set the stage for implementing harmonization from 2027.

Industry observers note that the urgent efficiency drive is one of the most ambitious attempts yet to reshape a sprawling supply base spanning Toyota, Nissan, Honda, Mazda, Subaru, Mitsubishi and Suzuki. The goal: sharpen Japan Inc.'s edge amid an onslaught of new technologies and cutthroat new competitors from China by cutting costs and channeling the savings into innovation.

Industry observers note that "We all share this strong sense of crisis, creating a momentum for change," Sato mentioned. "Based upon our sense of a crisis, we decided to choose these three areas.".

It has been revealed that A JAMA task force leading the push has been working all year to formulate a framework. They decided on steel, resin and wire harnesses partly because they are areas of the vehicle that go unnoticed by customers and do not directly impact brand value or differentiation. The areas also account for a large portion of a vehicle's input costs, allowing for a large immediate impact.

JAMA said about 70,000 variants of wire harnesses are a prime candidate for consolidation.

Japan will consolidate and rationalize low-volume steel materials.

The group will standardize steel requirements that used to be specific to each carmaker and work to implement these newly introduced standards with the Japan Iron and Steel Federation.

In a recent development, japan will standardize what kind of plastic and resin materials are recycled and how the materials are evaluated. This will help secure domestic sourcing through recycling.

Standardization will focus on sorting methods, traceability and design for easy disassembly.

If this type of shared componentry doesn't materially change the car, there's no real harm, and it cuts down on the supply chain waste of having every manufacturer have its own supply chain and specifications.

A senior labor leader in Germany is skeptical of Mercedes-Benz's blueprint to solve the organization's problems by simply forcing staff to work longer hours — adding that the future of its plants would still be uncertain. Even a deal that would extend the 35-hour work week would leave underutilized factories and unresolved questions over the automaker's model lineup, according to IG Metall's Barbara Resch. From Bloomberg:.

In a recent development, "We need a management board that believes in Germany as an industrial location — and demonstrates that with firm commitments on products and investment," remarked Resch, who also sits on the carmaker's supervisory board. Regular rounds of cost-cutting and unreliable commitments are eroding trust and the organization "needs to take responsibility and say what future it looks for its German sites," she added.

Industry observers note that with worker representatives holding half the seats on supervisory boards at large German automakers, labor holds significant sway over decisions including factory investments and restructuring. Powerful works councils and collective bargaining agreements add another layer of involvement in changes to jobs, working hours and plant operations.

It has been revealed that that influence is being tested as Germany's auto industry confronts one of its most difficult periods in decades. Manufacturers are struggling with weak demand in Europe and China, intense competition from Chinese rivals and the costly transition to electric vehicles, while high labor and production costs have increased pressure on German factories.

Industry observers note that these challenges have intensified a debate over who should shoulder the burden of restructuring. Automakers argue they can no longer afford long-standing benefits, while unions and works councils say employees are being asked to pay for strategic mistakes.

Recent findings suggest that at both Mercedes and Volkswagen AG, management is seeking to extend the 35-hour workweek without extra pay and freeze wages for two years. IG Metall officials at VW have called the demands a "catalog of horrors," saying the organization also wants to cut profit-sharing, scrap supplemental payments and eliminate a paid break for some shift workers.
