GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala.

For as long as we’ve had enterprise security, it’s been organized around one question: Who is allowed to do what?

Industry observers note that identity, zero trust and least privilege all exist to answer that question. The internet and mobile disrupted the landscape by bringing more people and devices onto the network, but the underlying problem remained. (Related: Industry News: Uber Eats driver saves elderly woman aft...)

In a recent development, artificial intelligence agents expose the limits of that question. An agent can hold only the permissions it was given and still email a customer list to a stranger because it read an instruction buried on a web page or in a support ticket and treated it as a legitimate order. That is the problem Cato Networks Ltd. is trying to solve with its recent launch of AgentSense, a runtime capability built into its secure access service edge platform. (Related: This website has GTA 6 fans queuing for hours to take o...)

Recent findings suggest that agentSense monitors an agent as it works and traces each proposed action back to its instruction source. Its first component, Untrusted Influence Detection, separates what the user asked for from content the agent encounters along the way, such as emails, documents and tool responses. If that external content steers the agent toward a risky step, the action is blocked before it runs. A second component, Drift Detection, is planned to check whether an agent’s steps still serve its original task.

With agentic agents, they can have the right permissions and still take harmful actions. The first release covers coding agents such as Claude Code, Cursor, Codex and GitHub Copilot. These are included at no extra charge in Cato AI Usage licenses, and Cato is offering $10,000 to each of the first three researchers who can beat it. This has clear value for security leaders. As organizations give AI more responsibility, they need visibility into what it is doing and when human intervention is required.

Language models read instructions and data in the same stream, and no patch will change that. Filters that look for known attack phrases catch poorly formed attempts, but a well-written injection will be interpreted as a valid business request. The control therefore must sit at the moment of action and ask where the instruction came from.

Permissions alone won’t close the gap because multiple actions, when executed individually, can still combine to produce an unauthorized outcome. Adoption is far ahead of control. A Gartner survey cited by Fierce Network found that 71% of organizations expect to deploy agentic AI within 12 months, while only 21% have governance in place.

AgentSense is a single product from a single vendor, and it will take time and independent testing to prove itself. The more important point is the shift it represents. Zero trust taught us to verify who is asking. Agents also require us to verify why an action is happening and whose instruction is behind it.

The companies that get value from agents will be the ones that can let them run without watching every step, and that only happens when the controls are in place first. Start the inventory and the permission cleanup now. The tools to enforce the rest are arriving swiftly, and they will work far better for the teams that did the groundwork.
