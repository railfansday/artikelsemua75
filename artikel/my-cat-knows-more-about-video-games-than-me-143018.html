It has been revealed that this is my cat, Robo. She is a 12-year-old brown tabby. She knows more about video games than me.

Unlike me, who didn’t really start engaging with video games until I was 9 or 10 or so, Robo has been watching people play video games since she was just two months old, when I first adopted her from the shelter with her sister (named Lucca, of course). We got her in 2014. So whenever she curled up in my lap between bouts of bouncing off the walls of our tiny rental, she was regaled with games like Shovel Knight and Smash Bros. for 3DS. As she got bigger and bigger and spent more time snuggling with me on the couch, at my desk, anywhere in the house, she absorbed games like Undertale, Splatoon, Overwatch, and countless more. She would often paw, gently and ineffectually, at the little characters wiggling around on a screen. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Robo knows more about video games than me in part because she has experienced, at this point, probably more games than I have. Not only has she seen every game I’ve played since 2014, she’s also seen every game played by my ex-husband during the five years we lived together. She watched my ex-husband struggle through Dark Souls 2 and Middle-earth: Shadow of Mordor in her very first year on this earth. While I played World of Warcraft with him, she got to see both our screens as we tried our very first raid, Highmaul. And she’s seen more of Mega Man X than anyone should thanks to his efforts to speedrun the game.

My cat also knows so much about video games because she has been with me for, almost down to the month, the entire duration of my career writing about them. When she was still less than a year old, she saw PokÃ©mon OmegaRuby before most human beings did. She watched me do permanent damage to my wrists reviewing Mario & Luigi: Paper Jam. She was there for the Nintendo Switch launch and sat with me as I played Breath of the Wild a week and a half early, and stalked in and out of our basement demanding attention when we invited friends over for 1-2-Switch. She was there when we celebrated my first full-time job in journalism when I was hired at GamesIndustry.biz in 2018, at IGN in 2021, and at Kotaku earlier this year. When I celebrate, she gets treats too, so she definitely knows good things are happening. (Related: Understanding BigBird's Block Sparse Attention...)

In early 2019, Robo went with me when I got divorced and moved to Kansas City. She snuggled with me while I cried and played Tales of Vesperia and the first chapter of Deltarune. For three years, it was just me, her, and her sister, trapped inside together through 2020 and 2021 playing games upon games. She was there for all five hundred hours that I played Animal Crossing: New Horizons, and she curled up on my yoga mat while I tried to do Ring Fit Adventure. As I began dating my now-husband long-distance, she participated in many of our date nights: yelling at me in the kitchen as we cooked meals together over video chat, and perching on the desk to watch us play Divinity: Original Sin 2 together online.

Sources indicate that she suffered through two three-day car trips with me: one to San Francisco in 2021, and one back to Kansas City in 2024. On the drive to San Francisco, she was probably pretty upset when, just before we got into the car to leave for the final leg of the journey, I fell into hysterical laughter watching the live reveal of Sora being added into Smash Bros. I moved in with and eventually married my husband, and she got familiar with his gaming tastes, including games he’s played early as a reviewer that I had no interest in. Thanks to him, she’s seen Hades 2, Consume Me, Unbeatable, Hyrule Warriors, Pragmata, 007 First Light, and so, so many others. He and I played Kirby and the Forgotten Land together, and Elden Ring side-by-side, pausing to pet whichever cat was in our lap at the time whenever we died, which was often. She watched me lose my mind trying to solve games like Animal Well and Blue Prince, sitting atop my notebook when I left it lying somewhere, almost like she was demanding attention before she’d give it back. Most recently, she’s even gotten to see a little bit of both our playthroughs of GTA VI. I hope she’s pleased with my husband, who stops to pet every in-game cat he sees.

It has been revealed that robo loves to lay on my lap when I work, so she’s not only absorbed hundreds of video games over the years, but she’s also seen countless news articles, essays, and critical pieces on video games. I know we all assume cats can’t read, but if she can, I’d have no way of knowing about it. So maybe the whole time she’s been sitting in my lap, she’s been judging my writing (and my husband’s!) behind those bushels of whiskers she has. She’s been a constant presence in meetings with coworkers at all three major outlets I’ve worked at, appeared on dozens of gaming podcasts to lend her expert voice, and in her own way participated in union activities at IGN. And she’s sat in on most of my interviews with developers, both those telling me glowingly about the wonderful games they’ve made and those sharing some of the hardest moments of their lives with me. Though I’ve always felt bad and apologized when she’s interrupted, people universally respond reassuringly, saying her presence was a joyful thing. I fondly remember Hironobu Sakaguchi grinning and saying, “Kawaii!” under his breath when she walked on screen during an interview several years ago. I remember all the times she comforted me when I was anxious, frustrated, or just plain sad while working on a particularly challenging report. And if you’ve ever written something nasty about me on the internet that I’ve scrolled by at some point, Robo has probably seen it. I like to think she is angry on my behalf.

Recent findings suggest that though she’s always been a bit of mama’s girl, she’s hung out with my husband and surely has read and judged his writing, too. She even participated in his journalism, making an important appearance in a video of various cats reacting to the game Stray. Clearly, she understands the importance of research:.

I heard once that one’s pets, after a time, grow to know you better than anyone else on earth, even yourself. Instinctively, they spend essentially all the time they’re not asleep watching you, trying to understand your movements, habits, and emotions. Robo has proven that time and time again. She once came and determinedly planted herself on my face when I was having a panic attackâlogistically unhelpful, but funny enough to clear my head a bit and help me calm myself. She forced me to get up, morning after morning, to care for her when I was in a deep depression after my divorce. And she curled up in my arms like a stuffed animal, night after night, when I cried at home alone because my now-husband was in the hospital after a stroke. I wonder if, based on her observation of my feelings and reactions to the games I’ve played this year, Robo knows what my Game of the Year is better than I do. I wish I could ask her, but the only thing she ever states to me whether she’s happy, hungry, or pissed, is a horrendous “EAH!” sound. Like a kitten mew, but with the gain turned all the way up.

According to recent reports, we found out a few weeks ago that Robo has lymphoma, and in the last few days her health has declined sharply. So in what I hope she understands, on some level, as an act of love, we’re choosing to say goodbye to her on Tuesday. I know logically that the world will go on after her, a world with video games and my work and my life but no Robo. But right now, I just can’t imagine what that world looks like. How am I supposed to play games when she’s not sitting with me? How can I even write if she’s not stepping all over the dang keys?

With all of the games she’s seen over the years, both played in front of her and streamed on TV or PC, and all of the bits of writing she’s seen, and all the video game discussions she’s been present for, surely Robo must know more about video games than me by now. But she’ll take her secrets and judgments, wise and strange and wonderful as they are, with her into whatever oblivion exists after this, and I’ll stay here and somehow manage without. Exactly what Robo knows about video games, what she thinks of them, I’ll never know. I’ll have to live with not knowing what she thinks of video games or anything, just trusting and hoping that whatever it was she knew, it was good, and warm, and full of all the happiness we tried to give her.

I hope Robo liked video games, and I hope Robo loved her life, and I hope Robo knows how much I have loved, and will always love, her.
