Long before making “52 Blue,” a film about a young man from India who travels to Qatar hoping to meet Lionel Messi during the World Cup, Egyptian filmmaker Ali El Arabi had a similarly ambitious dream of becoming a world champion boxer and meeting Muhammad Ali.

“My dad had a lot of worries and fear from the world. He wanted me to stay in the village,” El Arabi tells Variety at the Doha Film Festival, where the film is having a homecoming of sorts. “I wanted to run away to meet Muhammad Ali.” Related Stories Celebrity Streaming and Commerce Platform TalkShopLive Expands to TikTok Ahead of Black Friday Amazon Debuts $30 Streaming Bundle That Includes Starz, BritBox, AMC+, MGM+ and PBS Masterpiece In One Place.

With his mother’s help, El Arabi left his village and joined the national kickboxing team, winning his father’s approval only after appearing on television. His experience echoes through “52 Blue,” a story about family expectations, loneliness and the freedom to forge one’s own path. (Related: GTA 6 developer pays touching tribute to late mother by...)

The idea emerged while El Arabi was working on a music video for the 2022 FIFA World Cup in Qatar. There, he met Ashish, a worker from Kerala, India, who mistook him for a footballer and asked for a photograph with him to send to his mother. Their encounter inspired the film’s protagonist and drew El Arabi’s attention to the untold stories of workers preparing for the tournament. Recent findings suggest that the film takes its title from 52 Blue, an individual whale whose unusual calls earned it the nickname of the world’s loneliest whale, though it has never been sighted. For El Arabi, its isolation reflects both his childhood and his protagonist’s experiences.

Sources indicate that “This whale is me, and it’s Ashish too,” he claims. “Everyone wants to be part of the world through their own experience. Each of us deserves to have our own experience, on our own terms.”.

El Arabi, whose documentary “Captains of Zaatari” followed two Syrian refugees pursuing their football dreams, drew on that background to blur the boundaries between fiction and documentary. He shot during the actual World Cup, immersing lead actor Yadav Shashidhar among real stadium workers who believed they were participating in a documentary.

“I didn’t tell the workers that we were making a fiction film, give them directions, or give them or Yadav a script [for those scenes],” he explains. “I’m just filming how he’s blending in and melting in with them. I took all of this material to create my scenes.”.

El Arabi filmed one three-hour conversation among workers with four cameras, later shaping their exchanges into one of the film’s most powerful scenes. “Who am I to change the reality of these people?” he ponders. “I wanted to capture that reality and bring it into the film.”.

According to recent reports, filming during the tournament required considerable improvisation as the crew faced limited stadium access and little opportunity for retakes. “We didn’t have a shooting list. We didn’t have much time. We had a maximum of 100 minutes during each match,” he recalls. “We didn’t have the permissions necessary to really be everywhere since FIFA has a lot of rules.”.

For Shashidhar, whose previous credits included smaller roles in Indian films, Ashish’s obsession with Messi was rooted in loneliness. Having lost his brother, his character struggles to connect with his father.

“He was not able to express himself to his father. He wanted him to understand him, but that was not happening,” Shashidhar says. “He was always searching for companionship.”.

It has been revealed that although he was never an avid football fan himself, Shashidhar drew inspiration from the passionate Messi and Ronaldo supporters he knew growing up in Kerala, where enormous cutouts of football stars appear during tournaments. He also turned to an unusual source to understand Ashish’s emotional journey: the animated film “Finding Nemo.”.

Recent findings suggest that “People will die for these players, even if they are not from the same nation,” he claims of football’s global fandom. “That’s one thing which unites them, no matter what community they come from.”.

Recent findings suggest that for El Arabi, Ashish’s quest is about something more fundamental than meeting his idol. “He ran away from his family, from his life to meet Messi,” he says. “But actually he ran away to meet himself and to meet the world.”.

Sources indicate that the filmmaker sees Ashish’s struggle for independence as one he has faced both in sports and in cinema. “It’s not just about families. It’s about anyone who can control us,” he states. “Anyone who feels they can judge us, tell us what we can’t achieve or impose limits on our lives.”.

“My dream is for filmmakers and the younger generation to have a voice without anyone telling them what to say,” he adds.

Recent findings suggest that el Arabi is now completing his next feature, “2-1,” a music-driven drama that marks his first Egyptian film. He revealed the new title exclusively to Variety. The project was previously known as “Watar Wahed,” meaning “One String” in Arabic.

Industry observers note that the film stars Egyptian rapper Wegz, making his feature acting debut, alongside Asser Yassin, Tara Emad and Mayan El Sayed. It follows twin brothers, one deaf and the other a successful rapper. When the musician enters rehab, his brother assumes his identity onstage. Yassin plays the rapper’s manager.

Currently in post-production, the film, produced by Ambient Light and Film Square, is tentatively slated for an Egyptian theatrical release in early 2027.

“It’s about music. It’s about history, too. It’s about love. It’s about a success story,” El Arabi beams. “This is the first Egyptian movie for me. And this is the biggest one, I believe.”.
