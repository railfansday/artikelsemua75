Latest Update: Why is gas suddenly cheaper? Five factors driving the drop to $3.64 a gallon

The average price of gasoline in the U.S. has plunged nearly 90 cents a gallon in two months down to its lowest level since the early days of the Iran war, coming just in time for Thanksgiving travel.

Sources indicate that context, of course, matters. The average price for a gallon of regular unleaded was $3.64 on Monday. Though that’s 57 cents higher than the price a year ago, according to AAA, it has come down precipitously of late. And it would only hace to fall a bit further to $3.58 to hit where it stood on Thanksgiving Day 2022. Over the weekend, in some parts of the country, prices at the pump could be seen hovering at—or occasionally even below—the $3 mark, a huge psychological break for consumers who have suffered through years of rising prices. Industry observers note that so, why have prices fallen so sharply since late September? It’s a combination of geopolitical factors in the Middle East, typical seasonal declines, and refinery operational issues. And, no, there wasn’t any conspiracy to keep prices higher ahead of the midterm elections. However, President Donald Trump’s actions are key contributors.

Here are the five main factors causing fuel prices—especially gasoline—to tumble:.

Despite some ongoing conflict, the combination of U.S. naval escorts, complicated oil tanker transfers, and pipeline bypass routes have allowed the Middle East to return to nearly 90% of its pre-war oil exports, even though prices remain elevated from high tanker contracting and insurance costs. The rising levels of oil exports—despite depleted strategic oil reserves worldwide—have helped the global oil benchmark dip slightly below $90 per barrel as of Monday.

Prior to the elections, Trump openly mentioned he was delaying any major ramp-up in attacks until after the midterms. That warning was keeping oil prices inflated as oil markets anticipated more disruptions to global oil flows in November. While risks remain and no peace deal seems on the near-term horizon, the lack of an escalation has helped prices fall. “The biggest thing now is this looming uncertainty over whether there will be something concrete and long-lasting,” De Haan told Fortune.

From a simple supply-and-demand standpoint, fuel prices typically peak in the summer during the busy driving season and bottom out in the winter when more people are bundled up at home. Although there are brief travel spikes for Thanksgiving and Christmas, driving is typically muted near the end and beginning of each calendar year. Likewise, fuel is cheaper to manufacture during the colder months. Summer-grade gasoline is more expensive because of extra additives required to keep fuel components from evaporating in the heat. Winter-grade fuel is cheaper and less complex.

Industry observers note that globally, nearly 10% of refineries remain offline, largely in the Middle East and Russia because of wars. But, in the U.S., there also were supply disruptions at ExxonMobil’s Joliet and Phillips 66’s Wood River refineries in Illinois. Those temporary outages from weather complications and mechanical failures caused additional price spikes throughout the Midwest. “The refining issues were boosting prices ahead of the election,” De Haan said. “As these issues have been getting resolved, they’ve been pushing prices down, coupled with the seasonality, coupled with oil markets.” Outside of the Middle East and Russia, China also had mothballed some refineries during the Iran war. As China has depleted more of its own reserves, the country has of late ramped up its output, including its fuel exports, adding to global supplies.

Refining complexes aren’t built to operate like light switches for diesel or other petroleum products. When refineries are pushed to run at their maximum utilization levels for diesel, that also means increasing their volumes of gasoline. With diesel demand surging in the fall, the U.S refining industry is on overdrive and raking in record profits, often delaying planned maintenance work until next year. “Refineries are operating at their highest November rate since 1998,” De Haan said. “This may be the highest utilization year for refineries ever. It’s going to be really close.”.

Industry observers note that fuel prices remain elevated, but there’s no hiding how far they’ve fallen. As such, nearly as many Americans as last year proposal to travel for Thanksgiving. AAA projects 72.9 million people will travel on the road this Thanksgiving, only a slight drop from 73.1 million last year.

Recent findings suggest that gasBuddy’s survey data showed that almost two-thirds of Americans plan to hit the road this week. Close to 20% of travelers mentioned they will cut back on other holiday spending to cover the extra fuel costs.

If there are no more major energy disruptions in the Middle East, De Haan said, the national average for gasoline could bottom out as low as $3.35 per gallon over the next few weeks.
