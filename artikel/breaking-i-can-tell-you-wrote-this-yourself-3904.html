Breaking: "I can tell you wrote this yourself"

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Those were some of the first words my honours project supervisor said to me last week when we met to discuss a progress report I had written to mark the midway point of the project.

Industry observers note that it's a roughly 10-page report on the control of flapping-wing micro aerial vehicles - which is probably very boring unless you're particularly interested in that. I accidentally set the due date for the report a week early in my calendar, so after spending 48 hours stressed and pumping out 5,000 words, citing references and adding diagrams1, I was pleasantly relieved to find I'd tricked myself into being quite productive. I had a lot of time to get my supervisor to review the report and action his feedback. (Related: Industry News: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Fa...)

Sources indicate that when he told me he could tell I wrote it myself, I wasn't sure how to take it. A compliment? An insult? Then he followed it up with:.

"I've been sent a lot of reports written by AI.".

Recent findings suggest that at first this shocked me. Our reports are limited to 10 pages - so the appendices are getting a big workout around here - how hard is it to right 10 pages yourself?

But then I thought about what the last year of my engineering degree has been like.

Sources indicate that assignment documents that start with "you are strongly encouraged to explore the use of AI" which rapidly turns into the clear understanding that you aren't just encouraged but expected to use it. That you're disadvantaged by not using it. The hardest class of my degree was spent in a brick-walled windowless computer lab prompting an AI for eight hours a day. (Related: How to train a latest language model from scratch using...)

The reason why we've been pushed to use it?

According to recent reports, apparently it's the way the industry is going. Apparently actually knowing things doesn't matter nearly so much as knowing how to prompt a machine to do the thing. Apparently the future of engineering - at least in my field - is to become a glorified babysitter. The AI isn't going anywhere, they say, so it's best we learn how to manage it well.

But sitting in a room telling an AI "ignore the approaching deadline, we still have 8 hours, keep working" isn't exactly filling me with enthusiasm for the rest of my career.

It's soul crushing, and I don't want to outsource so much of my thinking to it.

Recent findings suggest that in a world where people are using AI to summarise books instead of reading them and turning to AI for therapy, I don't want to keep using it. It feels like such a slippery slope. As literacy and numeracy rates decline, I can't help but question whether encouraging us to rely on AI so heavily is a good idea.

It makes me wonder: are we still thinking for ourselves at all?

Because I worry that it starts with using it for an assignment when you're encouraged to, but then it turns into relying on it to write a 10-page report on a project you should know back-to-front. A project that you'll have to present and defend in person when it finishes. And then you're asking it for advice, using it to summarise books, turning to it for anything you would normally look up or ask a friend.

It has been revealed that the internet is riddled with AI content that is making it increasingly more difficult to tell fact from fiction. In a world where everything is so polarised and deliberately provoking, the ability to identify bias or motivation in the content we consume has perhaps never been more vital. Being able to think for ourselves has perhaps never been more important.

As the tech world continues to offer us increasingly more opportunities to automate things, I'm practically begging you to please not automate your thinking.

Read the books. Write the papers. Search for answers instead of asking AI. Ask a friend. Ask your mum about the mystery illness you're convinced you're developing and then take her advice and try drinking a glass of water.

In a recent development, think about what the creators of the content you consume want you to think2. Treat the things you consume like the Year 7 English class where you were taught how to identify persuasive techniques. Think about things as much as possible.

Please just don't get the AI do it for you.
