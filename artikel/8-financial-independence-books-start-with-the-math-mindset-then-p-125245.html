Before you plan an early exit from work, read two or three of the books behind the financial independence movement, one for the investing math, one for how you think about money, and one on the path you’d take. Borrow them from the library first, and favor authors who invest in low-cost funds over ones selling a shortcut.

In a recent development, A forum thread on FIRE, short for financial independence, retire early, gives you one stranger’s spreadsheet. A book gives you a whole plan and the reasoning behind it, so you can see where it fits your life and where it doesn’t.

According to recent reports, you don’t need all eight. A book on the math reveals how much saving and investing it takes to leave work early, one on the mindset helps you keep at it for years, and one on a single path shows what those years look like. One of each gives you a initiative to test instead of a pile of tips.

Recent findings suggest that judge any FIRE book you find later by how its author invests. A blueprint built on low-cost index funds, which own every stock in a market index, has nothing extra to sell you. One that only works with the author’s course, coaching, or trading system is selling a shortcut. (Related: Porting fairseq wmt19 translation system to transformer...)

The eight below start with the math and the mindset, move on to five different paths, and end with a counterpoint on spending. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

For more on investing itself, a few investing books for beginners go deeper on index funds, investor behavior, and stock picking.

Before you buy any of them, check what your library card covers, since thousands of libraries lend e-books and audiobooks free through apps such as Libby and hoopla. Then test whichever initiative wins you over against your own numbers, starting with your savings rate, before you change anything at work.
