​Let’s be honest about what a 42-point gap actually means.

Sources indicate that ​When you ask security leaders whether their teams are consistently hitting remediation service level agreements (SLAs), 57% say yes. When you ask the practitioners doing the actual remediation work, only 15% agree. That data point comes from my organization’s “State of Pentesting 2026” report, and it deserves more than a footnote. A 42-point gap between leadership perception and practitioner reality is not a rounding error, but a governance failure hiding in plain sight.

​The instinct, when faced with a number like that, is to question one of the two figures. Either leadership is deluded, or practitioners are pessimistic. That framing lets you dismiss the problem rather than confront it. The harder interpretation (and likely the correct one) is that both groups are reporting what they actually see. The gap is the finding.​.

According to recent reports, before deciding which read to trust, consider who lives with the consequences. When a significant AI security incident hits, 78% of security teams absorb the internal blame. Not the executive who approved the dashboard or the leader who cited the 57% figure in the board presentation; the team. The same team telling you the SLAs aren’t being met.

In a recent development, that asymmetry of accountability should inform how you weigh their assessment. The people closest to the exposure, who will be held responsible when it materializes, are telling you something different from what the summary layer is reporting. Standard governance structures are filtering out the hard read and replacing it with something more presentable. That is not a data problem. That is an incentive problem.​. In 2025, our research indicated 64% of organizations felt well-equipped to handle AI security challenges. That figure has dropped to 51%, which is a 13-point decline in a single year. Over the same period, calls for a strategic pause on AI adoption rose 13 points, reaching 61%, while 82% of teams report investing more in AI security than they did a year ago.​.

If you read these facts together and conclude that your team is underperforming, you are missing the point entirely. You are looking at people working harder against a target they can see clearly and that leadership has not defined honestly.

Effort without accurate situational awareness does not compound into readiness. Eventually, it just exhausts the people doing the work.

In a recent development, an organization cannot indefinitely ask practitioners to own the risk, override their read of it and expect them to keep showing up at full capacity. That is not a culture critique. Instead, it’s a basic observation about how attrition works.

​The gap between what leadership reports and what practitioners see is not static. It is a daily friction that practitioners feel acutely, because they are the ones watching the organization act on a cleaner story than the one in front of them. The people with the sharpest read and the most career options will feel that friction first, and they will act on it first.

In a recent development, ​The organizations that close this gap and build their remediation governance around what practitioners actually observe will systematically out-execute the ones managing from the executive summary. Not because they try harder, but because they are solving the actual problem instead of a flattering approximation of it.

​The blind spot was never in your stack. It was in your reporting structure. The 42-point gap is telling you exactly where to look.

To close this gap from the ground up, security practitioners can start with four concrete steps:.

1. Audit your metrics. Don’t rely solely on automated dashboard rollups. Track actual time-to-remediate against operational realities to surface hidden friction points to management.

Sources indicate that 2. Standardize reporting. Establish a formal process for flagging SLA discrepancies early, documenting systemic blockers (e.g., lack of context, tool noise) alongside the raw vulnerability count.

3. Establish realistic AI risk baselines. Require explicit operational definitions for “AI readiness” within your team rather than accepting vague executive benchmarks.

4. Push for shared risk ownership. Ensure remediation SLAs are tied to business-unit resource commitments rather than letting security practitioners absorb sole accountability for unmitigated risk.

When security teams and executive leadership share the same ground-level truth, risk governance stops being a flattering story and becomes a real operational advantage.​.
