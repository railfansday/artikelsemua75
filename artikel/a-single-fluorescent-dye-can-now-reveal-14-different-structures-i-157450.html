A Single Fluorescent Dye Can Now Reveal 14 Different Structures Inside Living Cells

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Researchers at Peking University have developed a fluorescent dye that can distinguish at least 14 membrane-bound structures in living cells using subtle differences in their chemical environments.

It has been revealed that watching the inner workings of living cells has long been difficult because most fluorescent dyes highlight only one cellular structure at a time. Now, researchers at Peking University have developed a dye called JONR that can distinguish at least 14 different structures using a single stain.

In a recent development, the dye changes color depending on the chemical environment surrounding it, allowing scientists to identify cellular structures without labeling each one separately. In laboratory tests, JONR identified organelles with three times the accuracy of Nile red, a fluorescent dye that has been used for more than a century. JONR is based on Nile red, which glows differently depending on its surroundings. It produces greener light in oily environments and redder light in more polar ones. However, Nile red’s limited color changes make it difficult to tell similar cell membranes apart.

It has been revealed that by modifying the dye’s molecular structure, the researchers increased its sensitivity to subtle differences between membranes. JONR also produces very little light in water, reducing unwanted background signals and making cellular structures easier to observe.

According to recent reports, using JONR, scientists were able to track interactions between structures inside living cells. They observed moving compartments called late endosomes pulling thin tubes of the endoplasmic reticulum, a network involved in processing proteins and other cellular activities.

It has been revealed that the dye also revealed differences in how tightly molecules are packed within membranes. With specialized microscopy, researchers could examine both membrane composition and molecular organization using the same stain. Experiments involving nerve cells highlighted that JONR could detect how molecules are arranged along nerve fibers. Additional tests in migrating cells revealed differences between the membranes of structures involved in cell movement.

Sources indicate that the dye revealed no significant toxicity in cultured cells, but the research has not yet demonstrated its performance in living animals or humans. Some measurements also require specialized microscopes.

Although further testing is needed, JONR could allow researchers to study several cellular structures and their interactions without relying on multiple fluorescent dyes.

Reference: “Far-red-emitting Nile red with boosted solvatochromism enables multidimensional imaging of membrane heterogeneity and dynamics” by Mingqiao Liu, Yunzhe Fu, Xiaopeng Li, Haolin Zhang, Yumiao Ma, Meiqi Li, Chunyan Shan, Hongxi Zheng, Xiaoyi Lu, Yan Zhang, Li Yu, Peng Xi and Zhixing Chen, 23 February 2026, The Innovation. DOI: 10.1016/j.xinn.2026.101319.
