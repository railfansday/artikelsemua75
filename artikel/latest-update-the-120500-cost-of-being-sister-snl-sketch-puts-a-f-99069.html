Latest Update: The $120,500 cost of being ‘Sister’: SNL sketch puts a face on America’s caregiving crisis

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that on the Nov. 14 episode of Saturday Night Live, host John Mulaney starred in a parody drug ad for “Sister,” “a live-in, 24/7 elder care provider that is completely free and also your sister.”.

The sketch spread across social media within days, largely because so many viewers saw their own families in it. Its jokes land on real pressure points in American caregiving: who does the work, what it costs them, and how much more of it is coming.

A 2025 report from AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving found that 63 million Americans, nearly 1 in 4 adults, had provided ongoing care in the past year to an adult or child with a medical condition or disability. That is 45% more caregivers than a decade earlier.

The work has also gotten harder. More than 40% of caregivers provide high-intensity care, and more than half perform medical and nursing tasks such as giving injections and managing catheters. Only 22% have been trained to do them. (Related: Leveraging Pre-trained Language Model Checkpoints for E...)

In a recent development, an aging population is the main driver, notes Geoff Gross, founder and CEO of Medical Guardian, a Philadelphia-based medical alert and connected-care manufacturer. But strains on the broader care system are pushing more Americans into the role. (Related: The Reformer - Pushing the limits of language modeling...)

According to recent reports, “What we’re also seeing is pressures on in-home care and labor overall,” Gross noted. “There’s a burden on caregivers right now that is not scalable.”.

Paid help is scarce and expensive. As gerontologists Jane Tavares and Marc Cohen wrote for Fortune, a home health aide runs a median of about $6,688 a month, while the direct care workforce remains underpaid and short-staffed.

The sketch opens with Mulaney’s character weighing a nursing home against a private aide who “could cost thousands a week.” For real families, the costs keep compounding long after the caregiving ends.

According to recent reports, A report published Nov. 4 by the AARP Public Policy Institute modeled the lifetime income losses of working caregivers born between 1981 and 1985. The typical caregiver in that group stands to lose about $120,500 in earnings, workplace retirement income, and Social Security, including roughly $15,400 in retirement and Social Security income alone.

Across that single five-year birth cohort, the losses add up to an estimated $2.3 trillion. Only 3.4% of working caregivers in the model lost no work hours to care. Chandra Bozelko, who cares full-time for her parents, ran those numbers on her own family in a recent Fortune commentary.

Sources indicate that for some, the strain reaches the basics. In the 2025 AARP survey, 1 in 5 caregivers mentioned they couldn’t afford necessities such as food.

The sketch’s sharpest line comes from a doctor, played by Mikey Day, who explains that “years of internalized sexism warp Sister’s brain into thinking caretaking is her responsibility and somehow not yours.”.

Research from the University of Michigan published in October suggests the gap behind the joke is widening. If current rates hold, U.S. women can expect to spend seven years of their lives caring for older family members or friends, up from six in 2011. Men’s lifetime total held steady at four.

The difference isn’t in caring for spouses, where men and women spend about the same time. It shows up in care for parents and grandparents. Edward Jones managing partner Penny Pennington has called it “the elder daughter phenomenon,” arguing that eldest daughters are quietly propping up family finances.

“Women still bear disproportionate financial burden, time burden, and energy burden,” Gross said, adding that any progress on the issue “isn’t happening fast enough.”.

According to recent reports, demand for care will only grow. The U.S. Census Bureau projects that in 2029, Americans 65 and older will outnumber those under 18.

“Caregiving is really a societal crisis, but it isn’t designated in that way as much as it should be compared to other things we read about each and every day,” Gross mentioned.
