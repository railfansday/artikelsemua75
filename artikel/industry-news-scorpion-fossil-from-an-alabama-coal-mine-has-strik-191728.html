A tiny scorpion fossil from Alabama reveals that modern scorpion features existed more than 300 million years ago.

According to recent reports, the world before the dinosaurs is remembered for its oversized millipedes, scorpions and dragonfly relatives. A small scorpion pulled from an old Alabama coal mine looks a lot like the ones alive today.

The fossil appears to be the oldest known North American scorpion with breathing openings arranged like those of living scorpions.

A comparably early example, complete enough to compare, had previously been reported only from the British Midlands.

The study was led by Genevieve Conway, an honours student in paleontology at Flinders University in Australia. Russell Bicknell of Flinders served as senior author.

With just one incomplete specimen to work from, the authors did not assign it to a known species.

“Well before dinosaurs roamed the planet, the scorpions, millipedes and dragonfly relatives of 315-320 million years ago were generally much larger than they are today,” Conway mentioned.

“That is why we suspect this fossil could be a juvenile, and one of the first of its kind to be examined and connected to the evolution of modern scorpions on the North American continent.”. (Related: Scaling up BERT-like model Inference on modern CPU - P...)

Its home rock is the Pottsville Formation, laid down in humid tropics about 10 degrees south of the equator.

According to recent reports, that places it in the Carboniferous Period, the coal-forming age. The beds at this locality are roughly 316 million to 319 million years old.

Recent findings suggest that union Chapel Mine, in Walker County, is now called the Steven C. Minkin Paleozoic Footprint Site. Scientists and volunteers helped save the old pit from being filled in after mining ended.

Two slabs were involved. One half of the split rock turned up on April 23, 2022, and the other a week later.

In a recent development, both were found lying on the surface by T. Prescott Atkinson of The University of Alabama Museums, who gave them to the museum.

Recent findings suggest that almost everything collected at Union Chapel comes out of spoil heaps. Exactly which layer held this scorpion is unknown.

It has been revealed that no fossil scorpion had ever been reported from the southeastern United States. The slabs were photographed at the American Museum of Natural History in Upcoming York.

Recent findings suggest that living scorpions famously glow under ultraviolet light too. Here, though, the orange comes from coal-like material that formed as the shell was altered by heat.

It has been revealed that three plates showed something else under the lamp: tiny dark dots, each one rimmed in brighter orange. Conway’s team reads them as breathing holes.

In today’s scorpions such openings sit mid-plate on rectangular belly segments. In some ancient groups they are thought to have opened at the rear margin.

Where they fall on the Alabama fossil is what links it to the modern group.

Paired, curving lines on the same plates may be traces of book lungs. Air enters these organs through the holes.

Good evidence of book lungs is scarce, and all of it comes from older animals. Later fossils from that era have yielded none.

Preservation or simple oversight might explain the gap, according to the authors.

In a recent development, the whole fossil is about 0.9 inch (22 millimeters) long. A quarter is a bit wider.

Recent findings suggest that its claws reach forward and the body bends slightly. To paleontologists those are telltale features of a molt, not a carcass.

That is the usual story; most fossil scorpions are molts.

Scorpions shed repeatedly as juveniles, which is why the team thinks this individual was young.

Shedding may also have destroyed the plates around the leg bases. Unfortunately, those are the plates used to sort fossils into families.

Chemically, the shell is now nearly pure carbon, the product of millions of years of heating.

Recent findings suggest that sulfur and calcium turned up in small amounts. Most likely they leached in from the shale around it.

The tail’s last segment, the one that connects to the stinger, is noticeably longer than those before it.

Most scorpions from the Silurian through the Carboniferous had tail segments of about equal length. The longer final segment shows up later and is still present today.

Sources indicate that longer segments go with longer, faster muscles, the authors wrote. That may have allowed the scorpion to sting faster.

Slim pincers paired with a thick tail complete the picture. In modern scorpions that build points to animals that rely on venom for hunting and defense.

They lean less on strong pincers to subdue prey.

“Remarkably, this species appears very similar to the characteristics of scorpions alive today, including those in Australia,” Conway stated.

“In fact, it probably looked really similar to Lychas, a species commonly seen by bushwalkers in Australia.”.

The team proposes that the lengthened segment evolved under pressure from predators such as early relatives of reptiles and mammals.

Those animals were diversifying at the time and were common around the site.

Before this discovery, the scorpions of Union Chapel were known only through trackways that researchers attributed to them. The body fossil lends support to that view.

“It’s the first fossil scorpion from this understudied site where lots of trackways have been found, including other fossils, by amateur fossickers and researchers,” Bicknell said.

Industry observers note that the fit is imperfect, though. Trackways at the site span about 0.6 to 0.9 inch (15 to 22 millimeters) in width.

The fossil’s body, by contrast, measures only about 0.16 inch (4 millimeters) across.

Leg spread could not be estimated, so the team cannot say whether an animal this size made any tracks.

Trails of varying width may have been left by scorpions of several sizes. That assumes scorpions made them at all.

Scorpions are the oldest arachnids in the fossil record, dating back at least 435 million years. At least one early species was a giant.

Some 170 extinct species have been described so far, against 2,918 that are living today.

In a recent development, many fossil species rest on a single specimen, occasionally little more than a pincer. Others proved to be one animal described under several names.

Industry observers note that the Alabama scorpion adds a body to that record but not a name. Its side eyes and the plates beside its legs, which would indicate a family, were not preserved.

It does differ from the only other scorpion known from the Pottsville rocks. That species, from Indiana, has belly plates with lobed edges.

Bicknell remarked more discoveries will be made as fossils are shared with museum collections.

A second specimen with an intact underside could show which kind of scorpion lived here over 300 million years ago.

The full study was published in the journal The Science of Nature.
