In Saturday Night Live’s “Sister” sketch, the family’s eldercare solution is The Sister (aka The Daughter). In this skit, Sister drops everything going on in her life to care for Dad. What struck me was one of the quick screens that flashed, describing why women feel compelled to act in this way: "years of internalized sexism," they say, have made caregiving feel like her responsibility.

As I watched this clip (over and over again, trying to understand how it could be both funny and triggering simultaneously), my eyes zoomed in on this particular screen. There are icons like a television, flag, music notes, religious symbols, and a book surrounding the female caregiver.

Recent findings suggest that to me, these indicators are like a recipe for how daughterhood is taught. It's not one single song, policy, television show, book, or religious text that makes a daughter feel like the default support person in a family. These are messages that accumulate over a woman's lifetime. The particulars of the influencing cultural messages differ across families and communities; and, of course, each influence trigger can also provide counter-messaging. But repeated, prolonged, non-stop ideas about who is naturally nurturing, who should sacrifice, and who is responsible for family care can begin to feel like common sense. It's like Stockholm syndrome in that it seeps into your consciousness that this treatment and behavior is natural and normal and you're the one who needs to do it. Sources indicate that daughterhood doesn't just start up one day when a parent becomes ill. It is built through years of everyday interactions with our cultural influences at play. For example, a young girl (eldest sister) was praised for being responsible, asked to remember a relative’s birthday, encouraged to help a younger sibling, or expected to notice when a parent is worried. High-level cognitions and emotions that are baked into our childhood tend to stay with us. These acts can be loving and ordinary. Either the family sees this as helping or they think it's part of her personality (if they notice her care provisions at all). Over time, though, repetition teaches daughter what others expect from her within the family system (and what she can expect for her own trajectory). According to recent reports, A 2021 review of research on unpaid family care describes socialization as role learning shaped by family, culture, modeling, and normalization. In many of the studies reviewed, daughters and daughters-in-law internalized caregiving responsibility in ways connected to gender, birth order, and marriage (McAllum et al., 2021). That reveals that women feel like they're supposed to be doing the daughtering and care work. The process of internalizing this expectation is not a single lesson. It is a decades-long apprenticeship: first watching, then helping, then becoming the person others rely on.

When a daughter becomes so smooth at anticipating needs, her skill and ability in this role can become obscured. She's so good at care work that the history and training that produced her competence become hidden. Relatives may say she is “just better at this,” as if a daughter's skill at anticipating and noticing are just instinct instead of practice. The more a woman does well for her family, the easier it becomes for others to step back. And because care includes so much planning, emotional attunement, and follow-through, the daughter is working and laboring and giving long before anyone calls it caregiving.

Industry observers note that in my book, Good Daughtering, I talk about the many facets of daughtering. It's the unpaid, often invisible work of holding a family together: checking in, stepping up, and smoothing over. Eldercare can bring that work into sharp focus, but it doesn't create a need for daughtering out of thin air. By the time a crisis arrives, a daughter already understands herself as the one who notices, remembers, and responds. She's been doing it for years by this point. The crisis only makes it visible to others.

That is why the instant activation of the SNL "Sister" character matters. While the text from her brother is sudden, the role of daughtering is not. She drops everything and gets to work. Of course, she feels like refusing makes her selfish, even when the family has never asked who else could take responsibility. Guilt can make a learned expectation feel like a personal obligation. And that's our pain point. Women must learn to sit in the tense feelings of guilt while claiming recognition for our labor.

The point is not to blame daughters for internalizing the messages around them, or to assume every family follows the same script. The massive point here is to ask how this "Sister" or "Daughter" social script became so familiar. And we also need to ask: who in the family benefits when one person carries the heaviest parts of the overall load?

It has been revealed that if daughterhood is built over time, it can also be renegotiated over time. Families can make the work of caring become visible, question the assumptions that just one specific daughter will step in and do it all, and definitely teach sons and other relatives to notice, initiative, and care without waiting to be assigned.

Industry observers note that the SNL "Sister" sketch is funny because it compresses a very common, yearslong story into a quick two minutes. But let's not forget the bigger, more difficult question that we should be asking much earlier in life: Why does everyone think it's the daughter who will be the one to go?
