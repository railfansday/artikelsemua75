Vanessa Piña has been writing as a journalist for over two years. As a senior writer, she focuses on timely coverage of the latest anime releases and popular comic franchises.

When it comes to works that explore the differences between humans and robots, the first name that comes to mind is likely Isaac Asimov, one of the world's most acclaimed science fiction authors. While many of Asimov’s stories have been adapted for the screen, such as Apple TV's Foundation series, Netflix’s catalog also features a single-season masterpiece that draws significant inspiration from his writing and brilliantly incorporates the principles that he proposed in his book I, Robot. The first principle of the "Three Laws of Robotics," devised by Isaac Asimov, states that a robot must not injure human beings. In I, Robot, Asimov uses these rules, which are embedded in the robots as basic instincts, to craft a compelling mystery centered on the loopholes that could lead these machines to cause harm. The 2023 Netflix anime series Pluto also centers its narrative on this premise, presenting a future society where technology has advanced to the point where AI robots have become fully integrated into human life until one of them is linked to a series of murders. Recent findings suggest that pluto is a reinterpretation by Naoki Urasawa, creator of Monster, of the "The Greatest Robot on Earth" story arc from Osamu Tezuka's Astro Boy. Darker and more introspective than Astro Boy, rather than a typical murder mystery focused on identifying a culprit or a human versus robot war, Pluto explores the blurred lines between humanity and artificial intelligence, much like Isaac Asimov's works. Beyond these parallels to Asimov's work, Pluto also draws heavily from the philosophical themes of Ghost in the Shell, one of the major cyberpunk franchises in history.

Industry observers note that in Pluto, robots enjoy rights similar to those of human beings: they have jobs, get married, and can take vacations. There are even advanced models that are indistinguishable from humans at first glance. The series then follows special investigator Gesicht, who discovers that both robots and humans are being targeted by a killer who leaves behind the same signature mark. As the investigation unfolds, the murders reveal a far-reaching scheme targeting the seven most advanced robots in the world.

Industry observers note that this coexistence between humans and robots, the dangers of AI spiraling out of control, and even details like robots being capable of dreaming strongly resemble works by Isaac Asimov, such as Robot Dreams. Pluto also uses these machines to propose profound philosophical questions, such as what truly defines humanity, a theme it also shares with Ghost in the Shell. Another thing they have in common is that both stories take place in a futuristic setting, within a society that treats individuals' memories and visual information as digital data.

According to recent reports, while in Ghost in the Shell, cybernetically enhanced individuals challenge humanity's boundaries, Pluto delves into whether machines programmed to mimic feelings can develop a "true heart" to the point of experiencing negative emotions such as grief and hatred. Furthermore, both Pluto and works like Ghost in the Shell: Stand Alone Complex peel back the layers of a larger conspiracy, digging into military conflicts, political intrigue, and moral dilemmas with a similar mature tone. Yet Pluto is mostly a slow-paced thriller, introducing the lives of various robots while inviting the viewer to reflect on what it is that makes life.

In a recent development, what's fascinating about the sci-fi genre is not always seeing creative future technologies but rather observing how humanity itself is shaped in response to it. In Pluto, for instance, robots are simultaneously treated as humans (mourned and respected) and viewed as potential weapons of mass destruction. That way, the series focuses on depicting trauma, the consequences of war, and discrimination from the perspective of these robots, adding a deep psychological layer that presents contradictions and truly embodies the philosophy behind robotics advancements.

According to recent reports, on the other hand, longtime fans of the sci-fi genre will notice that characters from Astro Boy, such as Atom and Dr. Tenma, appear in Pluto, accompanied by numerous references that those familiar with the classic anime will surely recognize. Even so, since these figures serve as secondary characters in Pluto, the series remains fully enjoyable even for those unfamiliar with the original story or the anime industry in general. That's because Pluto is a masterpiece that excels in every respect, from its visual quality, action sequences, and narrative to its well-developed characters, having established itself as one of the best short anime series of the past decade.

Recent findings suggest that across its eight episodes, each approximately an hour long, Pluto gradually unfolds its core mystery, offering a highly satisfying resolution as its various subplots converge. Thus, while it may feel heavy at times (as is often the case with slow-burn crime series), Pluto constitutes an ideal choice for binge-watching, as it is difficult to look away once it begins to unravel its story, being a thought-provoking work that will undoubtedly stir viewers' emotions just like the writings of Isaac Asimov or the universe of Ghost in the Shell.
