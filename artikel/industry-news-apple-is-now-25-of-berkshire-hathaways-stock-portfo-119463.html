It has been revealed that berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -0.47%) (BRKB -0.36%), under the direction of Warren Buffett, began buying Apple (AAPL -1.90%) shares in the first quarter of 2016. That investment has worked out extremely well. At one point, the Cupertino tech giant accounted for 50% of the conglomerate's portfolio.

Even after trimming the position from fourth-quarter 2023 through the end of 2025, as of Nov. 17, the ”Magnificent Seven” stock represented 25% of Berkshire Hathaway's total public equity portfolio, and it is still its largest holding. The number of shares remained unchanged this year. This was despite there being a lot of shuffling among other positions.

Here's what Berkshire holding on to the Apple stake tells investors.

Recent findings suggest that over the three-month period from the start of July to the end of September, Apple shares traded at an average price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of 36.8. Any rational investor would call this an expensive stock. That's doubly true for those who see equities through a value-focused lens. The share price has moved 29% higher in 2026.

According to recent reports, berkshire Hathaway didn't sell a single share during the third quarter. This might come as a surprise to those who have followed the diversified corporation's actions in recent years.

Warren Buffett oversaw the selling of Apple shares over multiple quarters from the end of 2023 to the end of 2025. For example, during the second quarter of 2024, when the technology stock traded at an average P/E multiple of 29, much lower than what it sells for today, Berkshire Hathaway cut its position dramatically.

While the single holding had become a significant part of the portfolio, it's fair to assume that valuation played a part in these decisions. Now, with a P/E ratio that's approaching 40, it looks like less emphasis is being placed on the valuation being an influential factor.

NASDAQ · As of 4:00 PM ET 11/20/26.

Greg Abel has been the chief executive officer of Berkshire Hathaway since the start of 2026. He has made notable moves in his short tenure thus far, but as outside observers, we have zero clue what he and Warren Buffett are discussing regarding the conglomerate's capital allocation strategy. All we can do is try to come up with some takeaways.

Staying true to Warren Buffett's investing philosophy, the Apple position is a clear signal that Berkshire Hathaway will hold on to its winners and let them run. "When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever," Buffett wrote in his 1988 shareholder letter.

According to recent reports, the iPhone maker has been in the portfolio for nearly 11 years. Given how much of the market is driven by short-term behavior these days, that time horizon seems like an eternity.

Sources indicate that if Berkshire Hathaway did sell some of its Apple shares in Q3, it wouldn't know where to move that capital. This is a persistent problem. Compared to the hundreds of billions of dollars on the balance sheet that earn low yields, an expensive Apple stock still offers the potential for higher returns. This benefits Berkshire Hathaway shareholders.

Valuation should always be viewed separately from the underlying business. In this instance, Berkshire Hathaway's inactivity is a vote of confidence in the quality of Apple's operations.

Sources indicate that it's hard to dispute that the consumer tech juggernaut is one of the finest companies we've ever seen. A valuable brand, ongoing pricing power, customer loyalty, and innovative DNA are attractive traits. There's also its incredible financial performance, shown by a fiscal 2026 free cash flow margin of 28%.

Sources indicate that this isn't a sign for the average investor to go out and buy Apple stock. It is proof, however, that buying great businesses and leaving them alone can work wonders for your portfolio.
