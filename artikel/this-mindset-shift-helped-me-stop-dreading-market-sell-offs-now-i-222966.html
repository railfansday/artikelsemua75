Sources indicate that big stock market sell-offs like the one we're going through right now aren't fun or easy. It's natural to feel worried or afraid for the future. But in a strange way, I'm in a pretty good mood this week. That's because, over my years of investing through bear markets and economic crises, I've learned a upcoming mindset for stock market sell-offs.

Recent findings suggest that I want to share my one-sentence mindset shift that helps me stay calm and confident during stock market crashes. Maybe it will work for you, too.

This mindset sounds simple, but I have repeated it to myself many times over the years when the stock market is tanking: "You don't need the money today.".

Industry observers note that here's what I mean by this. I am a long-term investor. The money that I've put into the stock market is not money that I need today. It's not my emergency fund. It's not my vacation fund. It's not money that I need to pay bills this month or the next. It's long-term money. "Leave it alone for 20 years" money.

Because of this long-term perspective, I can (most of the time) stay calm when the stock market is crashing. I'm not trying to get rich quick by flipping stocks for a fast profit. I'm trying to buy and hold a diversified portfolio of stocks, including low-cost index funds, that will let me build wealth gradually over the next few decades.

In a recent development, I don't need the stock market to make me rich today. I hope that the stock market will make me richer over the next 20 years.

It has been revealed that but just saying "I'm a long-term investor" isn't enough to build wealth. The stock market must deliver significant gains. Fortunately, that tends to happen. As part of that long-term perspective, I'm a student of stock market history.

In a recent development, the S&P 500 index has delivered 9.98% annualized returns since 1928. Think of all the stressful times, economic crises, and global catastrophes that have happened during those 98 years. That 9.98% average annual return happened despite the Great Depression. It includes World War II, and the 1970s stagflation, and the 2000 dot-com bust, and the 2008 global financial crisis.

Industry observers note that even with all the booms and busts along the way, the S&P 500 has been a strong investment for long-term shareholders. In the past 16 years since it was launched in Sept. 2010, the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO +1.28%) has delivered average annual returns of about 14.8%. VOO has delivered about 12% annualized returns in the past five years (as of Oct. 31).
