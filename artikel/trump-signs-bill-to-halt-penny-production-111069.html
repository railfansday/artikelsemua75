The penny is officially history under a bill President Donald Trump signed into law Friday that codifies the end of production of the one-cent piece.

The copper-plated coin is mostly composed of zinc and costs far more to produce than it's worth, prompting members of Congress at least since 1989 to consider killing it as an easy cost-saving measure for the federal government. (Related: Tech Report: Score a huge 49% saving on a 2TB Samsung 9...)

“Washington has a spending problem, and it demonstrates up in places massive and small,” the bill's sponsor, Rep. Lisa McClain, R-Mich., remarked Friday in a statement released by the office of House Financial Services Chairman French Hill, R-Ark. “If the federal government is spending nearly four cents to make a penny worth one cent, something is broken.”. (Related: AI data center power demand will double by 2030 thanks ...)

Hill mentioned in the statement that the bill also provides "clear legislative guidance for retailers, financial institutions, and consumers," referring to the measure's requirements that cash transactions not settled with exact change will be rounded to the nearest nickel.

It has been revealed that the U.S. Mint stopped producing pennies a year ago under an executive order. Pennies already in circulation will still be legal tender, and the Mint may still matter special collectors editions. The legislation, named the Common Cents Act, also sets composition specifications for nickels.

Like a well-traveled coin, the bill itself took a winding road to the White House. The House originally passed a similar bill in July and the Senate passed its version by unanimous consent on Aug. 7.

Recent findings suggest that but the Senate added a section that would require the Treasury secretary to notify Congress before discontinuing other coins. That sent the bill back to the House with a Senate number.

As the sponsor of the original measure, McClain insisted that the legislation have a House bill number. So, she reintroduced the revised Senate version, and the House voted on it on Sept. 14. The Senate passed it once again by unanimous consent on Sept. 28, sending it to the president's desk.

Senate Banking Chairman Tim Scott, R-S.C., noted the bill “modernizes our currency system and most importantly gives families and businesses clear rules for everyday cash transactions.”.
