House Flipping Profit Starts With Your Offer – Cap It With the 70% Rule

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that before you bid on a house to flip, cap your offer at 70% of its expected sale price after repairs, minus the repair budget. The other 30% has to cover the loan, the bills while you own it, and the cost of selling, so every dollar you pay above the cap comes out of your profit.

In the second quarter of 2026, the typical flipped home sold for $60,526 more than the flipper paid, before renovation or any other cost, property data firm ATTOM reports. The market sets the sale price, so most flips are won or lost at the purchase.

Recent findings suggest that the 70% rule caps that purchase price. Start with the after-repair value, what the house should sell for once it’s fixed, based on recent sales of similar updated homes nearby. Multiply it by 70%, then subtract a detailed repair estimate with 10% to 20% added for surprises. That’s your highest offer, not your target. The loan, the holding costs while you own the house, and the selling costs come out of the other 30%. Private lenders’ short-term loans to real estate investors, often called hard money loans, charged a median 9.99% interest rate in October 2026, plus up-front fees of 1.75% of the loan, the National Private Lenders Association reports. Holding costs are property taxes, insurance, and utilities, and selling costs are agent commissions and closing costs.

In a recent development, say a house should sell for $300,000 after a $40,000 renovation, with $35,000 in loan, holding, and selling costs. Seventy percent of $300,000 is $210,000, so subtracting the renovation caps your offer at $170,000. At that price, $55,000 is left before income tax. Pay $230,000 instead, and you’d lose at least $5,000, since a bigger loan costs more interest.

Sources indicate that sell within a year of buying, and the IRS taxes the profit as a short-term capital gain, at the same rates as your wages. Once flipping becomes your business, the profit is business income instead, which also owes self-employment tax for Social Security and Medicare.

Recent findings suggest that the typical flip in ATTOM’s report took 161 days from purchase to resale. A slow permit or a late contractor adds weeks or months of interest and holding costs, and a cooling market can cut the sale price.

Get an agent’s list of recent sales and a contractor’s written estimate before you bid, then write your cap down. Walk away from any house whose seller won’t come down to it.
