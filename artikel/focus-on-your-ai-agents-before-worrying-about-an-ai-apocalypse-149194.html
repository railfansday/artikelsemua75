Focus on Your AI Agents Before Worrying About an AI Apocalypse

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that A business can make progress with agentic AI and lose control of it at the same time. Such is the concern of AI agent sprawl, or the uncontrolled spread of agentic tools throughout an organization.

The conversation around AI risk often gravitates towards extreme scenarios. Anthropic’s IPO prospectus offers some fairly alarming examples, from resisting shutdown and blackmailing human customers to “existential risks to humanity.”. While these questions warrant serious consideration, organizations don’t need to imagine a rogue system bringing operations to a halt to understand the risks of poorly managed AI. The challenge businesses face, while more grounded, is far more immediate. It is ensuring that the agents they’re already deploying remain secure, useful, and accountable. (Related: Tech Report: Porting fairseq wmt19 translation system t...)

AI agents are becoming easier to develop and deploy by the day, and it demonstrates. By 2030, agentic AI is on track to account for two-fifths of all AI value, by which point, 42% of companies will have to grant agents autonomy, indicates BCG.

Between readily available off-the-shelf tools and increasingly accessible development platforms, it’s become easy for individual employees and teams to automate tasks without waiting for their IT department to build them a bespoke solution.

This shouldn’t be taken to undermine the value of agentic AI’s accessibility. It encourages innovation, creates the opportunity to improve entrenched processes, and enables those closest to a problem to experiment with ways of solving it.

It has been revealed that the difficulty is that these same conditions make the proliferation of AI agents almost inevitable. Agents can be created faster than an organization can establish how they should be monitored, maintained, and governed. Gartner predicts that the average Fortune 500 enterprise could have more than 150,000 AI agents by 2028, compared with fewer than 15 in 2025.

It has been revealed that A large agent inventory isn’t automatically a problem. What is an issue is whether an organization has sight of which agents exist, the data they can access, the permissions they have, and – importantly – who is responsible for the decisions those agents make.

Without that visibility, a business can expose itself to risk while believing it’s simply becoming more productive.

While some tools will prove useful and become embedded in everyday work, others might be abandoned or quietly forgotten. This creates a security risk. If organizations lose track of which agents remain active and what they can access, those tools can easily become entry points into sensitive systems and data.

A similar problem applies to information handling. An agent might process or share confidential material without sufficient human oversight of where that information goes or how it’s used. The more fragmented the environment, the harder it becomes for businesses to keep on top of these risks.

Industry observers note that accountability is another challenge. Agents make mistakes; they might apply the wrong policy or take inappropriate actions. But when things go wrong, identifying the responsible party shouldn’t depend on detective work. Businesses need to know who owns the agents, the rules they operate under, and who is expected to act when things go awry.

These aren’t problems that can be solved by asking employees to be more careful. They require a clear view of the systems operating across the organization, the permissions they have, the teams responsible for them, and the authority to manage them consistently.

Tackling agent sprawl doesn’t mean discouraging employees from building agents altogether. What businesses need is to change the environment in which their agents are created and managed. A move away from disconnected tools with their own assumptions and standards, and towards one governed environment that pulls together the shared knowledge, standards, and controls required to build fully informed agents.

According to recent reports, picture it as a sort of super-agent: in essence, one single agent with all the context it needs, from organizational expertise to organization policies and processes. It’s this agent that creates and governs all the other project-specific agents – like a Queen Bee in charge of her hive.

Recent findings suggest that this sort of governed environment ensures AI agents remain manageable, without stripping staff of the freedom to experiment and innovate. It gives businesses clear visibility of which agents exist, the purpose they serve, the information they can access, and whether they remain necessary, while ensuring they operate within the boundaries of established standards.

According to recent reports, and alongside effective oversight, businesses need to establish clear ownership. Not only should the purpose of an agent be clear, but so should the person or team accountable for it, ensuring human judgment and expertise remain embedded in each decision and process.

In a recent development, as agents move from individual experiments into business-critical workflows, the consequences of getting things wrong, while not existential, become more significant. What this means for businesses is ensuring governance doesn’t remain static. The controls around these systems need to evolve in lockstep with AI agents themselves.

Sources indicate that we are only beginning to see what agentic AI can make possible for businesses, but realising that potential will take more than deploying more agents. Shared context, consistent standards, and clear ownership will help businesses distinguish what works from what doesn’t. Businesses will be able to scale successful approaches and, critically, keep humans at the helm where judgment and experience matter most.

In a recent development, ultimately, AI maturity will be measured by how well organizations manage their AI agents. The real advantage sits with the businesses that understand what each agent does, why they do it, and who is accountable for the outcome.
