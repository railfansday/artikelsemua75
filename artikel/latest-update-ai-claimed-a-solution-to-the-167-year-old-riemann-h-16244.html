In a recent development, it’s a major week for Levent Alpöge: The mathematician saw his employer, Anthropic, become the first publicly-traded frontier AI lab. He also appears to have solved one of the most key problems in mathematics.

“The precise timing is horrible,” he wrote in an essay announcing his findings and describing how he messaged Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei last Sunday night to ask how to announce that he had found a Lean-compilable solution to the Riemann Hypothesis, which dates back to 1859 and is among the Millennium Prize math problems. Industry observers note that the Hypothesis conjectures that there is a hidden structure underlying the appearance of prime numbers. If the claimed result holds true, we’re entering sublime-mysteries-of-the-universe territory. (Related: Industry News: Judge blocks construction of Trump’s arc...)

Maybe the best way to understand how exciting this is to the mathematics community is to watch the experts puzzling through the result in a chat on Zulip. Mathematicians like Chris Birbeck, a number theorist at the University of East Anglia, are posting thoughts like this ….

In a recent development, the cohomology bound is proved by what looks like an automorphy-lifting argument. It genuinely uses the existence of Galois representations attached to regular algebraic, polarized cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n over CM fields, along with base change, Jacquet–Shalika, Clozel’s purity, Bernstein–Zelevinsky, the main theorem of CM, Deligne–Ribet and more.

For my part, I tapped out of mathematics at college calculus, so I can’t help decode exactly what that means.

But that comment was followed by twenty readers reacting 🤯.

According to recent reports, it will be a while before the mathematics community feels like it has a handle on the proof, but most appear confident that the formalized Lean result will hold. Lean is an an open-source language that essentially helps verify mathematical proofs.

Alpöge appeared in his essay to feel a bit guilty about releasing the proof without a truly human-intelligible explanation, but his obsession with the problem since childhood made him feel he “could not live” if he waited to release the finding.

Alpöge described a “stream-of-consciousness jaunt through the experience of finding all the pieces of the argument through incredible confusion with Claude” with more than a hint of AI euphoria, describing how the model “flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”.

However, for all that action to mean anything, we’ll have to wait for humans to be able to understand the result and explain it to each other, something Alpöge has committed to pursue.

Alpöge and a collaborator, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, also released another crucial proof related to the Navier-Stokes equations, problems that helped kick off the race between frontier labs and mathematicians to solve outstanding problems this summer.

It has been revealed that meanwhile, Ravi Vakil, a Stanford professor and the president of the American Mathematics Association, posted a proof of the General Riemann Hypothesis written by Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo, noting that “it is all human, with no AI. The hope was to bring Alessandro to Stanford for a year to polish the writing and then announce it after running it by enough people. Now it seems best to put out the current draft.”.

In a brief introduction his proof, which he noted is the result of twelve years of work, Masullo wrote that “if any message at all this work is meant to send to the mathematician who feels discouraged by the pace and tone of the current climate, it is this: we are still here. We are very much alive. We are not going anywhere.”.
