10 Thriller Books That Hooked Me From the Opening Chapter

At any given moment, Scott Simone has at least three books jockeying for his attention. He’ll never turn down a campus novel with a literary bent, nor will he hesitate to add yet another mystery to his Leaning Tower of Pisa-inspired to-be-read pile — which is built upon the bedrocks of too many short story collections, how-are-these-real true crime books, and classics waiting to be cracked open again. Topics that have him ripping through pages the fastest include closed-circle murders, unhappy professors, life-questioning philosophical deep dives, so long as they feature sharp sentences, a dark sense of humor, and at least a little malice.

Some thriller writers seem terrified you'll put their book down, so they throw everything at you: a murder on page one and an abduction on page three, all while a massive conspiracy begins to unravel. And that’s all before introducing who’s involved. Eventful, sure, but there's something almost desperate about all that hoopla. It’s as if the writer's afraid one quiet page will send readers straight back to their phones. (Related: Introducing the Open Arabic LLM Leaderboard...)

According to recent reports, the best thriller writers have a little more faith in their stories. They're in no great hurry to show their hand, allowing the reader to become acquainted with a character worth following. Of course, there's nothing wrong with opening on a murder or throwing somebody into immediate danger, but the excitement alone can only take a story so far. What matters is that the writer gives us time to find our footing, to become familiar with the world we're entering and curious about what lies beyond it. By the end of that first chapter, we might have little idea where the story is headed, but we're more than happy to go along for the ride.

In a recent development, A woman burning her father’s corpse in the garden is a shocking way to start a novel, but Liz Nugent somehow makes it relatable in the opening pages of Strange Sally Diamond. Sally's father constantly joked that she should put him out with the trash when he died, so she does. Yet the way she recounts it, with the practicality of someone listing yet another household chore, you understand it. It's an absurd explanation, but Nugent delivers it so matter-of-factly that you begin to appreciate Sally's reasoning.

That's what makes Nugent's introduction so effective: everyone else sees a 42-year-old woman who's done something appalling. The police have questions and turn Sally into a suspect. The neighbors are horrified and turn Sally into a monster. Sally is confused by all the hubbub. The reader is at an advantage here, having spent some time in Sally’s mind and following her peculiar train of thought, understanding her in a way nobody else does. The twists and the shock that follow only deepen that early compassion for a character that, on the surface, could be easily written off as psychotic.

When you’ve got revolvers and shotguns pointed at your head, it usually spells endgame. Lee Child flips that script when introducing Jack Reacher in the opening of Killing Floor. Reacher is simply eating breakfast as four police officers descend on a Georgia diner. But he doesn’t fly off into action, doesn’t protest or attempt to escape: he quietly finishes his eggs and tucks five dollars under his plate. Reacher, in fact, is so composed that the police, all that firepower be damned, begin to look like the nervous ones.

According to recent reports, it's a clever way to introduce a man who'll eventually become one of fiction's most formidable tough guys. Child could have staged some spectacular show of force, but instead he lets Reacher's observations do the work: he sizes up the officers and considers their tactics. And just when we're getting comfortable with the idea that Reacher is an innocent man caught in an unfortunate situation, Child drops a revelation: he hasn't killed anybody in this town. He hasn’t killed anyone in some time now. It’s the exclamation point to the chapter, a way of announcing the police may have arrested the wrong man, but he’s not a harmless one.

Sébastien Japrisot begins A Trap for Cinderella in just about the most non-thriller way possible: lulling the reader into a fairy tale. Mi and Do grew up together. The former is the ideal of the pair: beautiful and wealthy, the symbol of adoration for everyone around her. The latter is intelligent but poor, always getting the short end of the stick. But then Japrisot reveals that Do has invented this little fairy tale herself. She resents Mi, so she’s conveniently cast herself as Cinderella. It’s presented in such a way that we’ve no idea how much of what we've read is true.

Sources indicate that that quiet beginning is a trap in itself as the novel takes a dark turn, with a fire at a villa on the French Riviera. The blaze leaves one woman dead and another so badly burned that she loses her memory. The survivor doesn't know whether she's Mi or Do, and the fire may have been deliberate, meaning she could be either the victim of an attempted murder or the person who planned it. Japrisot has already shown how easily a story can be twisted by the person telling it, and then allows his protagonist to reconstruct her entire life from what other people tell her.

In a recent development, jean-Patrick Manchette’s opening to Fatale is a bit of a red herring. Almost the entire first chapter follows a man named Roucart on a hunting trip. We become overly familiar with his protruding belly and his deep regret at giving up smoking. It's the sort of attention you'd expect a writer to give someone we'll be following for the next few hundred pages. So when an attractive young woman appears in the woods, we assume she's wandered into Roucart's story. But she takes a shotgun off her shoulder and shoots him dead.

Ultimately, it’s clever the way Manchette makes us almost as susceptible to this woman as Roucart. He was so enthralled by her appearance that he didn’t for a second recognize the danger. We, too, dismiss the danger, sliding past the fact that she just committed a cold-blooded murder because we're so intrigued by her composure, so curious about who she is. She arrives in a provincial town where the wealthy residents have plenty of dirty little secrets: they're a vain, greedy, thoroughly unpleasant lot, and watching her exploit their weaknesses becomes something of a pleasure. Before long, we're almost rooting for a woman we know is capable of murder.

Industry observers note that many a thriller makes revenge look easy, the only logical next step in the grieving process. It can look a lot different when you’re staring it square in the eye. That's the predicament Kōtarō Isaka gives a grieving father in the opening of Bullet Train. Yuichi Kimura boards a Japanese high-speed train intent on killing the teenager who pushed his six-year-old son off a rooftop. He's got the boy's seat number, a gun fitted with a silencer, and every reason to want him dead, but not the confidence. So when he sees his target, an innocent-looking schoolboy, he hesitates. The boy doesn't.

And that's how Isaka introduces one of the novel's central ideas: Having a perfectly good reason to do something doesn't mean you're remotely able to pull it off. It's bad enough that Kimura's been outsmarted by a teenager, but worse, the boy finds his humiliation entertaining. And Isaka has plenty more fun at the expense of his characters as the train fills with professional killers, all convinced they know what they're doing. They're a dangerous bunch, sure, but for all their cleverness and carefully laid plans, they spend an awful lot of time making fools of themselves.

In a recent development, jo Nesbø warps a symbol of winter wonderland into one of holiday horror in the opening pages of The Snowman. Sara Kvinesland meets her lover at his house, leaving her young son waiting in the car. She's nervous about someone catching them, so when her lover notices a face staring at them through the bedroom window, they both panic. But it's only a snowman, its pebble eyes fixed on the house. Except, snowmen don't build themselves — somebody went to the trouble of putting it right outside that window.

Nesbø lets that thought linger as Sara returns to her car, where her son is trembling behind locked doors, seeing something that's scared him half to death. Sara's got other things on her mind: she's worried about her affair and keeping up appearances. Her son can barely get her attention, even when he tells her they're going to die. And that's where Nesbø really gets under your skin: the boy knows something's wrong, but Sara, the one person who really should be listening, is too distracted to notice. There's no murder and no grisly discovery; just a snowman standing outside a house, and already we’re hooked.

Thirteen Hours opens at 5:36 a.m. with a woman running for her life. We don’t know why, or who’s chasing her, and we don’t find out. Deon Meyer leaves her, exhausted and terrified, desperate to escape the men who seem to want her dead. Meyer cuts to Detective Benny Griessel, who's being dragged out of bed by a phone call about a body outside a church. And the man’s got problems of his own: an estranged wife, another woman he slept with recently, and a 156-day desperate attempt to stay sober.

Industry observers note that it's almost infuriating how casually Meyer abandons the woman, but that frustration only makes us more desperate to get back to her. Griessel, of course, hasn't the faintest idea she exists, and Meyer takes his time introducing the detective, giving us a man whose considerable professional experience is at odds with the mess he's made of his personal life. Each time Meyer takes us away from the woman, we're left worrying about what's happening to her while we're gone. And all the while, the clock keeps ticking, whether we're watching the woman running or not.

It has been revealed that an ex-con in a stolen car isn't exactly the person you'd entrust with an 11-year-old girl's safety, but Jordan Harper has us begging her to climb inside in the opening of She Rides Shotgun. You see, a prison gang has ordered the murder of Nate McClusky and his family, and he's racing to get his daughter Polly before the killers do. But Polly doesn't know: all she sees outside her school is a hulking man covered in tattoos and scars, a father she barely remembers and who's supposed to be behind bars.

Recent findings suggest that harper puts us in an uncomfortable position, knowing that the man Polly fears might be the only person capable of protecting her. And he plays with that contradiction throughout their uneasy reunion. Polly is painfully shy and bullied at school, carrying a teddy bear for comfort. Nate looks like he could frighten just about anybody. But as they're driving away, a police car passes, and Polly catches something on her father's face she'd never expected to see: fear. Now this intimidating man doesn't seem so invincible, and that's what makes the opening so compelling.

It's a familiar enough scene: a mother worried about a son who's out past his curfew. But Gillian McAllister takes that moment of parental anxiety to the extreme in the opening of Wrong Place Wrong Time. Jen is waiting up for her 18-year-old son, Todd, watching from the window of their Liverpool home as he finally comes down the street. She's relieved to see him, until a stranger approaches. Todd pulls a knife and stabs the man three times, and she hasn't the faintest idea why.

It's a horrible thing for a parent to witness, made worse by the realization that the child you've spent 18 years raising might be someone you don't know at all. McAllister gives Jen a chance to go back and find out. We watch as the clocks move backward through the rest of the novel, allowing her to revisit the days leading up to the murder. She knows who wielded the knife but not what drove him to it. This literal trip down memory lane allows her to scrutinize the years she's spent raising him, revisiting conversations and decisions she'd long since forgotten.

Recent findings suggest that it's easy to imagine what you'd do if someone broke into your house: grab the children, call the police, find something to defend yourself with. But Tracy Sierra makes those plans laughably optimistic in the opening of Nightwatching, in which a mother hears footsteps on the stairs. She entertains just about every possibility until a stranger appears at the top of the stairs, so tall he nearly hits his head on the ceiling. Even then, she struggles to accept what's happening.

Sources indicate that sierra makes us endure every second of that hesitation, making the mental cycle of decisions seem visceral. She thinks of screaming, but doesn’t want the intruder to know she’s there. She thinks about moving, but doesn’t want to wake the children. All the while, her mind also keeps latching onto the most random of details, from the man's dry sneakers to the light falling across the landing. There's something convincing about the way she thinks, grasping at anything that might help her understand the situation while struggling to do something about it.
