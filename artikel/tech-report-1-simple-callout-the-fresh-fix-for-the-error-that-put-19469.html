In a recent development, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) issued latest safety guidance on Thursday, urging airline pilots to verbally identify runways before crossing them, addressing a seemingly minor cockpit oversight that nearly led to a catastrophic collision at John F. Kennedy International Airport (JFK). The recommendation adds a safeguard against pilots inadvertently entering active runways.

Sources indicate that the change follows a January 2023 incident in which an American Airlines Boeing 777-200ER crossed directly into the path of a departing Delta Air Lines 737-900ER. The Delta pilots rejected takeoff at approximately 105 knots (121 mph; 195 km/h), narrowly avoiding a collision involving aircraft carrying 308 people. Under FAA Information for Operators 26016, pilots should announce the runway number shown on airport signs before crossing it. The recommendation applies to Part 121 commercial airlines, Part 135 charter operators, and Part 91K fractional operators. It is voluntary guidance, rather than a legally binding requirement, and allows exceptions where equivalent automated aural advisory systems are installed. Industry observers note that the procedure is straightforward. A crew cleared to cross Runway 31L should visually check the approaching runway sign and announce its number. If the sign instead identifies Runway 4L, the discrepancy could reveal a navigational error before the aircraft enters an active runway. The FAA believes this additional check could reduce mistakes caused by distractions and multitasking during taxi operations.

Sources indicate that the recommendation directly addresses findings from the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) investigation into the JFK incident. Investigators concluded that checking runway signs aloud would provide an extra opportunity to detect an incorrect turn, complementing existing procedures requiring crews to visually check for approaching aircraft.

The FAA explained the value of this additional safeguard.

"This practice serves as a double-check mechanism, confirming intended actions and preventing misunderstandings regarding clearances.".

On January 13, 2023, American flight 106, operated by a 777-200 (N754AN), was taxiing at JFK for a flight to London Heathrow Airport (LHR). Meanwhile, Delta flight 1943, operated by a 737-900ER (N914DU), was preparing to depart for Santo Domingo. American's crew had been instructed to cross Runway 31L via Taxiway K, but missed the required turn, continuing toward Runway 4L instead.

According to recent reports, at approximately 8:44 PM, the American aircraft turned onto Taxiway J as Delta began accelerating down Runway 4L. JFK's Airport Surface Detection Equipment, Model X (ASDE-X), detected the conflict and warned controllers. The local controller immediately canceled Delta's takeoff clearance. The following timeline, reconstructed from the NTSB's investigation, demonstrates how swiftly events unfolded:.

American misses the turn onto Taxiway K.

American turns onto Taxiway J; Delta begins takeoff.

American reaches the runway holding line.

ASDE-X alerts the control tower.

Controller cancels Delta's takeoff clearance.

Delta initiates rejected takeoff.

Delta reaches approximately 121 mph (195 km/h).

Delta stops short of the intersection.

The Delta aircraft eventually stopped approximately 500 feet (152 meters) short of the intersection, having reached its maximum speed just 2,300 feet (700 meters) from where American was crossing. Neither aircraft sustained damage, and all 308 occupants escaped injury. The NTSB concluded that the controller's intervention, prompted by ASDE-X, likely prevented a catastrophic accident reminiscent of the Tenerife Airport Disaster.

Recent findings suggest that the investigation's most revealing finding was that the American crew had performed their normal crossing checks. According to interviews, the captain declared "cleared to cross" and "clear left," while the first officer responded "clear right." However, neither apparently verified the runway number against airport signage. Investigators found that the signs should have been visible for approximately 10 seconds. The cockpit voice recording had been overwritten, so the precise exchanges could not be independently verified.

It has been revealed that investigators attributed the error to distractions, multitasking, and a loss of situational awareness. The crew was dealing with delayed weight-and-balance paperwork, while the captain followed a familiar but incorrect taxi route. Runway entrance lights also activated too late to prevent the incursion. Although ASDE-X successfully alerted controllers, the NTSB also called for technology that could warn pilots directly about approaching aircraft and runway conflicts.

Sources indicate that the JFK near-collision was not an isolated warning about runway safety. Less than a month later, Southwest Airlines and FedEx aircraft narrowly avoided a collision at Austin, demonstrating the importance of multiple safeguards. The FAA's latest recommendation cannot eliminate every runway incursion, but it addresses a particularly preventable mistake: pilots checking that a runway is clear without first confirming it is the correct one.
