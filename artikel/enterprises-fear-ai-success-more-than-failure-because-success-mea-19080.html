Enterprises fear AI success more than failure, because success means lock-in they can’t escape

The supercomputing world built its reputation on scientific simulation, but the artificial intelligence infrastructure now filling the same data centers raises a harder question. When models turn data into revenue, control over that data and the intelligence it produces becomes a board-level concern few companies discuss publicly.

A new buyer’s guide sets out to measure that control provider by provider. Sovereignty is not a single checkbox but a stack of control layers that buyers have to examine one at a time, according to Amit Eyal Govrin (pictured), lead analyst for sovereign AI at theCUBE Research and chief executive officer of Agentcy Labs. (Related: How Much Does a Will Cost? Complete Price Guide...)

“When it comes to sovereignty, it’s all about control,” Govrin said. “And when we have to look at control, there’s a lot of layers to go into it. And the first one is, are you in fact comfortable … ceding that control to somebody else? Are you comfortable being in somebody else’s jurisdiction where they can potentially compel your data if ever required?”. (Related: Leading House Democrat targets Trump’s inner circle for...)

It has been revealed that govrin spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how sovereignty pillars, the U.S. CLOUD Act and incoming European regulation are reshaping the way enterprises choose AI infrastructure. (* Disclosure below.).

According to recent reports, the guide rests on five pillars — territorial, operational, technological, legal and financial. Territory, meaning where data centers sit and where data is hosted, is the pillar most providers get right easily, Govrin explained. The operational layer is murkier, and he pointed to Amazon Web Services Inc. as one example while noting that AWS does run a sovereign region in Brandenburg, Germany, covered by EU citizens.

Recent findings suggest that “You’re in the AWS Frankfurt region as a German customer. Perfectly fine. Are you sovereign because you’re in that region?” Govrin remarked. “If that region goes down, that PagerDuty may not necessarily go to somebody out of Germany. It may go somewhere else. It may go into Seattle or to Hyderabad for all you know.”.

To build the guide, Govrin and his team graded seven hyperscalers across 40 offerings and handed out 117 grades in five regions. The scoring framework uses 17 yes-or-no tests, with legal assessed separately because local laws make it a poor fit for pass or fail, drawing on more than 300 sources and hundreds of buyer interviews. Google LLC’s air-gapped offerings scored relatively well, but the finding that surprised Govrin most applied to every provider.

Industry observers note that “No hyperscaler across the board had an answer to the U.S. CLOUD Act,” Govrin mentioned. “Now imagine you’re a European entity or anywhere else outside the world, and now there’s a production order from a U.S. court system that states that this hyperscaler needs to turn over your data. At that point, there is no contest. Your data becomes Uncle Sam’s data.”.

Furrier compared the stakes with the General Data Protection Regulation, a privacy rule he viewed as less operationally relevant. Sovereignty is more consequential because AI models running where data sits produce intelligence that drives revenue, according to Furrier.

It has been revealed that “When money’s on the table, you know what happens,” Furrier said. “Everyone fights for it or argues for it. So I think this is a tinderbox moment where one match could blow everything up.”.
