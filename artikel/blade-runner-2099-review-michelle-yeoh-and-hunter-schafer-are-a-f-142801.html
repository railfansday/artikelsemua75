From the brain-breaking perspective, what began as a novella (Philip K. Dick’s “Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?”) became a blockbuster flop (1982’s “Blade Runner“) before it was reclaimed by director Ridley Scott and canonized by cinephiles — so much so that Warner Bros. shelled out to make a blockbuster sequel (2017’s “Blade Runner 2049”) that, surprise surprise, also flopped (while still winning Roger Deakins his long-deserved Oscar and teeing up Denis Villeneuve for greater sci-fi success on Arrakis). It has been revealed that moreover, the “Blade Runner” films are worshipped for their indelible, inspirational visions; for the dystopian world Scott so vividly realized and Villeneuve so smoothly expanded; for their scores, designs, and cinematography, as well as the big ideas they support. Whether it’s 2019 or 2049, audiences are invited to sit and stare with awe and wonder as they ponder the nature of humanity — like visiting a museum from the future filled with moving, existential art.

These are films, not movies, and now you’re telling me, the next “Blade Runner” is going to be displayed in 20-minute chunks broken up by ads for what has to be a fake John Madden biopic and what I truly wish were fictitious gambling apps? Talk about dystopian!

According to recent reports, but setting aside the Amazon of it all, TV could also be viewed as the franchise’s natural endpoint — or at least its fated next frontier. Scott’s original inspired countless filmmakers whose artful sci-fi epics are indebted to his work, in addition to the handful of unofficial spinoffs from former collaborators and the man himself. But “Blade Runner” also spawned an array of books, comics, anime, RPG, and video games, proving its elasticity across mediums. Modern Hollywood loves shifting I.P. between the big and small screen, and at the time “Blade Runner 2099” was given the green light, TV was still peaking: Streamers were still looking for the next “Game of Thrones” instead of the next “Grey’s Anatomy.” Amazon still thought “The Lord of the Rings” and “Citadel” could be the next global blockbusters, and budgets for other epics were still eye-popping (aka appropriate). If a “Blade Runner” series could capture the aura of its cinematic predecessors (or at least, ahem, replicate it), audiences would have even more time to spend in its awesome world. Maybe even its universe.

Industry observers note that well, the question of whether Amazon dreams of more electric sheep has already been settled, as the “limited series” label networks tend to avoid these days has been slapped all over the Prime Original Series’ marketing materials. Despite it all (plus being unceremoniously dumped the day before Thanksgiving), these eight episodes are worthy of a longer rollout, and in a different era, a longer runway. “Blade Runner 2099” suffers from a few expected (and unexpected) hiccups, like comparably inelegant direction and homage-heavy characterizations, but Silka Luisa’s sequel series nevertheless constructs a gorgeous, inviting space to sit and ponder the fraying edges of humanity’s souls. Or how lucky we are to see Michelle Yeoh look this fucking cool again.

Sources indicate that to its immense credit, it does both without Harrison Ford’s O.G. Blade Runner (and possible Replicant), Rick Deckard, or even Ryan Gosling’s self-aware Replicant, K. Neither appear in the series (although there is an Easter egg or two buried in the set design), but fans should appreciate how “2099” builds from their legacies. The original story is set in the aftermath of the war K was warned would happen if he didn’t “retire” the child conceived by a Replicant. He didn’t, it did, and now Replicants run the world while humans carry out the menial labor they once forced upon their creations.

In a recent development, no one explicitly references Replicants’ ability to reproduce as society’s tipping point (the opening intertitle simply states the Nexus models took advantage of “chaos” on Earth), but they don’t have to; Replicants have moved on (exploring other worlds, rehabilitating Earth’s environment, reshaping society in their not-so-different image) and people just want to survive. Yet the same innate question that sparked K’s investigation — the question that’s driven the franchise from the start — still gnaws at our titular runners: What makes us human?

Cora (Hunter Schafer) has been on the run her whole life. As a child, she and her brother Elias (Lewis Gribben) crashed on their way off-world and were taken in by a mysterious woman named Helena (Sheila Atim), but now Cora has to care for herself and her ailing sibling alone — and in secret. They live under a false identity, hunted by Blade Runners (Replicants, when they took over the police force, reclaimed the name of their hunters), and when the Blade Runners come for her at work, Cora turns to her friend in government, Bebeto (Dimitri Abold), for a newly introduced identity.

According to recent reports, too bad the only one he’s got requires Cora to pose as a Blade Runner, putting the already terrified fugitive smack in the belly of the beast. As a condition for the newly introduced name, Bebeto also asks Cora for a tiny favor: Track down a confiscated item that could help humans reclaim control over their Replicant overlords. Cora is more of the lone wolf type, but she has no choice. She has to help. Maybe she’ll even see the value in Bebeto’s mission, but “Blade Runner 2099” isn’t “Andor”; it’s not a resistance-driven allegory, in part because Replicants aren’t the Empire; they aren’t the enemy. They’re just like us, and neither side is eager to acknowledge their similarities.

“2099” cleverly forces the issue by forcing Cora, a human, to convince her reluctant newly introduced partner, Olwen (Michelle Yeoh) that she’s a Replicant. It’s a matter of survival, first and foremost, and lucky for Cora, Olwen is preoccupied with finding her former partner, Marek (Johnny Harris), before she “retires” in eight days. But collaboration breeds companionship, and wouldn’t you know it, Olwen’s missing fellow Blade Runner and Cora’s power-flipping Macguffin are connected. Soon, the human pretending to be a Replicant and the Replicant facing a very human fate are zipping around the Southwestern United States in their flying police cruiser, straining to see each other before they’re both lost in time, like tears in rain.

While not an AI allegory (Replicants are not machines), “Blade Runner 2099” does wrestle with the fears evoked in the age of rampant tech overreach: being expendable, replaceable, and obsolete. Cora doesn’t have time for anything but survival, and Olwen is running out of time to decide if she’s lived her one life to the fullest.

Comparisons to a futuristic “True Detective” are as inescapable as they are apt, given the two franchises share neo-noir and philosophical origins. Some viewers may miss Marty Hart’s sense of humor, but that was never “Blade Runner’s” bag — Cora and Olwen are both closer to Rust Cohle’s bleak wavelength, and it’s the case, as much as each other, that exposes them to fresh perspectives on life, death, and who deserves to have a say in either.

Industry observers note that it takes a few episodes for those latest perspectives to pop in, which help the viewer absorb how a flipped human/Replicant dynamic changes Luisa’s dare-I-say hopeful vision for the franchise. Scott’s grimy, crowded aesthetic remains, as does his teeming city (“2099” employs an impressive number of extras), but it’s important that our new Replicant-controlled world doesn’t look any worse than the one humans let go to waste. Suffocating heat and hypnotic sunlight drench the once-rainy streets of Los Angeles. It can feel cold, as swelling ocean tides lift giant water treatment buoys connected to long black tubes that stretch to shore. But they’re rehabilitating the planet, so maybe Earth is a little better off even if humans decidedly aren’t.

Recent findings suggest that oh, but what humans we meet. Proprietors selling manufactured body parts for Replicants who want to experience aspects of humanity they weren’t offered at birth. Traitorous human hoarders who think their riches make them special enough to betray their fellow man, sans consequence. Innocent little girls given over to a system designed to strip away their empathy. Crazed cult members so obsessed with their divine purpose that they don’t realize they’re only talking to themselves. “Blade Runner 2099” comes roaring to life when Cora and Olwen encounter the unexpected stranger, and in these moments, it’s easy to see how Luisa could craft a thrilling episodic series simply by expanding and expounding on the franchise’s proverbial sandbox.

The core story, however, is less consistently constructive. Our Blade Runners’ central investigation is too familiar, especially compared to the side quests along the way, and it unfolds a little too slowly for the villain to assert themself. Our heroes only fare slightly better. Cora is all anxiety, all the time, and Schafer struggles in smaller moments when she has to shed the constant fear of being found out to impart deeper meaning. Oscar-winner Yeoh, on the other hand, excels in nuanced glances, gestures, and pauses, doling them out with precision as Olwen’s stoic, don’t-give-a-shit facade cracks to reveal the roiling feelings she’s trying to suppress.

In a recent development, and yet “Blade Runner 2099” feels spiky in ways resembling its predecessors. Production designers Chloe Arbiture and Paul Inglis (who also worked on “2049”) create immaculate, tactile, and memorable settings, D.P. Rob Hardy (“Devs”) finds the eerie beauty and terror in all of them, and Leopold Ross’ jazzy score lends proper punctuation to each scene, be it a subtle echo or a blaring saxophone. You want to spend more time in and around this Los Angeles just to see what peculiarities you can find and what ideas they’ll rouse.

“Blade Runner 2099” may not be as exquisite as its predecessors (and it won’t likely win over audiences who aren’t already fans), but it nails the franchise’s aura — and builds one awesome world.

“Blade Runner 2099” premieres Wednesday, November 25 on Amazon Prime Video. All eight episodes will be released at once.
