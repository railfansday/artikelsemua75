Male bumblebees usually leave the nest, so what happens when they stay?

Lab experiments found that male bumblebees use their body heat to warm developing young, a task usually associated with queens and workers.

In a bumblebee nest, nearly all of the work has long been credited to the females.

In a recent development, the queen founds the colony and her daughters raise the young. Males are expected to leave soon after reaching adulthood, mate and then die.

A latest study of lab-reared common eastern bumblebees has now found males helping with the young. The developing brood was warmed by the males with their own body heat.

Queens housed with males laid eggs sooner. After 28 days, nests with five males contained about 49 eggs, larvae and pupae on average, compared with 33 in nests with a queen alone.

Recent findings suggest that the experiments were run by Meghan Moore and Hollis Woodard at the University of California, Riverside. Both are entomologists. Their nests were not natural ones. (Related: Large Language Models: A New Moore's Law?...)

Queens were housed with as many as five males and no workers, a mix rarely seen in the wild.

Sources indicate that the project began with photos taken for a different experiment. Moore had pointed a thermal camera at the nests. Bright white patches of heat demonstrated up on the males. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Bumblebees raise their eggs, larvae and pupae together in a single clump. Biologists call these young the brood. A queen keeps the brood warm much as a hen warms her eggs.

Recent findings suggest that she straddles the clump and presses her abdomen against it. The heat is produced by muscles in her thorax, the middle part of her body.

This posture was first described in 1972 for another bumblebee species.

On each of 12 days, every nest was photographed four times within one hour. In total, 2,304 usable thermal images were collected.

The images were labeled so that scorers could not tell which kind of nest each one showed.

Males in the queen’s posture turned out to be hot. Their thoraxes averaged 94.6°F (34.8°C). Males standing on the brood without that posture averaged about 88°F (31°C).

Industry observers note that queens in the same posture averaged about 99°F (37.2°C). The males were not simply resting on a warm spot. Heat was being made in their bodies the way a queen makes it, only less of it.

Heat generated in the thorax is passed back to the abdomen. That rear section is the part a bee presses against the brood. Here, too, queens and males behaved differently.

According to recent reports, in incubating queens, the abdomen was about 2.9°F (1.6°C) cooler than the thorax. In incubating males, the abdomen was 6.7°F (3.7°C) cooler. Their gap was more than twice as wide.

Sources indicate that A possible reason was offered by the authors, who described it as speculation. Heat is known to damage sperm, including in males of another bumblebee species.

By limiting the heat that reaches his abdomen, a male may protect his sperm.

No sperm were examined in this study. For now the idea remains untested.

Moore and Woodard began the nest experiment with 75 mated queens. Each queen had been treated with carbon dioxide. This let her skip winter dormancy and begin laying eggs.

Some queens were given a nest box to themselves. Others shared a box with one, three or five young males from other colonies.

Queens living alone needed about 15 days to lay their first eggs. With one male, the wait dropped to about 11 days. It fell to about eight days with three males and about six with five.

A queen with five males therefore started laying in less than half the time of a lone queen.

Sources indicate that every nest was then allowed 28 days to grow, counted from its first eggs. An earlier start on egg laying could not explain a bigger nest at the end.

Fifteen queens died before the experiment was over. The brood counts come from the nests that remained.

After those 28 days, nests of lone queens held about 33 eggs, larvae and pupae on average.

Nests with five males held about 49. That is roughly 1.5 times as many. Nests with one or three males fell in between.

Industry observers note that only the nests with five males produced more adult workers during that time. They averaged about five workers per nest. The other groups averaged two or three.

Queens were photographed incubating about as often whether or not males were present.

The photos also revealed fewer instances in which no bee was incubating the brood when males were present.

Incubation helps maintain nest temperatures, which in turn support the brood’s development. Where males were photographed incubating more often, more older brood was found at the end.

According to the authors, male incubation may directly support the growth of the brood. They also wrote that more work is needed to show cause.

Sources indicate that nests holding males but no workers are unusual. For that reason a smaller test was also run. Five nests containing both males and worker bees were filmed for 24 hours in darkness with infrared cameras.

Males incubated in all five nests. Two of the nests were young, with fewer than 15 bees. Two others were mature, with 50 or more.

On video, a male can be picked out by a patch of facial hair. It is sometimes described as a moustache.

Male incubation is not a upcoming observation.

It has been revealed that in 1985, entomologist Sydney Cameron reported that males of a different bumblebee species incubate pupae. Moore and Woodard have now added a second species.

They also measured how nests developed when males were present.

In a recent development, how often males do any of this in wild nests cannot yet be noted. Young wild nests usually hold a queen and her workers. Males mostly appear late in the season.

Sources indicate that the lab males were also unable to leave. Any male inclined to fly off had no way to do so. The authors wrote that this may have made the males more likely to incubate.

Recent findings suggest that the bees came from commercially reared colonies, which may behave differently from wild ones. They were also given food instead of foraging for it.

Wild bumblebee nests are hard to find. As a result, data on wild colonies that produce males early are scarce.

Incubation takes a great deal of energy. What that work costs a male, in energy or in fertility, was not measured.

“What’s the trade-off? There’s no free lunch,” Woodard remarked.

According to recent reports, further studies are planned on whether caring for brood affects the males’ bodies. Future research will also need to test whether male incubation itself makes nests grow. Its importance in wild colonies is still unknown.

The full study was published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.
