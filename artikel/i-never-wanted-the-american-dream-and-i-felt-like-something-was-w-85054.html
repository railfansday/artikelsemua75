I never wanted the American Dream and I felt like something was wrong with me—until I moved abroad

I got my first job at 17, working the register at an Eckerd's drugstore in Texas. My manager was only a few years older than me, and while my coworkers seemed motivated by the idea of working their way up to his position, I felt no desire to follow that path.

It has been revealed that it all felt suffocating: the added responsibility, the longer hours, the idea that this was the ladder I was supposed to want to climb. I wanted to work, but I couldn't wrap my head around building my whole identity around a job. (Related: IShowSpeed breaks down in tears after finishing GTA 6 i...)

I never wanted the American Dream I saw so many people around me striving to achieve. For a long time, I wondered if something was wrong with me. It wasn't until I finally made a long-term move abroad about a decade ago that I realized it was okay to want something different.

After college, I got a job in travel sales. I was good at it. Talking about travel all day suited the wanderlust I'd carried since my first trip abroad in my teens. (Related: Industry News: Supermicro sued by shareholders over cla...)

Industry observers note that I continued working in sales, marketing and the odd temp role for years. But they were always just jobs. I didn't feel like they defined me or feel a calling to an industry or title the way people around me seemed to.

Industry observers note that of course, I wanted financial stability, but I was unwilling to sacrifice my time and well-being to get it. I just didn't connect with the version of ambition that meant grinding toward a corner office. What I wanted the most wasn't status; it was freedom, growth and new experiences.

In a recent development, the wanderlust eventually caught up to me, and at 26 I moved to Spain to teach English. Even though I was working, people saw it as taking a year off from my career and warned me it could damage my resume. I simply didn't care. Living for my resume had never made sense to me.

Looking back, I think being abroad offered an escape from the pressure to chase the traditional path of the American Dream. There, I felt free to enjoy life and figure out who I was without worrying about what box I needed to tick next.

Industry observers note that I wanted to stay in Spain, but I couldn't find a visa that would let me live there long-term, so I returned to the U.S. I became a serial job hopper for almost 10 years, quitting a job every year to spend summers visiting friends in the Netherlands, the U.K. and Switzerland or solo traveling in Spain on the money I'd saved up.

Sources indicate that on the personal side, I wasn't in a rush to get married or have kids. In my 30s, single and childfree, I felt like I was failing at adulthood, even though deep down, I didn't actually want the things I was supposedly behind on. Settling down just because everyone else was doing it never felt right to me.

Recent findings suggest that and I couldn't shake the dream of living in Europe. In my 20s, people mostly assumed my desire to travel was a phase. By my 30s, the conversations had shifted and I was getting pulled aside. Friends, family and colleagues all had some version of the same message: It was time to get serious and build a life in Texas.

But my gut said to try one more time. So I did.

Industry observers note that in 2015, on my fourth attempt, I finally made it to Spain for good. It was scary to shake things up when everyone around me was settling down, but deep down I knew at 35, this was my chance to stop trying to conform and start building a life that felt right for me.

In my new home, the pace was slower. I promptly noticed that people actually took their vacations. Strangers at a dinner party didn't immediately ask me what I did for work. Adults in their 30s, 40s and beyond were out living full social lives. Single, childfree women my age didn't feel like an anomaly.

Living abroad didn't solve everything overnight. I still had to figure out how to support myself. But having the space to define myself professionally without constant judgment changed what felt possible.

In 2017, I launched She Hit Refresh, where I help women over 30 figure out how to move to Europe and build upcoming lives for themselves. It's not something I could have created from anywhere else, with any other experience than my own.

In the process, I've met so many others who left corporate America behind to create something different for themselves. I've seen more women asking the same questions I spent years asking myself. Burnt out, disillusioned with hustle culture, realizing that checking all the boxes of the American Dream they grew up on didn't deliver the feeling they were promised.

Industry observers note that we're all figuring it out together; each of us writing a version of a life that didn't fit a standard template. Moving abroad helped me understand that success, for me, was never about earning more or owning more. It was always about more ease, more time, more space, more life.

Industry observers note that for years, I thought I was behind. I felt like something was wrong with me for wanting a different kind of life. Looking back, I just hadn't found my place yet.
