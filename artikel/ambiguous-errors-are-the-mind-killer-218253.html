In which I express frustration at the incredibly widespread FUD introduced by ambiguity in software error types.

I must not fear. Fear is the mind-killer. Fear is the little-death that brings total obliteration. I will face my fear. I will permit it to pass over me and through me. And when it has gone past I will turn the inner eye to see its path. Where the fear has gone there will be nothing. Only I will remain.

It has been revealed that have you ever written or encountered something you think would be an “ambiguous error”? No? How about HTTP 500 Internal Server Error? -EINVAL? Status.INTERNAL? Or even a {"status": "error"}?

In a recent development, ambiguous errors, that is errors that are represented opaquely, are quite literally everywhere. Compared to happy-path return values, errors have long been second-class citizens across all software. I always felt that this was some abstractly “bad” thing, but it wasn’t until recently that I was able to put substance behind these feelings.

Industry observers note that software has boundaries, whether that be between my application and the libraries it consumes, the operating system it runs atop, or the remote services it calls out to. Boundaries are powerful! They let us hide immense complexity behind simple contracts — AWS S3 being the canonical example. But across a boundary, the only information I have to act on is exactly what you return to me. (Related: Porting fairseq wmt19 translation system to transformer...)

In a recent development, so I’m left with two bad options. I can reverse-engineer your errors, in the process becoming a hated Hyrum’s Law perpetuator. Or I can throw up my hands and treat every catch or ? as if I were passing around a bomb. We consume tons of high quality, rigorously tested software, but can’t take advantage of it, because we don’t understand how it fails.

Worse, ambiguous errors are viral. Once you’ve consumed one, re-establishing what actually went wrong is far more work than just passing it forward. So you don’t try, and now your callers have the same problem.

Even Rust, with great support for strongly typed errors, isn’t immune. io::Error wraps the OS’s errno, itself a “god error” kept in line by the Linux kernel’s (very good!) docs. A NotFound from fs::remove_file is something most callers would consider a success, yet most code just ?s it upwards alongside everything else.

It has been revealed that the usual Rust advice is anyhow for applications and thiserror for libraries, but every application can become somebody else’s library! Typed errors should become part of your API. Adding new error variants should be considered breaking changes. How your software fails is part of its contract.

Software can fail in countless ways, but a caller can only do a handful of things about it: retry, fix its input, give up, or blame someone else. Errors should encode enough meaning to fill in those gaps.

According to recent reports, more concretely, at Modal we encounter an amusing variation of this. Running rollouts for Reinforcement Learning involves constructing sandboxed working environments, and when one fails, the trainer should be aware of how. Infrastructure failures should be thrown away, lest they bias training. But a reward-hacking model may simply learn to crash its sandbox if it thinks things are going sideways, in an attempt to escape penalization. Adding an error contract to this model allows the trainer to understand and account for these errors. (Related: How to train a latest language model from scratch using...)

According to recent reports, writing the contract forces you to answer questions like “How exactly does my software fail?” and “How might someone want to react to those failures?“. You may find that your application can’t fail in a way your interface hinted was possible, or that you yourself can be more resilient to upstream failures. With agentic coding assistants, it’s more and more possible to actually do this exercise. List out every error from the leaves of your execution graph upwards, eliminate the ones that don’t make sense, and add stronger types to the rest.
