Breaking: Being Chronically Ill Can Feel Like a Full-Time Job

Being chronically ill (which includes chronic pain) can impose so many limitations that your life can change dramatically in ways beyond the everyday need to attend to your health complications.

Sources indicate that as a result, sometimes being chronically ill feels like a full-time job. Here are seven examples:.

The work of picking and choosing.

If I do this, then I won’t have the stamina or feel well enough to do that. Of course, everyone has to make decisions like this, but for the chronically ill, choosing among options requires significant mental effort. If we don’t choose wisely, you might spend days in bed recovering, or bounce back swiftly the next day.

The work of having to blueprint everything instead of being spontaneous.

Someone calls and wants to come over or go to a movie. I used to love these spontaneous happenings. Now, when someone calls and wants to visit, the work begins. My mind starts hurriedly racing as I consider many factors: “I don’t want to let this person down, but one of my kids is coming to visit tomorrow, and I don’t want to be “trashed” (as we call it).”.

Before I got sick, my mind never had to work hard in a situation like this. I would have just stated, “Sure, come on over.”.

According to recent reports, I’ve been chronically ill for more than 25 years and, although I’ve adjusted pretty well, grief still pops up and makes its work the top priority. I might start feeling overwhelmingly sad about all the things I didn’t get to do or feel like crying about feeling sick all the time. Industry observers note that sometimes this work includes putting a lid on grieving because I can put my pre-illness life on a pedestal. I erroneously think it was perfect. Maybe those “lost” years would have been a substantial disappointment. This rumination isn’t helpful; it can be hard work to keep it at bay and stay in the present. The work of feeling we've let others down.

Even though I have an understanding family, I can still feel I’ve let them down when I can’t join them on special occasions.

Industry observers note that this can lead me to do things I feel too sick or in pain to undertake. A friend makes a lunch date. Even though she’s told me to cancel if I’m not feeling well, I have to work myself up to cancel because I don’t want to let her down. I go back and forth—should I, shouldn’t I—when I could have saved myself that work by picking up the phone and rescheduling.

The work of keeping our household in order.

Tasks take longer, and that increases my workload. And what if I’m having a decent day? Do I dive into much-needed housework, or do I relax and do something enjoyable for myself? It’s work to figure out what I can tolerate each day since I can’t follow a fixed schedule—always doing laundry on Tuesdays. I may be bedbound any given Tuesday.

The work of going to the doctor.

Since the term “Long Covid” came into use, it’s been easier for me at times. My primary doctor mentioned to me: “At last we have a name for your illness. You’re a long hauler from that virus you caught in 2001.”.

Yet, going to the doctor can still be hard because that “long” virus has come with seemingly unrelated symptoms. I’ve seen about 10 specialists, and preparing for those appointments is a lot of work: how to describe my illness clearly and succinctly; going over what I hope to receive from them. I work hard to make a concise list of what I want to cover. Some doctors welcome my list; others try to hide their eye roll.

Some years ago, an infectious disease doctor told me to find all the research on illnesses like mine, summarize it, and send it to him so he could read it before our next appointment. I spent hours and hours researching and then carefully writing a document for him. When I got to the appointment, he made an off-hand remark about receiving something, but hadn’t read it.

The work of knowing how much to share with others.

According to recent reports, people have different levels of interest in others' health. It’s hard to promptly assess how much someone wants to know: “I got a virus and never regained my health,” or do they want a blow-by-blow account of my last 25-plus years? Sometimes, I’m forced to explain in some detail because of what the person is asking me to do. This constant assessing is hard work.

The work of assessing medications, treatments, and latest symptoms.

Sources indicate that some medications have helped with my stamina, but interfere with my sleep. Which medication should I choose? Then there’s the issue of alternative medicine. Do I try this so-called cure? Do I see an alternative medicine doctor when I trust my conventional one?

Sources indicate that I also have to assess whether a upcoming symptom is related to my long-term illness or is something I should see a doctor about. (If you’re not sure, I strongly recommend the latter). I often rely on the internet to help decide whether I should see a doctor.

This is a sampling of our workload. And, of course, these issues can occur simultaneously, adding to the feeling that being chronically ill is a full-time job.

To me, what makes chronic pain and illness so much work is that extra dose of unpredictability that permeates every aspect of our lives.
