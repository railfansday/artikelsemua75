Industry News: WARN Act and Your Commuter Benefits — What Happens to Transit and Parking After a Layoff Notice

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A WARN notice gives you at least 60 days' warning before a layoff takes effect. That notice period protects your paycheck and your time to plan. It does not protect one smaller benefit many commuters overlook: the pre-tax money sitting in your transit and parking account. In most intends, that balance disappears on your last day of work.

Recent findings suggest that this matters right now. Layoff Atlas presently tracks 756 WARN notices covering 61,768 reported workers in the last 90 days, with 6,464 workers scheduled effective the week of November 21, 6,270 the week of November 28, and 9,467 the week of December 26 — the heaviest week on the calendar. These are reported looks, not confirmed job losses, and coverage varies by state. If your name is on one of those lists, check your transit and parking money first.

It has been revealed that commuter benefits are formally known as qualified transportation fringe benefits under Section 132(f) of the Internal Revenue Code. They cover transit passes, qualified parking near your workplace or a transit station, and vanpool transportation. Your employer lets you pay for them with pre-tax salary deductions, so the money comes out of your gross pay before federal income tax and payroll taxes.

For 2026, the IRS allows up to $340 a month in tax-free benefits for transit and vanpool costs combined, and a separate $340 a month for qualified parking. Maxing out both means $680 a month flowing through an account that exists only while you are an employee.

When your employment ends — resign, laid off, or fired — any unused balance in your commuter account is forfeited back to your employer. No refund, no cash-out, no transfer to a fresh employer's proposal.

Recent findings suggest that this is not your employer's choice. Federal regulations bar a initiative from refunding salary reductions beyond the benefits actually provided, and the IRS confirmed in Information Letter 2019-002 that the rule applies whether the departure was voluntary or involuntary. The tax code only allows these benefits for current employees, so once you are no longer employed the plan cannot provide benefits, reimburse new expenses, or release unused funds.

According to recent reports, that makes commuter benefits fundamentally different from a health savings account, where the balance belongs to you and travels with you, and from a medical flexible spending account, which can sometimes be continued through COBRA. Commuter benefits cannot be continued through COBRA at all. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

Recent findings suggest that good news: while you remain employed, unused commuter funds do not expire. Unlike a health FSA, which often forces a year-end spend-down, commuter balances roll from month to month and year to year with no spending deadline. Monthly limits still apply to what you can set aside, but there is no cap on how much can accumulate. (Related: Industry News: The Reformer - Pushing the limits of lan...)

Sources indicate that that is exactly why the balance can grow large enough to matter. Contribute $200 a month, bike or work from home for a few months, and you can quietly build up several hundred dollars — all of it at risk the day employment ends.

This is where the WARN Act helps, indirectly. During the 60-day notice period you are still an employee, so your commuter benefits keep working until your actual last day — not the day you receive the notice.

According to recent reports, every initiative has its own details. Before your last day, confirm your current transit and parking balances, your plan's run-out deadline for submitting claims, and whether your employer concerns physical passes or loads a smart card — a loaded card may have its own expiration or monthly spending window even when the account balance rolls over. If your employer subsidizes your pass directly rather than through salary deductions, that subsidy simply ends with employment.

It has been revealed that commuter benefits are use-it-or-lose-it in the sharpest sense: useful while you are employed, gone the day you are not. A WARN notice does not change the forfeiture rule, but the 60 days it gives you are enough to spend the balance down, stop new deductions, and file outstanding claims. With 6,270 workers due the week of November 28 and 9,467 the week of December 26, that is a check thousands of workers should make this week.
