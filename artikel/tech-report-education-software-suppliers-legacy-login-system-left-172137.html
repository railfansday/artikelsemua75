Tech Report: Education software supplier's legacy login system left 355,000 school email addresses exposed

Have I Been Pwned puts a number on September's attack on Bromcom.

More than 355,000 school staff and pupil email addresses ended up in the hands of hackers who broke into UK education software supplier Bromcom in September. (Related: How to Budget for a Wedding Without Going Broke...)

Industry observers note that the breach was added to Have I Been Pwned on Monday, revealing that 355,051 unique email addresses were caught up in the incident. The data also included single sign-on (SSO) registration reference numbers, although Bromcom insists that no passwords or authentication tokens were stolen. (Related: Industry News: Average Retirement Savings by Age: Are Y...)

Sources indicate that as The Register reported in October, the attackers got in through a legacy SSO registration component in Bromcom's Communications Server environment. The technology had already been superseded but was still running because an internal system continued to use it.

According to recent reports, bromcom discovered something was amiss on September 6 after customers reported problems accessing its SSO service. At the time, the manufacturer was still working with external forensic specialists to establish exactly what the intruders had managed to get their hands on.

Bromcom's FAQ indicates its investigation is complete and confirms that the intruders used a script to extract SSO registration information from the compromised server.

The supplier insists its Management Information System (MIS), used by schools to keep track of everything from attendance to pupil records, was untouched. The compromised service also couldn't be used to get into Microsoft or Google accounts, which have their own login systems.

Bromcom has since pulled the plug on the old component, tightened its authorization checks, and added more security logging. It says it hasn't found any evidence of accounts being hijacked as a result of the breach.

In a recent development, bromcom also confirmed it hadn't reported the breach to the UK's Information Commission's Office. Its reasoning is that it was the data processor, not the controller, so any decision to involve the regulator was for the affected schools and academy trusts to make.

In a recent development, as for the risk to staff and pupils, Bromcom considers it to be low. No passwords or pupil records held in the MIS were taken, it claims, although schools have been advised to watch for phishing messages and suspicious requests to reset passwords or approve sign-ins.

The supplier's software is used by more than 5,000 schools and 390 multi-academy trusts across the UK, though it hasn't mentioned how many were caught up in this particular mess.

The Register has asked Bromcom how many schools and pupils were affected, and whether the figure published by Have I Been Pwned matches its own findings, but has yet to receive a response.

Industry observers note that bromcom kept the superseded component running because an internal system still needed it. Unfortunately, the intruders found a use for it too. ®.
