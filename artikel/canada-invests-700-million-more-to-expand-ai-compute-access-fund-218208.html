The Canadian government expanded the AI Compute Access Fund with an additional $700 million on November 26, 2026, bringing it to $1 billion and opening a sector-specific call for health and life sciences projects.

Artificial Intelligence and Digital Innovation Minister Evan Solomon announced the next phase in Toronto. The expansion adds to the fund’s initial $300 million and is intended to help Canadian organizations develop, test and commercialize AI products by offsetting cloud-compute costs. Eligible work includes model training, inference and fine-tuning.

Industry observers note that solomon also declared more than $52 million for 45 companies under the fund’s initial phase, lifting cumulative commitments to more than $118 million across 89 Canadian companies. Those earlier-phase awards are distinct from the expansion and cover health care, agriculture, manufacturing, finance and transportation projects. Sources indicate that the updated program guide covers up to 80% of eligible cloud-compute costs from sovereign providers and up to 50% from Canadian-based providers. Sovereign suppliers must operate infrastructure in Canada, process and store data there, be incorporated and registered in Canada, be Canadian-owned, and keep governance and decision-making authority in the country.

Canadian-based providers must meet the infrastructure, data and incorporation requirements. The ownership and governance tests do not apply to that category. Multinational companies with Canadian-registered entities can therefore qualify when the infrastructure and data remain in Canada. The rate structure offers a larger potential subsidy for choosing a provider with Canadian ownership and control without excluding multinational suppliers’ Canadian operations.

It has been revealed that eligible proposals must include $100,000 to $5 million in compute costs and end no later than March 31, 2030. Applicants can be Canadian-registered for-profit companies, nonprofit corporations or charities, including qualifying postsecondary institutions; they generally need fewer than 500 full-time-equivalent employees, Canada-based research and development teams and a commercialization proposal. Contributions may be non-repayable, conditionally repayable or repayable, depending on the project’s assessed benefits and risk. (Related: AI for Game Development: Creating a Farming Game in 5 D...)

Health and life sciences applicants must submit a statement of interest by 11:59 p.m. ET on December 17, 2026. Those invited to proceed must file full applications by 11:59 p.m. ET on January 4, 2027, with compute contracts, invoices or other documentation in place or in progress. A selected applicant whose agreement is still in progress must finalize it before signing a contribution agreement.

It has been revealed that assessment covers market potential, organizational and financial capacity, technology readiness, expected economic and public benefits, and alignment with government priorities. Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada states it will endeavour to make funding decisions within 90 business days after the call closes. The department will also use AI tools to support analysis of application information, conduct risk-related research and assist with program administration, while retaining final authority over recipient selection.

An official recipient backgrounder highlights the range of supported work. Vancouver-based Apera AI is developing 4D vision and simulation-driven models for factory robots, while Toronto-based RetiSpec is working on a non-invasive retinal-imaging tool intended to detect early Alzheimer’s disease. Gotcare, also in Toronto, is building an AI-enabled home-healthcare management system focused on rural and underserved populations.

The sector calls sit within the government’s AI for All strategy, which names five priority areas: health and life sciences, energy and natural resources, transportation, agriculture, and manufacturing and robotics. The strategy describes Canada’s sovereign compute capacity as nascent and says most Canadian small and medium-sized businesses lack an affordable domestic option, leaving them reliant on foreign cloud platforms for training, testing and deployment.

Sources indicate that the government is also connecting the renewed fund to its commitment to bring 500 megawatts of sovereign AI compute online. The expanded program is intended to create demand for Canadian providers and support investment in domestic AI infrastructure, linking commercialization assistance to a broader infrastructure objective.

That makes the reimbursement gap central to the policy: companies receive the strongest support when their computing is owned, operated and governed in Canada. The open question is whether the demand created by these subsidies can support enough affordable domestic capacity while helping Canadian AI products reach the market.
