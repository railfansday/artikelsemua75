Ryanair Delays Free Seat Assignments, Indicates Passengers Who Don’t Pay “Clearly Don’t Mind Where They Sit”

Industry observers note that ryanair passengers who decline to pay for seat selection must now wait until only a few hours before departure to learn where they will sit. The airline indicates the change preserves more choice for customers willing to pay, which makes commercial sense. It also creates another incentive to purchase a seat simply to end the uncertainty.

Ryanair has changed when it assigns complimentary random seats to passengers who do not purchase one.

Previously, passengers generally received their seat assignment when checking in up to 24 hours before departure. Under the new approach, the seat may not appear on the digital boarding pass until approximately three to four hours before the flight. The change was introduced with Ryanair’s winter schedule at the end of October. (Related: Christmas market forced to cancel after backlash over A...)

Passengers can still check in beginning 72 hours before departure, but those who decline to pay for a seat may have to wait until the day of travel for the airline to upgrade their boarding pass with an actual seat assignment.

Ryanair told RTE that it received complaints from passengers who wanted to purchase a particular seat on the day of departure, only to find that desirable seats had already been distributed through free random allocation.

Its explanation included a very Ryanair-like observation:.

“Passengers who choose a free of charge random seat clearly don’t mind where they sit.”.

Therefore, Ryanair now keeps more seats available for purchase until closer to departure. Passengers who have selected random seating will have their digital boarding passes automatically updated once the airline assigns them a seat.

The logic is understandable: if someone is willing to pay for an aisle or window seat three hours before departure, Ryanair would rather sell it than discover it gave that seat away for free the previous evening. But let’s not pretend that preserving customer choice is the only objective here…those who are going to buy a seat usually do so in advance, so we are only talking about last-minute purchases.

Sources indicate that I have no problem with Ryanair charging for advance seat assignments. That is the low-cost carrier “unbundled” model: advertise a very inexpensive base fare and allow passengers to decide which extras are worth purchasing.

Sources indicate that I have flown Ryanair many times and approach it with those expectations. If I care where I sit, I pay. If I do not, I take whatever the airline gives me (of course hoping I end up with a window or aisle seat).

Recent findings suggest that but the timing of this change creates pressure that did not exist before. A passenger can complete check-in yet remain uncertain about the seat assignment until shortly before leaving for the airport. Some will become impatient or anxious and pay simply to resolve it.

That is certainly good ancillary-revenue strategy. It is also another example of Ryanair understanding exactly how to monetize uncertainty. The airline knows that passengers become increasingly willing to spend as departure approaches. A middle seat may be tolerable when the trip is weeks away, but somehow looks much less appealing on the morning of the flight.

Ryanair’s family seating policy remains vital here.

Sources indicate that under the airline’s published policy, children between two and 11 years old must be seated beside at least one accompanying adult. The adult is required to reserve a seat, while up to four children receive adjacent reserved seats without an additional seat-selection charge. Additional adults and passengers aged 12 or older who do not purchase seats may be assigned elsewhere.

Families should be aware that they are not guaranteed to be seated next to eachother (and it would not surprise me if the Ryanair algorithm specially avoids this in order to encourage famlies to pre-pay for seats on the next trip).

Ryanair now waits until approximately three to four hours before departure to assign complimentary random seats, allowing it to preserve more desirable inventory for passengers willing to pay.

Recent findings suggest that the commercial reasoning is clear, and I do not fault Ryanair for selling seat assignments. But delaying the free assignment also creates anxiety that will persuade some passengers to purchase a seat they otherwise would not have bought. It is clever revenue management, but hardly an improvement for passengers.
