I am the founder of Hirescoot, so read this as a builder's account of what I ran into, not as a neutral review.

Industry observers note that the job system is broken. People are being laid off because of AI, and employers use AI to filter out the rest of us. In the last semester of my master's I sent hundreds of applications, and nothing came back. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

So I tried the AI job appliers, several of them. All of them fell short. They pull their jobs from third party boards, so my money went on applications to jobs that were probably not there anymore, and none of them revealed real proof that it had applied. The tailored resumes were full of slop, with wrong information about me. Automated form fillers helped, but they are too rigid to use hundreds of times: they always stop at something they cannot handle.

Here is what watching software apply to jobs taught me.

In a recent development, A profile has your name, your email and your last job. A real form asks "Why do you want to work here?", "Are you willing to relocate?", and then the same work history again in its own boxes. Autofill fills what it recognizes and leaves the rest blank or wrong. To finish a form, something has to read each question and work out the answer from what it knows about you, the way you would.

Recent findings suggest that auto apply tools have a bad name, and they earned it: the same resume sent to hundreds of postings helps nobody. So each job gets its own resume, written from the person's own facts. Nothing is made up. Then a second agent scores the draft and sends weak ones back to be written again. Only a draft that passes goes out. (Related: Industry News: School sends out minibus to catch absent...)

A form can fail quietly: a required box, a timeout, an upload that did not take. I stopped counting an application when the agent pressed the button. It counts when the employer's own site confirms it, and the employer's confirmation email goes to the person who applied.

This is the one I did not blueprint for. If software applies in your name, you want to know exactly what it wrote. So every application has a screen recording. You can watch the form being filled in on the manufacturer's own website, answer by answer. Only the person it was sent for can watch it.

Three things were easy to build, and I left them out.

It has been revealed that A button that sends one resume to every posting. It is the fastest way to a big number and the surest way to no replies.

In a recent development, made up experience. The writing agent works only from what the person told it. If a job asks for something they have not done, the resume does not claim it.

In a recent development, A black box. If I cannot show a person what was sent, I should not send it.

It has been revealed that today it reads more than 100,000 open jobs a day on employers' own hiring sites. It takes about 4 minutes to write a resume for a job and about 2 minutes to fill in and send a form. It works for jobs in the United States only, for now.

Recent findings suggest that employers use AI to read applications, and applicants now use AI to write them. I do not think more volume is the answer. I think fewer, better applications are, with proof of what was sent. Hirescoot opens in December, and there is an early list if you want to try it.

So, a question for people here who have hired or job hunted this year: if an agent applied for you, what would you need to see before you trusted it?
