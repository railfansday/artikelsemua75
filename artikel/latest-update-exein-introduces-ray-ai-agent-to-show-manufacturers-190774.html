Latest Update: Exein introduces Ray AI agent to show manufacturers which firmware weaknesses to fix first

UPDATED 07:00 EST / NOVEMBER 24 2026.

Italian physical artificial intelligence cybersecurity manufacturer Exein SpA today rolled out an AI agent called Ray that it claims can help manufacturers find the security weaknesses that matter most in their connected-device firmware.

In a recent development, ray is built into Exein Analyzer, the firm’s firmware security platform, which examines firmware for vulnerabilities, security weaknesses and compliance issues. Engineers can then use Ray to explore those findings in a conversational way, looking at the security posture of a single product or an entire fleet.

Recent findings suggest that the new offering is designed to address a growing security gap for manufacturers. Some connected products contain thousands of software components, and a single scan of one can flag hundreds of potential issues, Exein noted. Which of those weaknesses actually needs to be fixed first is said to be the harder question.

According to recent reports, rather than working through separate vulnerability lists, security scores, trackers and compliance reports, engineers can put questions to Ray about Analyzer’s findings. Among the questions engineers can ask are what is driving a product’s security score, which vulnerabilities need attention first and which compliance challenges affect a firmware image. To pick out the small number of complications that pose the greatest risk, Ray draws on Analyzer’s scan results, vulnerability reachability data and compliance findings. An explanation of why each issue matters is provided alongside the results. In cases where the same weakness appears across multiple products, Ray is noted to help teams see when addressing one issue could improve security across an entire product line.

Recent findings suggest that exein is launching Ray after a run of attacks on connected infrastructure across Europe, including cyberattacks on supermarket refrigeration systems that disrupted stores in several countries in October and November. Similar risks extend to industrial equipment, healthcare, energy and transport systems, according to the organization.

“Europe is connecting more of the physical world to software,” co-founder and Chief Technology Officer Giovanni Falcione stated, “but too many products were never designed for the threat environment we have today. The recent attacks on refrigeration systems are a reminder that a weakness buried inside firmware can have consequences far beyond IT.”.

In a recent development, the rate at which those weaknesses can be found and exploited is being “dramatically” accelerated by AI, Falcione said. With Ray, he said, engineering teams get a clearer way to work out what needs fixing before products reach the field, without every firmware engineer having to become a cybersecurity expert. According to recent reports, also included in Ray is support for reading Analyzer’s findings against regulatory requirements such as the European Union’s Cyber Resilience Act. Reporting obligations under the act for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents have applied since September, with the wider requirements taking effect in December 2027.

Recent findings suggest that founded in 2018, Exein offers technology that is distributed across more than 2 billion connected devices in sectors including industrial automation, automotive, energy, healthcare and robotics. The company’s other offerings include Photon, which provides runtime security for connected devices, physical AI and autonomous AI agents. It raised $270 million at a $1.7 billion valuation in September.
