Industry observers note that toyota’s surprising decision to start selling fresh kei trucks under the Scion brand in the United States as low-speed vehicles (LSVs) has generated a lot of excitement. How could it now? Just look how cute these little trucks are. It’s enough to make you want to drive to Costco and bring home a single large box of cereal tied down in the pint-sized bed.

But why wait for the Scion to get here? Right now, there’s a legally imported keijidosha pickup over at Bring a Trailer (which, like Car and Driver, is part of Hearst Autos): a 2000 Daihatsu HiJet, which happens to be the same model on which the new Scion is based.

Industry observers note that even if these wee haulers look like they've escaped from the pages of a Richard Scarry book, the kei truck segment is serious business in Japan. Over there, these little pickups work just as hard as larger trucks, carrying a little less cargo but doing the work of road repair, smaller construction projects, plumbing work, and, of course, gardening. For the latter, there's even an annual competition to put together the best bonsai garden in a kei truck bed. (Related: Building the Open Agent Ecosystem Together: Introducing...)

On this side of the Pacific, you'll need to carefully check what your state laws say about the use of kei trucks on public roads, as some states allow them to be driven on roads with a speed limit of 35 mph or less, while others only allow them to be used on private property.

Even with those restrictions, this HiJet would be incredibly useful for ferrying around livestock feed on a farm, or shuttling small deliveries around a busy factory complex, or even getting evil minions around a hollowed-out volcano lair. If it does pass muster for on-road use where you live, these trucks are surprisingly plucky to drive, and would make for the ultimate DIY partner. Plenty of hunters and fishermen also appreciate four-wheel-drive kei trucks for their ability to squeeze down narrow tracks where full-size pickups can't go. (Related: Granite 4.0 Nano: Just how small can you go?...)

In a recent development, the bed is also surprisingly flexible, with fold-down sides that transform it into a mini-flatbed truck. The payload is stouter than you'd think, rated for 770 pounds, so though it may be tiny, it's still mighty.

Daihatsu has plenty of experience building kei trucks and cars, including as a subcontractor for other companies, including Toyota. The HiJet has been around since the 1960s, making it Japan's version of the F-150.

Motive power comes from a 659cc three-cylinder engine plonked in the middle of the chassis, good for a whopping 42 horsepower. That power goes to the rear wheels through a five-speed manual transmission, or you can hit a button and get power to all four corners. The selling dealer has recently replaced the battery and done a mild tune-up, including fresh spark plugs and an oil change.
