I am SNL’s ‘sister.’ caregiving is an act of love, but love isn’t a care proposal

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Saturday Night Live’s “Sister” sketch struck a chord with families across the nation. Why? Because they saw themselves in the story. Let’s face it: when an aging parent needs care, it’s often the women, the “Sisters”, who step up and take on the responsibility.

According to recent reports, I’m grateful to SNL for bringing this topic forward. Sometimes humor opens the door to a conversation we have been avoiding. But recognizing the problem is only the beginning. We have to change what we expect from the women in our families. (Related: Taco Bell is bringing back the Steakhouse Boss Burrito ...)

For me, watching the video was painful and hit very close to home. And the many texts I received afterward, as CEO of A Place for Mom, were even more heartbreaking. I am the sister. I know firsthand what that responsibility feels like because my mom and I cared for my incredible grandparents for nearly 20 years. (Related: Nebraska voters appear to reject online sports betting ...)

In a recent development, I came to the United States as a teenager and helped my grandparents navigate senior care while still learning the system myself. I know what it means to feel tremendous guilt, worry about people you love, and not know where to turn.

According to recent reports, for 25 years, we have been helping families navigate one of life’s most difficult and emotional journeys: finding care for an aging loved one. More than 2.3 million families have turned to us for support, and approximately 40% of family caregivers we serve are daughters, daughter-in-laws, nieces and sisters-in-law. In fact, more than two-thirds of the people that reach out are women.

Sources indicate that once we connect with the family, our goal is to help them understand where to start, make sense of their options, navigate difficult decisions, and feel more confident about what comes next. When families are ready to explore care, we connect them with providers across our network of more than 15,000 senior living communities and home care agencies, helping them find options that align with their individual needs, preferences, and budgets.

The service we provide is at no cost to families as we are paid by our provider network.

Sources indicate that over the past seven years at the company, I’ve also witnessed firsthand the uncertainty, guilt, and fear that so many of the families we support experience. When Mom or Dad falls, who gets the call? Who manages medications, makes appointments, and researches care options? Nationally, women account for 61% of unpaid caregivers, according to AARP. And once she starts, everyone assumes she will keep doing it. And she does. In A Place for Mom’s 2026 Women and Caregiving report, we found that women caregivers report stress, guilt, and overwhelm more often than men, and 53% have trouble sleeping at least once a week. They also give up more of what they earn: 33% of household income, compared with 29% for men, from households already bringing in about $15,000 less.

We have somehow talked ourselves into the idea that the more a woman sacrifices, the more we should praise her. She steps back from work. Puts off her own appointments. We tell her how strong she is. We should be asking what support she needs and taking responsibility for providing it.

According to recent reports, first, caregiving has to be a family responsibility. Brothers, sons, and other family members need to be involved. Many already are. We need more.

In a recent development, make the appointment and handle the follow-up. Research the care options. Commit to regular support. “Tell me what you need” still leaves her responsible for figuring it out and assigning it.

It has been revealed that every person’s contribution will look different. Distance, finances, health, and work matter. Discuss those realities instead of assuming one person will cover everything.

Second, we have to stop treating sacrifice and suffering as proof of love. You are not a better daughter because you are losing income or neglecting your health. Getting support does not mean you love your parents less.

This holiday season, my wish is that families have an honest conversation about what help Mom or Dad needs and who will take responsibility.

Sources indicate that an easy way to start is by discussing needs, expectations, and what each person can contribute. Importantly, the conversation needs to be candid, specific, and have clear follow-through. And please, don’t leave it to “Sister” to organize that, too. Siblings can start together, then bring Mom or Dad into the conversation, with their preferences at the center of the plan.

Having a sister is a gift, not a senior care strategy.

Recent findings suggest that to our sisters: We see you. You deserve support and time to be a daughter or sister, not always the caregiver.

To everyone counting on her: Take responsibility for something she can stop carrying.
