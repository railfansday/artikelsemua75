Sometimes a detective catches the killer, and you're still thinking about the mother who spent weeks waiting by the phone. Or the detective who solved the case but couldn't fix his own family. The best detective stories stay with you because of what the investigation does to people, not just how the mystery unfolds. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

These eight miniseries understand that. They show what happens when a case follows detectives home, when people they trust turn out to be hiding things, and when finding the truth means confronting their own mistakes. The mysteries give you a reason to keep watching, but it's the people caught up in them that make these highlights worth remembering.

According to recent reports, an old murder file. A newly reopened case. A clip from Bullseye. Steve Wilkins (Luke Evans) gets all three into focus in The Pembrokeshire Murders, and that last item seems almost too ordinary to matter. John Cooper (Keith Allen) had appeared on a family game show years before police used the footage to help identify him. (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

Recent findings suggest that the case is not one murder but two double murders. Richard and Helen Thomas at Scoveston Manor, then Peter and Gwenda Dixon on the coastal path. Wilkins has to connect them with evidence that can survive court, while Cooper sits across from him in interviews and lies without hesitation. There is no dramatic confession to wait for. The team sorts through lab work, old witnesses, recorded answers, then spends three days questioning Cooper. When the Bullseye footage returns, his Saturday-night television appearance has become part of the case. The investigation reveals just how patient the detectives had to be.

Police have already shot the suspected serial killer when Rellik begins. DCI Gabriel Markham (Richard Dormer) leads the case. The series then runs backward, moving from arrest toward the first murder, and makes every confident conclusion look different once the events leading up to it become clear. Gabriel is a detective with an acid-scarred face, while Elaine (Jodi Balfour), his colleague and lover, is carrying her own history into the case.

Recent findings suggest that the killer’s attacks leave people physically and mentally scarred, and Gabriel himself has been attacked. The investigation is about his pursuit, but the earlier the story goes, the clearer it becomes how badly Gabriel needs it to have one clean answer. His certainty helps him at work but makes his personal relationships more difficult. Elaine has her own decisions to make, rather than simply being part of Gabriel’s story. And once you know what really happened, that first episode hits completely differently.

At work, Jeb Pyre (Andrew Garfield) is a devout Mormon detective investigating the deaths of Brenda Wright Lafferty (Daisy Edgar-Jones) and her baby, Erica. At home, he returns to his wife and daughters and tries to live inside the faith that has shaped his whole life. The case asks him to examine the Lafferty brothers’ claims of divine instruction without deciding that their violence represents every Mormon.

According to recent reports, bill Taba (Gil Birmingham), Pyre’s Paiute partner, keeps bringing him back to the evidence and to Brenda as a person. She was a young mother who argued openly with her brothers-in-law and would not simply accept their authority. Under the Banner of Heaven moves through the Lafferty family’s history while Pyre listens to witnesses and questions what he was taught. Even after leaving an interview, Pyre cannot escape those questions. He still has to go home, sit with his family, and face the possibility that the beliefs he has spent his entire life trusting might not have all the answers.

1280 metres. Dogs mark the area. Divers go down. Nothing is found. That is The Investigation, with Jens Møller (Søren Malling) and his team trying to build a case in the murder of journalist Kim Wall. The series follows search patterns, missing evidence, divers returning to the water, and the hours before anyone can tell Wall’s parents what has happened. Møller has to make each request precise because a conviction depends on what the team can establish.

And then there’s Kim’s family, waiting for answers while the case moves at the speed of real procedure. The series gives ordinary tasks their full duration. People check maps, call colleagues, listen, and go back to work. Each update to Kim’s parents has to be honest about what they know and what remains missing. Imagine having to choose every word carefully when two parents are desperate to hear something about their daughter. The restraint can feel almost unbearable. It honors Wall as a journalist and a daughter, while keeping the investigation in view.

In River, John River (Stellan Skarsgård) speaks to people who are dead. They stand beside him at work, in his flat, in the car, and say things he cannot hear from anyone else. His partner Stevie (Nicola Walker) is the person he misses most, but her presence does not give him easy comfort. She questions him, teases him, and forces him to admit how much of their friendship he may have misunderstood.

At her wake, River also meets her brother Jimmy (Steve Nicolson), newly released after serving sixteen years for a brutal murder, convicted partly on his own sister's testimony. That one detail changes the whole mood of the room. River is grieving a colleague while wondering whether she helped convict the wrong man. Meanwhile, a murdered teenager’s mother is still asking him to find her daughter’s body. And that's what makes River so heartbreaking to watch. He can talk to Stevie whenever she appears, but he can never get the one thing he actually wants, which is to have her back.

Marie Adler (Kaitlyn Dever) reports an assault. The people who should help her ask her to repeat the story until they decide they do not believe her. Later, two Colorado detectives compare cases that sound familiar. Karen Duvall (Merritt Wever) works methodically, Grace Rasmussen (Toni Collette) pushes hard, and together they see a pattern that links attacks across jurisdictions.

Unbelievable keeps those investigations apart for a long time. Marie is trying to rebuild a life after being publicly discredited, while Karen and Grace are still learning what the other one needs in a partner. The show lets us watch a notebook filled with details, a victim speaks in her own time, and a detective returns to an interview with a better question. Karen and Grace gradually find their rhythm as partners, but Marie's scenes are the hardest to shake. She told the truth from the beginning, and watching her finally be believed makes it even harder to accept how badly she was treated when she first asked for help.

In Mare of Easttown, Mare (Kate Winslet) has a lot on her plate. She has to reopen the missing-person case for Katie Bailey, investigate Erin McMenamin’s (Cailee Spaeny) murder, answer to her mother Helen (Jean Smart), keep her grandson Drew safe, stop her daughter Siobhan (Angourie Rice) from moving away, and pretend she is coping with her son Kevin’s death.

Industry observers note that there is also the decision Mare makes about Carrie (Sosie Bacon), Drew’s recovering mother. She plants drugs in Carrie’s car to keep custody of the boy. It is cruel, and Mare knows it. The shame is already eating at her before she confesses, and admitting what she did doesn't undo the damage. The investigation into Erin’s murder keeps moving through the same kitchens, streets, and family gatherings where everyone already knows everyone else’s business. When detective Colin Zabel (Evan Peters) joins the case, Mare has to work beside someone who notices her habits and still respects her. And that's what makes Mare such a fascinating character. She can solve a murder, earn everyone's respect as a detective, and still make a complete mess of things with the people she loves most.

In 1974, reporter Eddie Dunford (Andrew Garfield) goes looking for missing schoolgirl Clare Kemplay and finds police willing to close the case before her mother has stopped asking questions. In 1980, Peter Hunter (Paddy Considine) is sent in to review the Yorkshire Ripper investigation. He is supposed to be the clean outsider, but the force does not welcome his questions. Then, in 1983, solicitor John Piggott (Mark Addy) takes up a wrongful conviction, while Detective Maurice Jobson (David Morrissey) has to face what he helped bury years earlier.

Red Riding gives each year a different investigator and links the cases through the same institutions and people. Eddie has the reckless confidence of a young reporter who still thinks getting the facts into print will help. Piggott, on the other hand, has no such confidence and has to be persuaded to take the case at all. And, the worst part is realizing just how many people had to look the other way for all that corruption to continue for so long.
