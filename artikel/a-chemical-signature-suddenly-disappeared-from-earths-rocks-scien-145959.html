A Chemical Signature Suddenly Disappeared From Earth’s Rocks. Scientists Think They Know Why

Scientists have uncovered a mysterious chemical change in ancient rocks that reveals a major turning point in Earth’s geological history.

A chemical fingerprint preserved in some of Earth’s oldest rocks disappears from the geological record around 2.5 billion years ago. Researchers at Curtin University have traced this abrupt change across thousands of rock samples, identifying a major transition in how Earth’s crust formed and recycled. Determining when plate tectonics emerged has been a longstanding challenge. The movement of Earth’s tectonic plates continually reshapes continents and ocean basins, while recycling enormous amounts of crust into the planet’s interior. This process destroys much of the geological evidence needed to reconstruct its earliest history.

In a recent development, the researchers investigated chemical signatures that can survive when older crust melts and contributes to the formation of new rock. These signatures can help identify the original material from which younger rocks formed.

“New crust often forms by melting older rock, leaving behind a distinctive chemical fingerprint,” said lead author Dr. Janne Liebmann, a research fellow at Curtin University. “We searched for this fingerprint in thousands of rocks from around the world, spanning almost four billion years of Earth’s history.”.

“We found it consistently in rocks older than 2.5 billion years, showing they were derived from a pool of ancient crust that formed about four billion years ago.”.

“But after 2.5 billion years ago, that fingerprint disappears from the geological record, and despite examining thousands of rocks, we never see it again.”.

The disappearance suggests that the processes responsible for generating upcoming crust underwent a fundamental transformation. Rather than repeatedly melting and reworking ancient crust in place, Earth increasingly developed a system capable of transporting and recycling crust across much larger distances.

“That tells us something fundamental changed in the way Earth’s crust was being formed and transformed, marking a transition towards the dynamic tectonic system we see today,” Liebmann said.

Study co-author Professor Chris Kirkland, from Curtin’s School of Earth and Planetary Sciences, connected the findings to the geological processes that distinguish Earth from planets such as Venus and Mars, which lack a comparable global system of active plate tectonics.

“Plate tectonics is fundamental to the way Earth has evolved over billions of years, continually reshaping the continents and oceans and influencing the planet’s atmosphere and climate,” Kirkland said.

“What makes this finding remarkable is that we can see the fingerprints of an ancient Earth disappear from the geological record at a very specific point in time.”.

“It marks the transition from an early Earth where ancient crust could be repeatedly melted and reworked in place, to a system capable of moving and recycling crust on a much larger scale.”. (Related: Introducing Optimum: The Optimization Toolkit for Trans...)

The results identify a major change in crustal evolution around 2.5 billion years ago, although they do not establish that modern plate tectonics began suddenly or that all its defining features emerged simultaneously.

“This gives us our clearest geological evidence yet for when the tectonic engine that drives the modern Earth ‘switched on’.”.
