Foreign investors are returning to Indian equities in record numbers, but the country's accelerating inflation, which last month breached the central bank's comfort level for the first time in two years, could spoil the party.

India's retail inflation rose to 6.09% ​in October, crossing the Reserve Bank of India's 6% upper limit, driven by surging food and transport costs.

Experts told CNBC that businesses have already started passing on increased costs to consumers, with signs of slowing demand visible in the rural economy and expected to hurt urban consumption by early next year.

"Rising inflation will squeeze household real incomes, while higher interest rates will weigh on investment and consumption, potentially putting pressure on corporate earnings," Alexandra Hermann Prasad, lead economist at Oxford Economics, told CNBC.

That could "temper foreign investor enthusiasm in the near term, even if India's structural growth prospects remain attractive," she added.

High-frequency indicators like car sales and credit card transactions softened in September while air passenger traffic declined for the fourth month in a row, Indian brokerage HDFC Securities remarked in a report on Tuesday.

In a recent development, as per data from the Federation of Automobile Dealers Associations, released on Nov. 6, car sales fell 14.2% year on year in October. The industry body said that its "pressing concern is inventory" with dealers, which was elevated at 45 days in September and October, as against the usual range of 30 days. The inflation print "is not good," Gaura Sengupta, chief economist at India's IDFC First Bank, told CNBC, adding that inflation pressures led by supply-side shocks are spreading, and if that persists, it will affect corporate margins and household consumption.

She expects the RBI to raise benchmark rates by 25 basis points each over the next three monetary policies, taking the terminal interest rate to 6.25% by April next year.

The tightening of monetary policy is also likely to slow the recovery of the private investment cycle, denting India's economic outlook, experts noted.

Recent findings suggest that so far in November, foreign investors have pumped in more than $10 billion net in Indian equities. If that sustains, it will be the highest ever foreign capital inflow in a single month.

This would be a sharp contrast to earlier this year in March when foreign investors pulled a record $12 billion from India on worries that the energy shock from the Iran war could squeeze the economy and slow growth.

India's growth story, however, has remained intact so far, with corporate earnings showing a sharp improvement over the last two quarters. That resilience, alongside a rotation from AI names, has drawn investors back to India, as they look to diversify into non-artificial intelligence trades.

In a recent development, the fall in oil prices and the easing of tensions between Iran and the U.S. after the midterm poll results have also worked in India's favor, though experts warn that it is still too early to call November's rebound of foreign capital "a structural reversal.".

"November's return of capital inflows primarily reflects lower oil prices, changing global risk sentiment following the US midterm elections, and growing concerns about the sustainability of the AI cycle, rather than a clear structural shift towards Indian assets," Hanna Luchnikava-Schorsch, head of Asia-Pacific Economics at S&P Global Market Intelligence, told CNBC.

According to recent reports, that makes rising inflation a big worry for sustained inflows. Higher inflation could further weaken household demand, prompt monetary tightening, and slow earnings growth, making the return on Indian assets "less attractive," Luchnikava-Schorsch said.

It has been revealed that india relies on imports to meet 85% of its crude needs and around 50% of its supplies transited through the Strait of Hormuz before the Iran war. Energy supply disruptions due to the Iran war have led to a sharp rise in inflation across several major economies in the world, including India.

"What matters more for attracting stable, long-term capital, and for weathering short-term swings in flows, is whether India can sustain the high growth path it is on," Madhavi Bokil, executive director, credit strategy & standards at Moody's Ratings, told CNBC, adding that higher inflation is a risk to that path.
