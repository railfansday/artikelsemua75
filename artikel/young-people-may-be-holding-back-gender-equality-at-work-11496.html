Young people may be holding back gender equality at work

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

About two decades ago, a substantial body of academic research started to provide evidence for what many had long suspected: Young people were leading the way toward a more gender-equal world. European Social Survey data from 2004 and 2010 found that younger adults were considerably less likely than their older counterparts to endorse traditional gender roles. In 2009, Pew Research found that Americans under the age of 30 were considerably more liberal in their attitudes toward women’s roles than older generations. And in 2015, a study published in Psychology of Women Quarterly found that millennials held more egalitarian attitudes toward women’s work and family roles than baby boomers had at the same age. The direction of travel seemed clear: Each upcoming generation was broadly more supportive of gender equality than the last. Over the last few years, though, there’s been a disconcerting shift. In several recent surveys, young men—and in some cases young women—have expressed more traditional attitudes toward gender roles, and greater skepticism about efforts to advance women’s equality, than men old enough to be their fathers or grandfathers. And there’s evidence that this generational divide extends to attitudes toward leadership, too. This month, the Reykjavík Index for Leadership—an annual study conducted by global research firm Verian and the Reykjavík Global Foundation, an Iceland-based nonprofit—found that younger adults are considerably less likely than their older counterparts to believe women and men are equally suited to lead. “The assumptions around sociodemographics and values and attitudes that we were able to make until the 2020s no longer hold in the same way,” says Michelle Harrison, global president at Verian Group and cofounder of the index. “We understand we are in a new geopolitical and technological paradigm.” The findings raise troubling questions for businesses that have spent decades working to advance gender equality: What happens when the next generation of leaders appears less convinced of its importance than the last? And what can organizations do to change people’s minds? A growing generational divide Launched in 2018, the Reykjavík Index measures perceptions of gender-equal leadership across 23 sectors, from politics to technology. Its latest survey questioned more than 11,000 adults across the G7—Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, and the United States—and four other countries, asking whether men, women, or both are equally suited to lead. A score of 100 represents universal agreement that men and women are equally suited to leadership. After reaching a peak of 73 in 2019, the G7’s overall index score has steadily declined. This year, it edged up for the first time in seven years, rising one point to 69. But the modest recovery masks a real generational divide. Across the G7, 18- to 34-year-olds recorded an index score of just 65, compared with 74 among 55- to 65-year-olds. The gap itself is not new—it has existed since the index was launched in 2018—but it has tripled from three to nine points in that time, and the youngest group’s score has also fallen five points over the same period. The divide is particularly stark in Britain and France, where younger adults trail their older counterparts by 14 points. And while young men’s attitudes have shown signs of improvement in several countries over the past year, the broader picture remains troubling. Younger adults are still the least likely to view men and women as equally suited to lead. These findings corroborate other studies. In 2025, a 30-country study by Ipsos and King’s College London found that Gen Z men and women were more divided on questions of feminism, gender roles, and women’s rights than any other generation surveyed. Some 57% of Gen Z men agreed that efforts to promote women’s equality had gone so far that they discriminated against men, compared with 36% of Gen Z women and 44% of boomer men. The power of the manosphere Experts offer different explanations for what has caused the generational shift in attitudes. Many, however, mention social media’s ability to shape cultural scripts. “Contemporary online communities such as the manosphere are vocally opposed to women behaving in agentic or assertive ways. This includes in the home and in the workplace,” says Rachael Robnett, a professor of psychology and director of the Women’s Research Institute of Nevada at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas. “To the extent that young men are disproportionately engaged in the manosphere relative to other generations, it makes sense that they would show less positive attitudes about women’s leadership ability,” she adds. Jess Carbino, a sociologist and author, agrees. “I believe the generational divide may reflect cyclical changes happening in how feminism and gender roles are being signaled in the media, particularly on social media,” she says. Another possibility, Carbino suggests, is that we’re witnessing a backlash against changing economic and social norms. The share of women who earn as much as—or significantly more than—their husbands has approximately tripled over the past 50 years, according to Pew Research data. For a brief period earlier this year, there were more women in non-farm payroll jobs in the U.S. than men, according to federal data. These changes have challenged long-standing assumptions about men as breadwinners and women as caregivers, and for some men, Carbino suggests, that shift can feel threatening. “When masculinity feels threatened—by earning less than a partner, for example, or by losing status at work—some men withdraw from domains culturally coded as feminine, like caregiving and housework,” she explains. Such responses can reinforce the very stereotypes that gender-equality initiatives have sought to dismantle: assumptions about who should lead a manufacturer, who should earn the family income, and who should stay home to raise children. Related to this, Kent Bausman, a professor of sociology at Maryville University of Saint Louis, says that economic pressure and economic insecurity can “breed a sense of zero-sum gain in the competition for jobs and resources.” Young men who are “still socialized as the traditional economic provider interpret women’s expansion in opportunities across various walks of life as a threat to their own economic prospects,” he adds. A workplace opportunity For Harrison, the latest findings present employers with both a challenge and an opportunity. While the generational divide threatens to complicate efforts to advance gender equality, workplaces may also be among the most important settings in which attitudes can change. Unlike the online spaces where young people may encounter rigid ideas about masculinity and femininity, workplaces can expose them to different models of leadership, collaboration, and authority. Seeing women succeed in senior positions and men thrive in roles traditionally associated with women helps to challenge assumptions about who is suited to lead. Carbino argues that employers can make those examples more visible, creating opportunities for employees to learn from leaders who defy traditional expectations. “Employers can elevate and highlight women’s leadership,” she says. “They can engage in programs in which women mentor both men and women.” Ultimately, Carbino remains hopeful that the “pendulum will swing back.” Historically, she notes, periods of backlash against feminism have often been followed by renewed progress. Robnett, too, cautions against interpreting the findings as evidence that an entire generation has rejected gender equality. Although some young men hold more traditional attitudes than their older counterparts, not all of them do. “Although we need to continue to push for change in these negative attitudes, it is encouraging that most young men perceive women and men as equally suited to leadership roles,” she says. Indeed, there are tentative signs of progress: The G7’s overall index score has begun to recover, and young men’s scores have risen in five of the seven countries surveyed. Whether that recovery lasts remains uncertain, Harrison says. But, she adds, the findings suggest that the next generation’s attitudes toward gender equality are not set in stone—and that the workplaces they enter could help shape what comes next.
