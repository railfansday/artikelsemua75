Tech Report: Tiny reptile found in Wyoming sandstone is a prehistoric ‘dragon’

Neutron and X-ray scans revealed a tiny horned reptile inside Wyoming sandstone, offering a new clue to where its extinct relatives fit on the reptile family tree.

About 156 million years ago, the remains of a small horned reptile were buried in river sand in what is now Wyoming. Its head and body were found apart.

Its remains stayed hidden inside a slab of sandstone until scientists looked through the rock with neutrons.

Recent findings suggest that inside, they found a species nobody had seen before. Its tiny, almost intact braincase may help settle a long-running argument about where an odd group of extinct reptiles belongs on the tree of life.

The research was led by the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI) in Switzerland and the University of Zurich, together with the Aathal Dinosaur Museum and the Swiss materials research institute Empa. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

In a recent development, in January 2023, PSI materials scientist Pavel Trtik took his three sons to the Aathal Dinosaur Museum near Zurich. The boys loved the teeth and armor.

Sources indicate that their father was more interested in the rock. Trtik works at the PSI Center for Neutron and Muon Sciences. He uses neutrons to see inside solid materials.

On a whim, he left his business card at the front desk with a note: “If you ever have something you want investigated, just get in touch.”.

Sources indicate that ten days later, the museum called. It had a fossil from the United States that nobody could identify.

A museum excavation team had found the fossil in August 2022 at the Meilyn Quarry in Wyoming.

That part of the American West has produced giants such as Diplodocus, Stegosaurus, and Allosaurus.

Sources indicate that this find was much smaller. A row of tiny vertebrae highlighted in the sandstone, and the rest of the skeleton was still buried inside.

Recent findings suggest that it could have been an early snake, a crocodile relative, or a baby dinosaur. Nobody could say which.

Neutrons turned out to be the right tool for the job.

“Neutrons are particularly sensitive to hydrogen,” Trtik explained.

“And hydrogen is also present in hydroxyapatite, a mineral component of bones. This allows us to distinguish the fossilized remains from the surrounding sandstone.”.

The team also made X-ray scans at PSI, at Empa in Dübendorf and at the University of Zurich.

According to recent reports, “The two techniques provide different kinds of information,” Trtik stated. “Only by combining them can we get the clearest picture.”.

They then sorted, combined, and interpreted tens of thousands of cross-sectional images. Slowly, the animal took shape.

According to recent reports, they found pelvic bones, part of a hind leg and a tiny skull with an almost complete braincase. The skull turned out to be the real prize.

The reconstruction demonstrates a reptile about 45 centimeters long with two horn-like spikes at the back of its head.

Its head and body were found apart, and fine sand buried both before the sand slowly turned to stone.

“It’s difficult to determine why the skull became detached from the torso,” explained Torsten Scheyer, the University of Zurich palaeontologist who co-led the study with Trtik.

“But we cannot rule out the possibility that a larger predator was involved.”.

If a predator did take the head off, it was lucky for science that the skull wasn’t eaten or carried away by the current.

The braincase is less than two centimeters across, and it holds the most key evidence in the whole fossil.

It has been revealed that the skull highlights that the animal belonged to the choristoderes, a group of long-extinct reptiles. Later members of the group included long-snouted, crocodile-like river hunters, and some of them outlived the dinosaurs.

Paleontologists have argued for a long time about how choristoderes relate to other reptiles.

“The unusual shape of their skull bones has long made it difficult for palaeontologists to determine how they are related to other reptiles,” Scheyer remarked.

The latest clue comes from the bones of the inner ear.

“They bear a striking resemblance to those of the so-called crown group of reptiles, which includes all living reptiles,” the palaeontologist explains.

“This could suggest that choristoderes are more closely related to living reptiles than previous fossil evidence indicated.”.

Recent findings suggest that the little reptile is also a latest species. It is the most complete early member of the choristodere group found so far, and the oldest one known from North America.

Scheyer is a massive fan of The Lord of the Rings and of hard rock, and both shaped the animal’s name.

“When I looked at the three-dimensional images of the little reptile, with its head possibly torn off, I knew straight away: such a spectacular creature deserves an equally spectacular name.”.

He called it Meilynodraco amonamarthi. The first part means roughly “Dragon of Meilyn,” after the quarry and the horns on the back of the head.

The second part honors his favorite band, the Swedish melodic death metal group Amon Amarth.

The band took its name from Tolkien, since Amon Amarth is the Elvish name for Mount Doom, the volcano where the One Ring was made and finally destroyed.

The fossil will go on display in early 2027 in “Rock Fossils on Tour,” a traveling exhibition at the Aathal Dinosaur Museum.

“The little dragon will be in excellent company here, with other spectacular fossils named after famous metal musicians and rock bands,” said the museum director Yolanda Schicker-Siber.

PSI hopes to scan much bigger fossils in future. A proposed test facility called UFO, short for “UltraFast neutron irradiation for large Objects,” would fit objects up to a meter long.

It is being considered as part of a possible upgrade to PSI’s neutron source.

The study is published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.
