I've played tennis all my life, first in school and now recreationally in adult leagues. I've had my wins and losses. Recently, I looked up my computer records—my cumulative record over the last 20 years. A 0.500 win-loss percentage! Couldn't be more perfect.

Years ago, I heard a line in a commercial: "If only I didn't have to work, I could have been a pro!".

I laughed out loud. The irony, of course, is that you have to work precisely because you're not good enough to be a pro! (Related: Course Launch Community Event...)

According to recent reports, even though I rationally knew that my weekend matches didn't matter all that much, when I was younger, I wanted to win so badly! I would have given anything if somebody had told me the secret to winning.

What I didn't understand in my youth, being goal-driven and perhaps a bit intense, was that while commitment and engagement are critical to achieving our goals, obsessive attachment to a particular outcome can sometimes be counterproductive, preventing us from getting closer to what we want so badly.

Sources indicate that one of the brain's remarkable strengths is its ability to focus, learn, and adapt. Sustained attention and practice allow us to develop specialized skills, whether playing the guitar, shooting a basketball, or designing a bridge. (Related: Understanding BigBird's Block Sparse Attention...)

I wrote in a previous post that we become what we attend to. But focus has an opportunity cost. The more intensely we attend to one thing, the less we notice everything else.

I now realize that what my younger self really wanted was to become a better tennis player. Winning had become a proxy for that larger goal. Over time, however, I began to mistake the proxy for the goal itself.

Sources indicate that this isn't advice to say that winning doesn't matter and we should just chill out. It's perfectly fine to want to win and be fiercely competitive on the court. But once I realized that I had become overly attached to winning as proof that I was a good tennis player, I was able to loosen my grip on the outcome.

Recent findings suggest that sure, I still don't like to lose. But by reflecting on what I love about the game—learning and expressing different skills through my body and enjoying time with like-minded players—I've become better able to learn from my losses and mistakes, and to be genuinely in awe of my opponents' amazing plays.

According to recent reports, dancing on the dance floor and losing yourself in the moment can be exhilarating. But sometimes, stepping onto the balcony allows you to reflect and see the larger picture.

It has been revealed that of course, reflecting is easier noted than done, especially when something genuinely matters to us. Not getting a promotion genuinely hurts. It's also OK to leave the dance floor for a while and get a change of scenery. Going on vacation, spending time with family, or removing ourselves from the situation may create some space.

And when we come back, we may have renewed interest in getting that promotion next time around. We can recommit ourselves to work and perhaps make an effort to seek feedback from our colleagues. Or we may realize that we've been sacrificing so much for work and that the promotion was a proxy for the recognition we craved. We may then decide to change course by setting upcoming boundaries or pivoting to a different line of work.

Sources indicate that letting go does not mean that we stop caring or trying. It means loosening our attachment to a particular outcome. We can still want something deeply without letting that desire define us.

And when we let it go a bit, curious things may happen. We may relax, become more open to other possibilities, or feel more comfortable in our own skin. Sometimes, this even helps us achieve what we originally wanted. But that isn't the point—or a guarantee.

Industry observers note that the word "freedom" keeps circling back in my mind here: the ability to choose and re-commit to a goal worth pursuing. This freedom does not necessarily weaken our commitment, but it makes our commitment more meaningful.

Industry observers note that of course, if someone had told my younger self to stop caring so much about winning, I probably wouldn't have listened. I might even have been annoyed. Sometimes, we have to learn these things through our own disappointments.

Recent findings suggest that would my younger self have considered a 0.500 winning record after 20 years a success? Hard to say. But along the way, I made many friends, experienced the ups and downs of competition, and learned not only new skills but also something about myself.

The numbers may not show it, but I would consider that a success.

Sometimes, years later, you get what you once wanted so badly.

Sometimes, you realize that what you want has changed.
