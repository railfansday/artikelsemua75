Recent findings suggest that investors who went overweight in tech and artificial intelligence (AI) stocks may be wondering if the worst is over or if it's yet to come. History gives us some guidance, although it may not be as comforting as they're hoping for.

In a recent development, before this week, the Nasdaq had dropped 10% or more in a single week exactly 10 times since 1971. The list, unfortunately, reads like a history of stock market crisis periods: Black Monday, the 2000 tech bubble, the 2008-09 Great Recession, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the "Liberation Day" tariff sell-off.

In a recent development, each one happened during what became a bear market. The Nasdaq Composite is essentially there right now, so the current correction fits right in. The question is where the decline stops.

Here's what the index did following those past instances of 10%-plus weekly declines:.

In a recent development, at a high level, the results seem encouraging. The median return over the following one-month, three-month, six-month, and 12-month periods are all positive and modestly above the stock market's long-term averages.

But the real story, I think, is the volatility.

Across the data set, individual returns vary widely. Even in a relatively short one-month forward return, the Nasdaq Composite was down more than 11% twice but up more than 15% three times. That doesn't do much for predictability.

Even within the same crisis period, returns were very volatile.

It has been revealed that the key takeaway isn't that this is a consistent buy-the-dip opportunity. It's that this could be just the beginning of an extended period of above-average market volatility. And volatility tends to mean a higher risk of further declines.

No matter what forward-looking time frame you consider, the 10% single-week decline rarely marked a low for the Nasdaq Composite. In many cases, additional declines of 10% or more were still in store.

Recent findings suggest that plus, big drops also tended to cluster together. In seven of the 10 instances, the index suffered another weekly loss of 7% or more within the following 13 weeks. That's consistent with other studies that have shown sharp up and down moves often happen in close proximity to each other.

In the three cases where the 10% down week occurred in close proximity to the market's bottom (September 2001, April 2000, and April 2025), the Nasdaq had already declined sharply in the lead-up.

Sources indicate that in 2026, the Nasdaq was already about 8% to 10% below its all-time high prior to last week's 10% decline. That's not nearly as large of a correction as the market saw before the three instances mentioned above. But last week's huge decline didn't come out of the blue either.

Sources indicate that prepare for more volatility. History shows that a 10% single-week decline usually isn't a one-off, and that additional whipsaws in stock prices can last for months.

In a recent development, for long-term investors with 10-plus years yet to go before initiating any drawdowns, I think this is a good opportunity to either stay the course you already have planned and/or use the current drawdown to opportunistically buy the dip if you have cash for this purpose. Try to avoid making major changes to your asset allocation based on the past six weeks.

But if you've found that the current correction has you losing sleep at night, consider adjusting to a more conservative allocation. It'll likely yield better long-term results than trying to time in and out of the market. It's best to avoid investing beyond your risk tolerance.
