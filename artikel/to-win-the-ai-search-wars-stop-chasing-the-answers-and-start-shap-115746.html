AI & Tech 📈 Bullish

To win the AI search wars, stop chasing the answers and start shaping the questions

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 21 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1522 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Sources indicate that AI is changing how people choose between brands.
  • In a recent development, AI is changing how people choose between brands.
  • Personal agents like Meta’s Muse take their cues from things like your email inbox and run with what they find.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that AI is changing how people choose between brands. And it’s moving from answering people’s questions to taking action on their behalf. Personal agents like Meta’s Muse take their cues from things like your email inbox and run with what they find. Now if that sounds scary, you’re behind the curve. More than 10 million people in the U.S. and Canada started using Muse in its first six weeks. The number one thing they’re using it for? Shopping. And not just for massive purchases. In a McKinsey survey across 11 markets, nearly one-third (29%) of people who shop with artificial intelligence noted it was the strongest influence on their last purchase. Groceries are now the second most common thing people buy directly inside an AI tool, just behind clothes. Availability bias Historically, brands have grown on the back of a human shortcut called availability bias. When it comes to most everyday choices, the average person instinctively reaches for the brand that’s first to mind and first to find. AI behaves differently. It’s more literal than it is lazy, and the same is true whether it’s ChatGPT or a Grok Bot. It favors well-known brands when everything else is equal. In a recent study of AI skincare recommendations, the models picked the well-known brand every time when the products were identical. But give a lesser-known rival a rating that’s even less than a tenth of a star higher, and that stopped. Most of the time AI is prepared to compare, matching what a person wants against what a brand delivers. No wonder then that generative engine optimization (GEO), which means making sure your brand is the answer when someone asks AI, has become big business. And it works. BCG reports that a luxury fashion group fixed the structured data behind its website, and within eight weeks the rate at which AI search linked to its site had more than doubled. The problem is that it mostly works when you’re the only one doing it. In the skincare study, when every brand used the same technique, the gains all but canceled out, and brands that sat it out disappeared from the recommendations. GEO looks destined to become a cost of entry, not an advantage. A better way So, is there a better way? Rather than chasing the answer, what if a brand could shape the question people ask? Every choice has two sides: the person’s criteria and the brand’s attributes. A brand gets chosen when the two line up. GEO is about optimizing how a brand presents its attributes. What I’m suggesting is optimizing the criteria people bring. Marketing has a track record of changing what’s key to people. Brands can make things matter more than they’ve mattered before. Volvo, for example, made safety the way to judge a car. Nike did the same for cushioning in running shoes. Others changed how people judge. Dyson moved vacuums from suction power to suction that lasts. Avis moved car rentals from who’s biggest to who tries hardest. And some gave people something new to look for, as Head & Shoulders did with dandruff. Better still, frame the question around an attribute only you have. Ask AI for the best phone for FaceTime and there’s only one answer, and 88% of American teenagers already own an iPhone, according to a spring 2025 survey from Piper Sandler, the investment bank. In China, where people choose skincare by ingredient, L’Oréal’s Pro-Xylane became one of the most sought after, and only L’Oréal brands can use the name. Where do you start? Find the attribute you win on, relative to your size, and check that people and AI agree. If they disagree, shaping the question can backfire. If people believe something about your brand and AI doesn’t, you make the question matter and AI recommends a rival. If AI believes something about your brand and people don’t, AI recommends you, but nobody asks. Consider Dr. Whitney Bowe, the New York dermatologist who coined “skin cycling” on TikTok. She gave people a new question to ask, and retailers like Boots published guides on what to buy for it. Then she launched her own brand, with a skin-cycling kit. She shaped the question, then built the brand to answer it. Maybe there are two new questions for every brief from here on out. What attribute do we win on? What question do we want people to ask? Shape the question, and you won’t have to chase the answer.
