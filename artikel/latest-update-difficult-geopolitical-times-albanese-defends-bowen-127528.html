Coalition calls for climate change minister to resign immediately or be sacked over ‘catastrophic diplomatic disaster’, despite offering no alternatives on reducing emissions.

Bowen served as president of negotiations at the talks in Turkey, helping run the conference with his Turkish counterpart, Murat Kurum.

Speaking to journalists late on Sunday after a list of countries rejected a proposed “cover decision” – an overarching agreement – for the conference, Bowen noted “Australia left nothing on the field” but the “red lines” he and his staff tried to negotiate to find consensus between nearly 200 countries were unbridgeable. It has been revealed that the biggest sticking point was whether the agreement would mention the need for the world to transition away from fossil fuels. The European Union, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Vanuatu, Norway and Belize told the conference they would not accept a deal without it.

Saudi Arabia and Russia led a group of oil-rich countries that vowed to veto a decision that included mention of fossil fuels.

Bowen told reporters: “I would have been happy with [a] transition away from fossil fuels being in the text, but that was not going to get support.”.

Recent findings suggest that he remarked despite the breakdown, “in the real world” the transition away from fossil fuels was inevitable and happening at speed. He said more than 90 countries had signed a pledge to increase electrification to 35% by 2035 – a step that should help cut fossil fuel use if delivered – but acknowledged the result was “very disappointing”.

Australia was sharply criticised during the conference for its continued support for new coal and gas export developments. Speaking on Monday, Albanese noted Australia’s efforts had been critical in building relationships with Pacific neighbours.

“It’s evidence that we’re living in difficult geopolitical times,” he said of the result. “That is the reality. The important work of Cop is the work that we’ve done with the Pacific, where Australia and our Pacific leaders have worked constructively together.

“In order to have credibility [in the Pacific], the entry fee is action on climate change.”.

The Coalition’s energy spokesperson, Dan Tehan, remarked Bowen should “resign immediately”.

“If Chris Bowen does not resign immediately, the prime minister should sack him,” Tehan said. “As the lead negotiator, Chris Bowen is personally responsible for this catastrophic diplomatic disaster.”.

The Greens’ deputy leader, Steph Hodgins-May, noted it was a “disastrous embarrassment” that Australia had chaired negotiations over the failed deal. In a statement, she said Labor was “not fit to lead – not on the world stage for climate negotiations, and not at home when it comes to protecting Australians from climate destruction”.

Sources indicate that the Coalition, and Tehan specifically, have consistently attacked Labor’s bid to host the conference and Bowen’s installation as president of negotiations. Bowen argued for the role – newly created for Cop31 – after Turkey won the hosting rights after a protracted stand0ff.

According to recent reports, tehan did not say what the Coalition would have done differently if he was in Bowen’s shoes, other than saying the opposition would not have pursued hosting Cop or the position of president of negotiations. The Coalition has abandoned its support for Australia reaching net zero emissions by 2050 – a target introduced under former Liberal prime minister Scott Morrison – and promised to abolish Labor’s climate policies if elected.

It has not put forward any policies to reduce emissions.

Industry observers note that tehan remarked the consensus model of agreement used at the UN summit was “completely and utterly broken”. “Chris Bowen should have known that,” he said.

While the failure to get countries to agree to the “Antalya pact” in Turkey was a significant moment in the history of UN climate summits, the talks did not collapse completely.

The sticking point was the attempt to find language to reflect the “transition away for fossil fuels” that had been agreed to in 2023 in Dubai.

In the final stages of the talks, countries were presented with a text that only referred back to that decision and did not explicitly say “fossil fuels” – infuriating a sweep of countries.

Bill Hare, the Australian chief executive of Climate Analytics and a Cop veteran, mentioned the lack of agreement on the main document at Cop31 – there was consensus on some minor concerns – should not be seen as a failure of the entire Cop process but as “a successful defence of the [UN climate convention’s] integrity against a coordinated attack by a few countries implacably opposed to action on climate change, in particular to any mention of the need to transition away from fossil fuels”.

Amanda Cahill, founder of the Next Economy, was in Antalya to offer advice on elements of the conference dealing with the so-called “just transition” – a wide-reaching set of guidelines meant to protect communities and workers affected by climate change and the shift away from the fossil fuel industry.

“While we didn’t get the outcome we wanted at this Cop, we maintained the global commitment to justice and equity as countries transition their economies to deal with climate change,” she remarked.

“We’ve got a lot of work ahead of us, but parties have committed to ensuring the Just Transition Mechanism is set up and that countries can get the support they need to implement meaningful and just climate action.”.

Adam Bandt, the former Greens leader and chief executive of the Australian Conservation Foundation, noted countries had “understandably” rejected a pact that he said “failed to even mention the main causes of the climate crisis, let alone address them”.

“The prime minister’s snub of the UN secretary general by failing to mention coal and gas in his speech set the tone early in this summit,” he stated.
