In a recent development, we all want to raise children who are kind, respectful and considerate. But sometimes, the very behaviors we praise are the ones teaching our children to put themselves last.

A child who never complains, always agrees and goes out of their way to make others happy might seem like a parent's dream. But what if they've simply learned that being easy to love means trying to please everyone all the time?

As a conscious parenting coach who's worked with over 200 families, I've seen how people pleasing can begin in the most ordinary, well-intentioned moments at home. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Here are six parenting habits worth reconsidering.

We love telling our children how easygoing they are, especially when they rarely complain or make demands. But when being easy earns our approval, children may start believing their needs are a burden and their feelings are better kept to themselves.

What to do instead: Make room for your child's disappointment, frustration and disagreement, even when it's inconvenient. They need to know they don't have to be happy or agreeable to be accepted by you.

When children question our decisions, it's tempting to respond with "Don't talk back" or "Because I said so." But when we repeatedly shut down their opinions, we may be teaching them that having a voice comes at the cost of a connection with us.

Industry observers note that what to do instead: Separate disrespectful behavior from differences in perspective. You can say, "You don't have to agree with me. I want to understand what you think.". (Related: Industry News: Hyperparameter Search with Transformers ...)

Statements that start with "After everything I've done for you…" may seem harmless in the moment, but they can leave children feeling responsible for their parents' emotional well-being.

When children hear this sentiment after misbehaving, they may become more concerned with how to make us feel better instead of thinking about what they did wrong. And they can begin confusing being good with keeping other people happy.

According to recent reports, what to do instead: Address your child's behavior without making them feel guilty for disappointing you. Children need to learn to take responsibility for their actions, not for managing their parents' emotions.

It has been revealed that we praise children for sharing, compromising and letting others have their way. But when self-sacrifice is consistently celebrated, children may begin to believe that being a good person means putting themselves last.

Industry observers note that what to do instead: Teach your child that kindness doesn't require self-sacrifice. Let them know it's okay to opt out, express what they want or choose not to share something. Someone else's disappointment doesn't automatically mean they've done something wrong.

We teach children to say "please," "thank you" and "sorry," but how often do we teach them that they're allowed to say "no"?

When children are expected to hug relatives, share everything or agree in order to avoid offending someone, they may learn that other people's comfort matters more than their own.

What to do instead: Let your child know they can disagree with an adult, decline a hug or express a different opinion without being impolite or disrespectful. Respect means treating others with consideration, not automatically doing what they want.

Children notice when we say yes even though we're overwhelmed, apologize for things that aren't our fault or avoid difficult conversations because we don't want to upset anyone.

We can tell our children to stand up for themselves all day long, but if they watch us constantly abandon our own needs to keep the peace, they'll learn something very different.

What to do instead: Let your children see you set boundaries, express your needs and respectfully disappoint people when necessary. Show them that saying no doesn't make someone selfish and that healthy relationships can withstand disagreement.

Raising a confident child means teaching them that being kind and caring for others doesn't have to come at the expense of being themselves. Because the goal isn't to raise a child who's liked by everyone. It's to raise one who doesn't have to lose themselves to be liked.
