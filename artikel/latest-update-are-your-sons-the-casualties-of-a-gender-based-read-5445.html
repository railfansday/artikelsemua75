It has been revealed that when British author Ian McEwan, one of my favorite storytellers, thinned out his home library of duplicates and extras to make room for new stock, he took boxes of books into the park next door and gave them away to passersby. To his surprise, the only ones accepting these gifts were women. He came away from this experience with the realization that if females ever give up on reading, this remarkable invention of human technology will surely die with them.

Thankfully, females have not yet turned their back on the printed page, but ominous trends in reading habits for girls and boys suggest that at least the level of enthusiasm for reading among both genders is in decline. And as motivation to read slips, so does reading skill.

Sources indicate that where this relationship is most pronounced, however, is with boys, who have significantly lower levels of reading engagement than their female counterparts and fall well short of girls in reading achievement on global, national, and state-level reading assessments. When it comes to enjoyment of reading, time spent reading for pleasure, and diversity of text read (i.e., newspapers, magazines, fiction, nonfiction) girls in the U.S. and in most countries of the world have significantly and consistently higher levels.

According to recent reports, I’ve often discussed in my books on the topic of boys and reading, as well as in speeches and lectures at conferences, that for those of us with sophisticated literacy skills, we hope the same for the guys within our care. This is because we desire boys, and indeed all youth, to experience the joy and benefits of the type of highly developed literacy abilities that we value and that have in very real ways contributed to our personal enrichment and professional accomplishments. However, there’s no shortcut to getting there. We can’t expect boys who rarely read to ever possess the level of literacy we wish for them. Parents who accommodate their sons’ non-reading habits are in some ways enabling stunted literacy growth.

It has been revealed that research tends to do a good job of documenting the decline in reading engagement and achievement without always specifying the causes. Thus, it’s helpful to look at the literature related to motivating students to read and then by inference appreciate the reasons for the lack of motivation. For example, researchers have discovered factors that positively influence motivation to read, such as (a) creating contexts for free self-selected reading, (b) giving students more text choices, and (c) structuring collaboration for reading and learning. Looked at from the opposite perspective, then, one can infer that students are disengaged when time is not set aside for free reading, when students’ choices about what to read are restricted or non-existent, and when students only read in isolation without opportunities to discuss and share texts. These same lessons from research can help guide parents who are eager to create supportive environments for reading for their sons.

I’m fond of saying at the conclusion of my talks on this subject that “A boy’s love of reading doesn’t begin with scholarship, it begins with discovery." The more we do to help boys discover or rediscover the joy and pleasure of reading, the greater the chances they’ll become engaged and skillful readers for life.
