In this age of climate change, wildfires are increasingly becoming a common occurrence. Forests in the American West evolved alongside wildfire, albeit far less intense than today’s blazes. But for some forests, fire is a wholly new phenomenon. Nowhere is this more true than in the Amazon Rainforest, where a recent study analyzing sediment cores (long tubes of ancient mud drilled from lake beds across the Amazon) reveals that the forest experienced virtually no wildfires throughout the past glacial period. In fact, fires only started happening with any regularity following the arrival of one particular animal in the region: Homo sapiens.

According to recent reports, as climate change and shifting weather patterns have brought drought to the region, wildfires have increasingly ravaged a rainforest whose plants never evolved defenses against such a fiery foe. The first real wake-up call arrived in the summer and fall of 1987, when satellites recorded an estimated 170,000 fires sweeping through the Amazon. According to recent reports, fast-forward four decades, and the Amazon is on the edge of a potentially devastating tipping point, exacerbated by this year’s “Super El Niño.” El Niño is hardly a new visitor to South America. Peruvian fishermen named the phenomenon long ago for the warm currents that arrived around Christmas (El Niño refers to the Christ child), but its fingerprints go back much further. Sediment cores from Ecuador’s Laguna Pallcacocha suggest that El Niño’s modern rhythm, returning every two to seven years, took hold roughly 5,000 years ago. Archaeologists have linked catastrophic El Niño floods and droughts to the decline of Peru’s Moche civilization around the sixth century CE.

Industry observers note that el Niño’s effects are felt across the continent. When the equatorial Pacific warms, the Walker circulation—a loop of air over the tropical Pacific—shifts eastward. Air that normally rises over the Amazon to form storm clouds instead sinks, and the rain stops. The result is a seesaw between droughts and floods. During the 1982–83 event, one of the strongest El Niños on record, floods swamped northern Peru and Ecuador while drought withered southern Peru and the Bolivian Altiplano. The Amazon, especially its northern and eastern reaches, tends to be on the dry side of the seesaw: droughts occurred in 1997–98, in 2015–16, and again in 2023–24, when the Rio Negro at Manaus fell to its lowest level since records began in 1902.

That raises a question: If El Niño has been drying out the Amazon for thousands of years, why did the forest so rarely burn? The answer is that in the Amazon, lightning mostly arrives with the rain, so historically speaking, dry spells didn’t have the match to start the fire. But humans used fire to clear land, and deforestation has left fragmented forest edges that are hotter, drier, and more flammable. Brazil’s meteorological agency has already confirmed that this year’s El Niño will delay the arrival of the region’s rainy season, and the parched landscape will be extremely vulnerable to wildfires. It also doesn’t help that the forest is still recovering from the last El Niño, which caused unprecedented damage.

It has been revealed that “It’s similar to if you are already sick and then you get an infection,” Oxford University's Erika Berenguer told The Guardian earlier this year. “That infection is going to have much greater impacts on your body because your immune system is already compromised fighting the sickness.”.

As The New York Times warned in the 1980s, a burning forest pumps carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. But scientists now fear something worse: If enough of the Amazon burns, the forest could reach a tipping point, disrupting its own moisture cycle and turning large swaths of the region into savanna. Since the Amazon Rainforest is Earth’s largest carbon sink on land, that transformation would have disastrous consequences for the world’s fight to curtail global warming, among other economic impacts.

Brazil increased its firefighting budget following the 2023–2024 fire season and saw significant improvements the following year, but the region will need to remain on high alert as the “lungs of the world” walks headlong toward potential disaster.
