Industry News: Average 401(k), IRA balances fall as more workers tap their accounts, Fidelity indicates

Retirement account balances fell slightly in the third quarter amid market volatility, according to data released Monday from Fidelity Investments, the nation's largest provider of 401(k) savings looks.

Some workers also tapped their retirement accounts for cash, signaling that persistent affordability pressures continue to strain household finances, said Mike Shamrell, Fidelity's vice president of thought leadership. (Related: Breaking: Retirement home residents as old as 86 have b...)

In a recent development, the average 401(k) balance slipped 2% from the previous quarter to $152,500, while the average individual retirement account balance was down 1% to $142,261, Fidelity found. Both figures hit record highs during the second quarter. (Related: Breaking: AI for Game Development: Creating a Farming G...)

Despite the third-quarter decline, average balances in IRAs, 401(k)s and 403(b) plans — which are 401(k)-type plans offered by entities such as public schools, churches and nonprofits — are higher year over year and continue to trend up over the long run.

Wall Street's major averages finished the third quarter on mixed footing, driven in part by the war with Iran, which blocked passage through the Strait of Hormuz, a key transit artery for global energy supplies, and caused oil prices to spike. The stock market's slowdown in the third quarter marked a cooling after the double-digit gains posted in the previous quarter.

The S&P 500 index added 2.03% over the three-month period, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1.9% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite ended the quarter 2.2% higher.

At the same time, financial pressures pushed more savers to reduce their contributions to retirement prepares.

Recent findings suggest that the average 401(k) contribution rate, including employee and employer contributions, edged down to 14.3% from 14.4% in the second quarter, Fidelity found. Fidelity's recommended combined annual savings benchmark is 15%.

It has been revealed that "We know that there is a lot of concern out there ... [and] people are likely making changes to their personal financial world," Shamrell said.

Some savers also dipped into their accounts to free up cash, a sign of more acute financial strain amid persistently high prices and ongoing inflationary pressures.

According to recent reports, the share of workers with an outstanding loan in the third quarter was 19.6%, up slightly from 19.5% in the previous quarter, according to Fidelity. The percentage of participants taking a new loan from their 401(k) held steady at 2.8%.

Separately, the share of workers taking a hardship withdrawal rose to 2.17% from 2.12% in the second quarter, Fidelity found.

"There is a rise because of economic reasons," mentioned Philip Chao, a certified financial planner and chief investment officer at Experiential Wealth, based in Cabin John, Maryland. "No question about it.".

According to recent reports, A hardship withdrawal can be taken from a retirement plan without paying an early withdrawal penalty as long as the investor has an "immediate and heavy financial need," according to the IRS, such as avoiding foreclosure, eviction or unforeseen health expenses. Such distributions are also typically taxable.

Sources indicate that so-called leakage from 401(k) plans, particularly when workers cash out their accounts before retirement, can significantly erode long-term wealth by undermining the power of compound growth. Withdrawing funds during a market downturn can be especially damaging, making it more difficult to recover losses and benefit from a future rebound, financial advisors say.

"This is not a piggy bank," Chao stated.

However, despite the general consensus that savers shouldn't tap their retirement accounts, there may be situations in which investors don't have another good option, Chao stated.

In other words, the financial "ideal" doesn't align with "reality" for households, he said.

Retirement strategy withdrawals are happening as rising inflation has pressured households' wages and savings, other data also highlights.

"Real" average hourly earnings — or wages after accounting for the rising cost of living — fell 0.7% in the year through September 2026, according to the latest annual figures published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Households are saving at their lowest rate since 2022, and consumers are increasingly turning to debt such as credit cards to make ends meet.

According to recent reports, fidelity said earlier this year that 401(k) investors took more loans in 2025 relative to 2024. The findings are consistent with other reports as well.

Vanguard Group, another 401(k) administrator, remarked in an annual report that savers took a record number of hardship withdrawals last year.

A separate report issued this month by the Initiative Sponsor Council of America, a trade group representing employers, found that the share of 401(k) participants with a loan outstanding from their workplace retirement plan rose "significantly" in 2025, nearly doubling from about 12% the prior year.

The percentage of 401(k) investors who took a hardship withdrawal from their blueprint also increased for the third consecutive year, to 3.4% in 2025 from 1.5% in 2022, according to the PSCA report.

Such "401(k) loans and hardships are there for participants when they need to tap the funds," stated Hattie Greenan, the PSCA's research director. "Clearly there's a need right now.".

There are situations in which a retirement plan withdrawal could be OK for workers, Chao said.

For example, 401(k) loans are generally a cheaper form of borrowing than credit cards, because investors ultimately pay themselves back with interest, he said.

It has been revealed that "If you just need it for a month, two, three, that's fine," Chao noted. "It's not going to kill you, borrowing from yourself.".

However, a 401(k) loan may make sense only if the saver believes they'll be able to repay it, he remarked.

Failure to repay a loan can lead to income taxes and penalties that erode long-term savings, Chao stated.

Industry observers note that depending on the specific terms of a workplace retirement strategy, investors who are laid off or leave their job may have to repay their full loan relatively promptly. Just 34% of plans allow for continued repayment of a loan after separation from service, according to the PSCA report.
