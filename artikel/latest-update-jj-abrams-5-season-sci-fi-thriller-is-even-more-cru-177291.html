It has been revealed that the best sci-fi stories work because they reflect society's current state or predict what's to come. The Twilight Zone featured many episodes in which aliens and the supernatural served as metaphors for societal issues like racism or fascism. Minority Report still resonates thanks to its unflinching look at how predicting the future can go horribly wrong. 15 years ago, CBS delivered a television series that blends the best of a long-running sci-fi saga with the network's procedural formula. That series has grown even more timely as technology like Flock cameras and generative AI become the subject of fierce debate.

Sources indicate that that series is Person of Interest. Created by Jonathan Nolan (The Dark Knight) and produced by J.J. Abrams (Lost), Person of Interest centers on the unorthodox team of former CIA operative John Reese (Jim Caviezel) and reclusive billionaire Harold Finch (Michael Emerson). In the wake of September 11th, Finch developed a machine for the government that can identify potential terrorist attacks; he hires Reese to prevent potential crimes using the information the Machine provides. This premise carried Person of Interest for five seasons, and like any good sci-fi, it raised more than its share of time. (Related: Compound Interest Calculator: See Your Money Grow...)

Anyone who has watched J.J. Abrams' previous work on Lost or Fringe knows he tends to back series with a labyrinthine plot, a great hook, or both. Person of Interest is such a series, as it begins to reveal what caused Reese to drop off the grid after his CIA days and how Finch created the Machine. It also introduces a number of threats, like Decima Technologies, a organization that wants the Machine for its own nefarious ends, and Samaritan, another source of technology like the Machine that's willing to go to extreme lengths to eliminate potential threats. Like Minority Report before it, Person of Interest digs into the dark side of government surveillance and what can happen when that technology falls into the wrong hands.

Perhaps the most interesting sci-fi elements in Person of Interest come with the introduction of former Marine Sameen Shaw (Sarah Shahi) and the brilliant hacker "Root" (Amy Acker). Both women have their lives radically reshaped by the Machine: Shaw worked for a government program that used its intelligence to root out threats, while Root becomes its living avatar after growing obsessed with it. They also serve as foils to Reese and Finch, creating a great dynamic in Person of Interest's later seasons. Another great dynamic is the unorthodox partnership between Reese and NYPD detective Joss Carter (Taraji P. Henson), as Carter goes from wanting to arrest Reese to seekinghis help on certain cases. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

In a recent development, person of Interest was a massive success for CBS, earning some of the network's highest ratings and critical acclaim throughout its run. However, its biggest legacy remains the questions it raised about artificial intelligence, especially in a landscape where AI is debated across the entertainment industry. Jonathan Nolan discussed these themes in an interview, noting that Person of Interest grew out of his work on The Dark Knight and Interstellar—both movies that tackle surveillance and artificial intelligence, respectively.

"More than almost anything I’ve done, people talk to me about Person of Interest...I wouldn’t credit us with any particular gift for prophecy. The pieces of it were right there if you cared to look at them.".

The dangers of unchecked technology are a theme Nolan has returned to in his current projects, particularly those he's developed with his wife, Lisa Joy. At its peak, Westworld added upcoming layers to the age-old story of artificial intelligence run amok, while Fallout builds on the video games that inspired it by showing the lengths those in power will go totest new technologies. None of it would have been possible without the success of Person of Interest, and now that the series is streaming on Peacock, it's worth a watch.
