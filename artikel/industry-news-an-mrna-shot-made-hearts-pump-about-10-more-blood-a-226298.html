An experimental mRNA treatment helped mouse heart cells divide after a heart attack, improving pumping function, but human trials are still needed.

An experimental treatment pushed adult heart muscle cells in mice to divide again after a heart attack, something those cells normally cannot do.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge delivered a modified messenger RNA that briefly switches on two proteins inside heart cells, Myc and Cyclin T1.

Treated mice pumped around 10 percent more blood with each heartbeat than mice given a mock treatment, the university reported.

In a recent development, the gain held when the animals were reassessed at 12 weeks. The work was done in mice, and the treatment has not been tested in people. (Related: AI for Game Development: Creating a Farming Game in 5 D...)

An adult human heart can lose up to one billion heart muscle cells in a heart attack. Those cells are never replaced, which weakens the pump and leaves scar tissue behind. (Related: Industry News: Uber Eats driver saves elderly woman aft...)

Recent findings suggest that an estimated 920,000 people in the U.K. are living with heart failure, and a transplant is the only cure, according to the British Heart Foundation (BHF).

“Getting the human heart to repair itself is the holy grail for cardiologists,” said Professor Bryan Williams, chief scientific and medical officer at the BHF.

Myc is better known as a cancer gene. Switching it on makes liver cells multiply within days, yet in the heart it does almost nothing.

A 2020 mouse study explained why. Myc protein binds DNA in heart muscle cells but cannot turn genes on, because the heart is short of a partner protein, Cyclin T1.

“When the Ccnt1 and Myc genes were expressed together, the heart switched into a regenerative state,” said Dr. Megan Bywater, a senior research officer at QIMR Berghofer Medical Research Institute and first author of that work.

The newly introduced experiment turns that finding into something closer to a therapy. Instead of altering genes, it uses mRNA, the kind of instruction a cell reads and then breaks down.

The team injected 100 micrograms of modified mRNA straight into the heart muscle during surgery. Injections guided by ultrasound did not work efficiently.

Myc and Cyclin T1 protein indicated up in heart tissue one hour and four hours after the injection, and had gone by 24 hours.

A fluorescent version of the molecule, used to track where it travelled, was still visible around damaged heart cells 24 hours after the injection.

The university notes the brief, localized signal is not expected to cause any permanent modification, which is meant to limit the risk of overgrowth.

Ultrasound scans tracked each animal on days three, six and 28 after treatment.

In a recent development, ejection fraction, the share of blood the left ventricle pushes out with each beat, dropped by 26 percent in treated mice by day 28. In untreated animals it fell by 37 percent.

At 12 weeks the decline was 7.1 percent in the treated group, against 16.1 percent in controls.

Markers of cell division rose in the treated hearts, and the number of heart muscle cells had climbed by day five.

Tagging cells and their descendants showed 41.5 percent of labeled heart muscle cells sitting in adjacent pairs after treatment, compared with 5.5 percent in control hearts.

A genetically engineered version of the same approach did even better, holding the day-28 decline to 9 percent against 23 percent in controls.

Industry observers note that scar tissue told a different story. By day 28 the scar was no smaller in treated mice, although the volume of working muscle in the left ventricle had grown.

According to recent reports, waiting did not ruin the effect. In the transgenic model, switching the two proteins on seven to eight days later held the ejection fraction drop to 14 percent by day 36, against 30 percent in untreated animals.

No tumors were found in the treated animals at either checkpoint, and laboratory tests found no added capacity to transform cells.

Switching Myc on across the whole heart, by contrast, killed seven of eight mice in the transgenic version of the experiment, which is why the team confined the signal to the injured area.

Among the animals that received the mRNA, survival across 12 weeks did not differ significantly from controls.

Human heart muscle cells grown from stem cells in a dish also highlighted more signs of division after treatment.

The Cambridge group is now refining the treatment with BHF funding.

Larger animal studies, and then human trials, are needed to establish safety and effectiveness before the approach could help a human heart heal after a heart attack.

“These are really exciting results,” said Dr. Catherine Wilson, an associate professor of pharmacology at the University of Cambridge and co-senior author of the study.

“We’re not just treating the symptoms of heart failure – this approach could help the heart to repair itself,” Wilson added.

“That makes it exciting when studies like this are able to reignite regeneration in heart muscle cells,” Williams stated.

The study was published in the journal Nature Cardiovascular Research.
