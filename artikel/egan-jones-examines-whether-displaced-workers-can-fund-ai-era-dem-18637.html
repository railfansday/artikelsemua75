Sources indicate that NEW YORK, Nov. 20, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Egan-Jones released a commentary examining how artificial intelligence could reshape the services economy, and whether workers displaced by the technology will keep the income to buy what they want. It offers institutional investors a framework for assessing the risks.

Industry observers note that services now dominate US employment, and professional services such as accounting, audit, legal and consulting, along with software, face the most immediate effects of AI. Much of that exposure sits outside public markets: ten of the 20 largest US accounting firms are backed by private equity. As AI reprices billable work, the commentary finds disruption risk falling on partners and private equity sponsors, who hold the equity, and on the private credit funds that hold much of the debt. Against that, Egan-Jones argues that demand is highly elastic: people buy more and expect higher quality as prices decline. It points to radiology: after AI pioneer Geoffrey Hinton stated in 2016 that people should stop training radiologists, demand for scans kept climbing, and US programs offered a record 1,478 training positions this year. (Related: Latest Update: Should I Quit My Job? A Decision Framewo...)

Egan-Jones also expects the number of consumers to grow, as always-on AI agents effectively add buyers.

The harder question, according to the commentary, is how people will obtain capital to act on those demands if they cease to be economically useful in a world where intelligence is no longer scarce. AI makes entrepreneurship easier, yet many employed people lack the capital, skills or desire to start companies, and many fresh ventures may fail. Egan-Jones expects destitution for some workers.

That is where the commentary locates political risk. It suggests a wealth tax, under which the government takes partial ownership of assets without liquidating them, appears more viable than the old model of distributing wealth. It also recalls that earlier transitions, including the Industrial Revolution, contributed to mass revolutions and suffering in several countries. Investors are advised to watch for regulation and tax restructuring.

Egan-Jones concludes that appetite alone pays no bills: AI will hit software and services businesses, many of them in private hands, and displaced workers may lack the income to spend. Navigating this economic transition, it says, must be done cautiously.
