I’ve spent much of the last decade investigating buildings with problems ranging from ice dams and moisture damage to comfort problems and unusually high energy bills. Rarely does the answer reveal itself with a visual-only inspection. More often the investigation involves gathering clues, testing theories, and using specialized tools to understand what the building is trying to tell us. In many ways, building investigators function like building doctors.

Some of the tools used during investigations are familiar to most contractors, while others are highly specialized measuring instruments. Depending on the problem being investigated, they may measure temperature, humidity, pressure, airflow, energy use, or moisture hidden within building assemblies that cannot be identified through visual inspection alone.

Industry observers note that this article covers many of the core tools used in building investigations, energy audits, and commissioning work. Though not a complete list, these are some of the instruments many experienced “building doctors” rely on to collect data and better understand how buildings perform.

Recent findings suggest that before any investigation begins, safety comes first. Building investigators routinely carry wearable carbon monoxide monitors, combustible-gas detectors, and other personal safety equipment because they often enter homes without knowing the condition of the combustion appliances, venting systems, or gas piping. Understanding building performance is important, but first you need to know the building is safe to occupy. Then you can use these specialized tools to reveal what’s happening inside the building enclosure and mechanical systems.

Doesn’t just measure leakage—it reveals how a building behaves as an air system.

Sources indicate that A blower door is one of the most recognizable tools used in building diagnostics and commissioning. By temporarily pressurizing or depressurizing a building, investigators can measure how much air leaks through the building enclosure. The system typically consists of a calibrated fan mounted in an exterior doorway along with digital pressure gauges and software used to calculate leakage rates.

Blower doors provide both quantitative and investigative information. In addition to measuring total building leakage, they can be combined with thermal-imaging cameras, smoke tools, or pressure diagnostics to help pinpoint where leaks are occurring. Many homes contain thousands of small leakage pathways—and occasionally larger holes—hidden around the enclosure. Modern software can estimate the energy penalty associated with excessive leakage based on climate, utility costs, and HVAC efficiency.

Many assume that a blower door is only useful for measuring air leakage. In reality, much of its value comes from helping investigators understand how air moves through a building and how different assemblies are connected. Blower-door testing is great for diagnosing air-leakage problems, but sometimes it points to an entirely different concern. More than once I’ve tested older homes expecting to find significant air leakage, only to discover the house was relatively tight. In those cases, the blower door helped rule out air leakage as the primary problem and directed the investigation toward other causes of the comfort, moisture, or energy-use concerns—something I wouldn’t have known without testing.

In a recent development, researchers and builders began paying much closer attention to air leakage in the late 1970s after recognizing how uncontrolled airflow affects energy use, comfort, indoor air quality, and moisture movement within assemblies. Air leakage can transport large amounts of moisture into walls, attics, and roof assemblies, sometimes creating hidden condensation and long-term durability problems. Code-required blower-door testing for latest homes became widespread with the 2012 edition of the International Residential Code, although the equipment had already been used in building research and energy auditing for decades.

Operating a blower door is relatively straightforward, but interpreting the results requires training and experience. Organizations such as Building Performance Institute (BPI) and the Residential Energy Services Network (RESNET) offer courses that teach both testing procedures and building-science fundamentals. For contractors contemplating a blower-door purchase, the most important considerations are support, training, and compatibility with local programs or certification requirements.

Industry observers note that two manufacturers dominate the residential blower-door market: Minneapolis Blower Door and Retrotec. Both use calibrated fans, digital manometers, adjustable frames, and nylon panels to measure building air leakage, and each is widely accepted for energy audits, code-compliance testing, and building investigations. Retrotec offers several fan/system configurations, while Minneapolis Blower Door just released its newest system, the Model 5.

Provides powerful visual clues, but only when paired with building-science knowledge and verification.

It has been revealed that thermal-imaging technology has improved dramatically over the past two decades. As performance has increased, prices have dropped, making thermal cameras far more accessible to builders, remodelers, and energy auditors. Better resolution, digital image overlay, multiple color palettes, and picture-in-picture displays have all made thermal images easier to interpret.

Recent findings suggest that even with these advances, it’s important to understand what a thermal camera is showing. It doesn’t “see” air leaks, moisture, or missing insulation. It visualizes surface temperature differences within its field of view. In most cases, a temperature difference of about 10°F between indoors and outdoors is enough to produce useful images, though experienced people can often gather meaningful information with less by adjusting the camera’s settings.

One of the most valuable uses for thermal imaging is in combination with a blower-door test. Depressurizing or pressurizing a house exaggerates leakage pathways, allowing the camera to reveal temperature patterns caused by moving air. Being able to see and document these leakage paths makes it much easier to diagnose problems and explain them to clients or crews.

Industry observers note that thermal imaging has helped me locate everything from disconnected ductwork hidden above finished ceilings to wall cavities that were missed during insulation installation. Some common discoveries are air leaks around incomplete air barriers and unsealed wiring, plumbing, and framing penetrations between conditioned and unconditioned spaces.

In a recent development, thermal imaging is also useful for finding compressed insulation, identifying active moisture problems, and spotting temperature anomalies in electrical or mechanical equipment. But interpretation requires caution. Reflective surfaces such as glass, polished metal, and foil-faced materials can create misleading images by reflecting infrared radiation rather than showing the actual surface temperature.

The best way to learn thermal imaging is through hands-on training, where people can compare what the camera reveals with real-world conditions. That said, experience is the real teacher. The more images you capture—and verify through other diagnostic methods—the better your interpretation skills become. Online tutorials and manufacturer training resources can shorten the learning curve. Before purchasing a camera, consider that image resolution and thermal sensitivity often have a greater impact on usefulness than additional software features.

Thermal-imaging cameras are available from several manufacturers, including FLIR, Fluke, Testo, and Seek. Performance varies significantly depending on image resolution, thermal sensitivity, field of view, and reporting capabilities. While entry-level cameras can identify obvious insulation deficiencies and air-leakage patterns, higher-resolution models often provide much more useful information during detailed investigations.

Identifies what’s dry, what warrants ­monitoring, and what indicates risk.

Water is one of the biggest threats to any structure. It finds ways into building assemblies through leaks, air movement, and vapor diffusion—and often remains hidden until visible damage appears. By the time paint peels, trim swells, or mold becomes visible, the problem may already be well advanced. Moisture meters help detect these challenges early, before they become expensive repairs.

Recent findings suggest that the two most common types of moisture meters are pin and pinless. A pin-type meter uses two sharp probes inserted into the material being tested. The meter measures electrical resistance between the pins and converts that reading into moisture content. Because different materials conduct electricity differently, settings often need to be adjusted depending on the building material being tested.

Industry observers note that pinless meters work by sending an electromagnetic signal into the material without puncturing the surface. This allows for quick scanning across large areas without leaving pinholes. Pinless meters are useful for locating suspicious areas before confirming readings with a pin meter. However, pinless meters can be affected by metal fasteners, wiring, metal framing or ductwork, foil-faced insulation, and some roofing materials, all of which can create false readings.

When taking moisture readings, I often begin with a pinless meter to quickly scan a large area without damaging finishes. Then if I find an elevated reading, I verify using a pin meter before drawing conclusions. More than once, that second step has prevented me from chasing a false positive caused by hidden metal or other materials behind the surface.

In a recent development, one place I frequently use a moisture meter is along the interior side of roof eaves. Wind-washing through fibrous insulation can create temperature patterns that look very similar to moisture problems on a thermal image. A quick scan with a pinless moisture meter can confirm whether moisture is actually present or if the matter is related to air movement and insulation performance instead.

For wood framing, moisture content below about 15% is generally considered dry. Readings above 20% deserve attention, as sustained moisture levels in that range can create conditions favorable for mold growth and decay. Finish materials such as hardwood flooring often require much tighter moisture tolerances to avoid movement, cupping, or other failures.

Like most diagnostic tools, moisture meters are most effective when paired with context. Elevated readings don’t always indicate an active leak. They may reflect seasonal humidity changes, recently wetted materials, or temperature-related measurement variations.

Training requirements for moisture meters are relatively minimal, but understanding what constitutes a normal moisture content for a given material is just as crucial as understanding how to operate the meter itself. Good interpretation requires understanding the assembly being tested and confirming findings through visual inspection or additional testing.

It has been revealed that delmhorst, Tramex, and Lignomat are among the most recognized manufacturers of moisture meters used in building investigations. Most investigators eventually find themselves using both pin and pinless meters. Pinless meters are excellent for promptly scanning large areas without damaging finishes, while pin meters are often used to verify suspicious readings and obtain measurements that are more precise.

Reveals current conditions and long-term trends in temperature and humidity.

Temperature, humidity, and dew point all play a major role in building performance. Together they influence indoor air quality, comfort, condensation risk, and the long-term durability of building materials. Relative humidity describes how much moisture the air contains compared to the maximum amount it could hold at that temperature. Because warm air can hold more moisture than cold air, relative humidity changes as temperature changes. This relationship becomes important when evaluating dew point—the temperature at which air can no longer hold its moisture, causing water vapor to condense into liquid water.

Sources indicate that many building failures begin when warm, moisture-laden air encounters a cold surface. If that surface is at or below the dew point, condensation can form inside wall cavities, on window glass, or within other vulnerable parts of the enclosure.

Sources indicate that most hygrometers combine humidity and temperature sensors into a single device, and many modern models can log data over time while transmitting readings through Bluetooth or Wi-Fi. This allows users to monitor conditions remotely and identify patterns that would otherwise be missed during a short inspection.

Hygrometers with data-logging capabilities are particularly valuable because many moisture-related concerns are intermittent. A crawlspace may perform well during one visit but experience elevated humidity during seasonal transitions or heavy rain events. An attic may remain dry most of the winter but reach condensation conditions during a brief change in temperature. Continuous monitoring helps reveal these trends.

According to recent reports, during one investigation, I left temperature and humidity loggers in a guest cabin after the homeowner reported recurring mold growth on interior surfaces. Conditions appeared normal during my visit, but several weeks of data revealed short periods when indoor humidity spiked while the cabin was occupied. Whatever the guests were doing—cooking, showering—was enough to push humidity levels into a range that supported mold growth. Without the data loggers, the cause of the problem likely would have remained undiagnosed.

When selecting a monitoring system, focus on accuracy, data-storage capabilities, battery life, and ease of exporting data.

It has been revealed that manufacturers such as SensorPush and Onset (HOBO) produce hygrometers capable of measuring temperature, humidity, and dew-point conditions. Less expensive monitors may be perfectly adequate for occasional spot checks, while professional-grade tools typically offer tighter, more robust reporting features.

Demonstrates how duct tightness affects not only energy use but also airflow, comfort, and durability.

Duct-leakage testing uses a calibrated fan—called a duct tester or duct blaster—to pressurize or depressurize the duct system and measure how much air escapes through leaks. Similar in concept to a blower-door test, the process provides a quantitative measurement of duct leakage.

In a recent development, duct leakage is not simply an energy problem. Leakage can affect pressure relationships, comfort, humidity control, and indoor air quality. Even when ductwork is fully inside the conditioned enclosure, air leaking from the system means that conditioned air is not being delivered at the rate for which it was designed. That can create room-to-room comfort imbalances, reduce system efficiency, and complicate airflow balancing. This is one reason why duct-leakage testing has become increasingly important in modern energy codes. Beyond identifying performance problems, duct testing is also useful for verifying installation quality.

According to recent reports, current code requirements recognize that duct leakage affects system performance regardless of where the ductwork is located. But when ducts run outside the building enclosure—in vented attics, crawlspaces, or garages—the consequences become much more serious. Leakage can create pressure imbalances that drive unintended air movement through the building enclosure. The house may become pressurized or depressurized, leading to increased air infiltration, humidity problems, comfort complaints, and indoor air quality concerns.

Recent findings suggest that consider a common example: leaky supply ducts located in a vented attic in a hot, humid climate. Conditioned air escapes into the attic, resulting in an immediate energy loss. At the same time, the loss of supply air depressurizes the house. That negative pressure pulls hot, humid outdoor air into the living space through leaks in the enclosure, increasing both sensible and latent cooling loads. The result is a double energy penalty, along with increased moisture risk and reduced occupant comfort. The only way to know how much leakage is occurring is to test. As an added benefit, a duct-testing system can often double as a blower door for testing smaller or exceptionally tight structures.

For contractors already performing blower-door testing, duct-leakage testing is a natural extension. Training is available through BPI, RESNET, HVAC organizations, and the manufacturers themselves.

The residential duct-testing market is largely represented by Minneapolis Blower Door’s Duct Blaster and Retrotec’s DucTester systems. Both use calibrated fans and digital manometers to quantify duct leakage and are widely employed for code compliance, commissioning, and troubleshooting HVAC performance problems.

Reveals invisible pressure relationships that drive air movement through buildings.

Sources indicate that air moves from areas of higher pressure to areas of lower pressure. A digital manometer measures those pressure differences, helping investigators understand why air is moving through a building and where problems may be occurring.

A digital manometer is one of the most versatile tools in building diagnostics. It serves as the “brain” behind blower-door and duct-leakage testing, but its usefulness extends far beyond those applications.

While blower doors often get most of the attention, the digital manometer is often the tool doing the real analytical work behind the scenes. Digital manometers are commonly used to measure duct static pressure, evaluate total building pressure, and perform zonal pressure diagnostics. One important application is combustion appliance zone (CAZ) testing.

In homes with atmospherically vented combustion appliances, excessive negative pressure can cause combustion gases to spill back into the living space rather than vent outdoors. A manometer can help an investigator diagnose these pressure imbalances before they become safety hazards.

Sources indicate that manometers are also used to check pressure differences between bedrooms and the main living area. Closed bedroom doors combined with inadequate return-air pathways can create pressure imbalances that affect comfort and air distribution throughout the home.

Industry observers note that manometers can also reveal how strongly an attached garage is connected to the living space, or whether an attic is unintentionally communicating with conditioned areas. In addition, manometers can be used with other diagnostic tools, including pressure pans, exhaust-fan flow meters, and products such as the Digital TrueFlow Grid, which is employed to measure HVAC system airflow.

It has been revealed that while digital manometers are relatively easy to operate, pressure diagnostics can take some practice. Training through BPI, RESNET, and manufacturer-sponsored courses can help owners develop the skills needed to interpret pressure relationships within buildings.

In a recent development, A common frustration for upcoming manometer users is incorrect tubing connections, which can produce confusing readings. Developing a habit of checking hose locations and understanding reference pressures can eliminate many troubleshooting headaches.

Many building-performance tests rely on digital manometers to measure pressure differences and airflow. Commonly used instruments include The Energy Conservatory’s DG-1000 and DG-8 as well as Retrotec’s DM32X and Solo series.

Identifies hidden energy loads and patterns of consumption.

Sources indicate that not every building-performance problem involves air leakage, moisture, or comfort complaints. Sometimes the glitch is simply electric equipment operating when it shouldn’t be.

Recent findings suggest that an electric crawlspace heater running continuously or a well pump stuck in constant operation can quietly add more than a hundred dollars to a monthly utility bill. I’ve diagnosed both problems recently using a clamp-on ammeter, which measures electrical current. In each case, identifying the unexpected electrical load solved the mystery much faster than inspecting insulation levels or mechanical equipment.

Industry observers note that investigators often use a clamp-on ammeter to measure current draw directly at the electrical service panel. By checking the main service conductors and then isolating individual branch circuits, it is often possible to identify unexpected electrical loads.

Because this work is performed inside energized electrical equipment, it should only be done by trained professionals familiar with electrical safety procedures. Not all building investigators have the training needed to safely perform panel-level electrical diagnostics, and in some cases an electrician may be needed.

In a recent development, for plug-in appliances, a simple watt meter can provide immediate insight into electrical use. Unlike panel-level electrical diagnostics with a clamp-on ammeter, watt meters do not require specialized electrical training and can be used safely by most building investigators and homeowners when evaluating plug-in equipment.

Sources indicate that these devices plug into a standard receptacle, with the appliance connected through the meter to display real-time power consumption. Many can also log usage over time to reveal longer-term patterns.

It has been revealed that for electrical investigations, clamp-on ammeters from manufacturers such as Fluke and Klein are commonly used to measure current draw within electrical systems. Plug-in watt meters, such as the Kill A Watt, provide a simple and accessible way to evaluate the energy use of individual appliances and equipment without the need for panel-level electrical diagnostics.

One of the most frequently used tools in building diagnostics is something nearly everyone already carries—a cell phone.

Photographs of equipment nameplates, hidden conditions inside attics and crawlspaces, unusual assembly details, or visible failures often become crucial references later in an investigation. Photos and videos can also help explain findings to homeowners, making invisible or hard-to-access problems easier to understand. A quick product lookup, a manufacturer specification search, or even a phone call to a colleague can sometimes provide the missing piece needed to solve a diagnostic mystery.

In a recent development, like every diagnostic tool, a phone is only as valuable as the person using it. Technology continues to improve, making diagnostic tools more capable and accessible than ever before. But no tool diagnoses a building on its own. The most valuable part of any investigation remains the person holding the instrument—the ability to observe, ask questions, interpret data, and connect seemingly unrelated clues. In building investigations, observation and critical thinking remain the most important tools of all.
