Americans are paying $290 to meet strangers as friendship apps spread to more than 200 cities

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, startups are betting on America’s loneliness epidemic, and Americans are buying in. Apps such as 222, Real Roots, and Timeleft are turning friend-matching events into a growing consumer market, and some consumers pay hundreds of dollars to make upcoming friends offline.

Alexandra Pitzer paid $290 through Real Roots last year to meet a group of strangers, The Upcoming York Times reported. She had just moved from Nashville to Honolulu for her husband’s Army job and was looking for connection. The app placed her at a brewery with eight other women.

“I can see most of them being lifelong friends,” Pitzer told the Times. “We have a genuine connection.”.

The business is working, at least for now: each of the three companies brings in millions of dollars in revenue a year, according to the Times. But the model carries an unusual tension. These companies are tapping into demand for in-person connection at a time when many people struggle to build relationships in a digital age. Rather than meeting people the old-fashioned way, consumers are matched with strangers based on shared interests and personality, then sent to curated events. 222 began as a college research project. Its co-founders later started hosting dinners in their backyard, and the project became a full-time business in 2023, according to the Times.

It has been revealed that the company has since raised $13.7 million in venture capital, according to Business Insider, including $10.1 million in 2025. It uses machine-learning models trained on users’ feedback after each meetup to decide who should meet whom.

“I think our generation craves the in-real-life environment of meeting each other in a more intimate setting, without digital preconceptions,” Arman Roshannai, one of 222’s co-founders, told the Times.

In a recent development, 222 charges $22 a month to match owners and curate events, before the cost of the activity itself, such as a yoga class or a meal. Interested in dinner and collage-making? That will be $38, not including food and drinks.

In a recent development, the company now operates in 21 cities, employs 27 people and hosts at least one experience a day. Its co-founders told the Times that revenue tripled last year and is on track to quadruple this year, into the high seven figures.

Competitors are scaling, too. Real Roots, which coordinates activities for groups of women, is in 120 cities, while Timeleft, which matches strangers for dinners, is in more than 200. Newer entrants keep arriving, including the professional-networking app Clockout, whose founder raised $3.5 million to help Gen Z feel less alone.

According to recent reports, all of them are competing for consumers who want a genuine relationship beyond social media. Ironically, building these friendships starts with an app.

About 21% of U.S. adults reported feeling lonely in a May 2024 survey by Making Caring Common, a project of the Harvard Graduate School of Education. Among those lonely adults, 81% reported anxiety or depression.

Industry observers note that sixty-five percent of lonely respondents mentioned they felt fundamentally disconnected from others or the world. And when all respondents were asked what contributes to loneliness in America, technology topped the list, cited by 73%.

Others pointed to families not spending enough time together, people working too much and mental health challenges that strain relationships.

Industry observers note that founders in the space argue that this gap is a market in its own right. As the co-founder of group-travel startup WeRoad argued in a Fortune commentary, people who haven’t found meaningful connection online are now willing to pay for it in person.

Turning friendship into a business doesn’t guarantee meaningful connections. Some consumers have complained on social media that they didn’t click with their group or that the experiences felt awkward and forced, the Times reported.

There is also a larger question about the sustainability of the business model. If the product works and customers develop genuine friendships, they may no longer need the service.

Industry observers note that that creates an unusual challenge for companies built around solving loneliness. Their success hinges on helping people establish relationships, and every relationship that sticks could mean one fewer customer coming back.

The companies appear to be designing around that problem. If a Real Roots group gets along, its members can pay the tech giant to keep arranging meetups for the same group in the weeks that follow. Business Insider reported that 222 is building tools that help owners strategy their next meetup together and claims its users are split roughly evenly between those looking for friends and those open to romance.

For investors, the question isn’t simply whether Americans will pay to make friends. It is whether friendship can be a durable business without turning human connection into a subscription.
