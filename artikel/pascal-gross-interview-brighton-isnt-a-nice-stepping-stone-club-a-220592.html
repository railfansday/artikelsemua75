‘There were some games in the past where we went (away to substantial teams) and you're not sure you can really do it,’ Gross tells Daily Mail Sport. ‘But we should now have a mindset that we can win every game. I really believe in that. We have the players. That’s what I said.

‘People say we are nice club and, yeah, maybe we are. But on the pitch, it was time to be ruthless. Time to kill games, be a big club. That’s what this is about now.’.

According to recent reports, gross may feel his ambition has been vindicated, at least in part. Brighton sit second in the Premier League table, despite a disappointment at Hull last weekend that saw them lose a game they had dominated at the death. Gross, 35, ended that one on his knees in his own penalty area, having tried in vain to stop Oli McBurnie sliding in Hull’s second goal.

‘That felt really, really bad,’ he says. ‘Maybe today (Tuesday) is the first day I am starting to feel a bit better about it. I would be more worried if we didn’t create chances. We did. We just didn’t score.’.

Brighton are six points behind leaders Arsenal – who they have already beaten 3-0 at the Amex – but are the Premier League’s top scorers by a distance, with 26.

Gross, the German who returned to Sussex after a short spell at Borussia Dortmund, has created an astonishing 31 chances for his team. That puts him only two behind the best in the country this term, his friend and Germany team-mate Florian Wirtz.

Industry observers note that the debate about Wirtz’s value or otherwise to Liverpool has swirled around the country since he arrived at Liverpool in the summer of 2025 for £116million. The conversation even reached Brighton’s training ground.

‘Jamie Redknapp was there and he asked me all about it just a couple weeks ago,’ Gross explains. ‘I just told him: "He's too good not to prove himself." People here still don't know how good he is, but they will. He's a complete package and he knows I support him a lot.

‘Football is a team sport. It’s not ever on one person. Also, nobody should forget just how difficult and different the Premier League is when you first come.’.

According to recent reports, gross knows this well. He smiles at the mention of his own first English away game – a 2-0 defeat at Leicester in August 2017 that brings to mind the Patrice Evra quote about his first experience of playing in England being akin to ‘an afternoon spent in a washing machine’.

Industry observers note that ‘I was on the floor so much that day that I looked like a little child,’ Gross recalls. ‘It was just all too physical. I knew I had to improve fast, play faster. My decision-making had to be quicker.

Recent findings suggest that ‘My father called me after and said I wouldn’t last three weeks if I played like that again. But I already knew. Going home that day, I really questioned a lot of things. But that’s just how hard it is when you come to the Premier League.’.

Gross had a brutal introduction to the Premier League in 2017, but it was a reminder of the level he needed to reach.

In a recent development, the Hull game was Gross' 300th in a Brighton shirt, and he is perhaps playing at a higher level than ever with four goals and four assists in the first 11 league games this season, as well as a goal in the Carabao Cup win at Manchester United. On an official club podcast, former striker and team-mate Glenn Murray labelled him Brighton's best-ever player, and last week he signed a new contract keeping him on the south coast until the end of next season.

Signed by Chris Hughton from Bundesliga side Ingolstadt after the Seagulls' promotion to the top division a little over nine years ago, Gross has had an inside view of the most successful period in the club’s history.

Industry observers note that during his time, Brighton have sold enough players for massive profit to make a pretty good Premier League XI – Robert Sanchez, Ben White, Jan Paul van Hecke, Marc Cucurella, Moises Caicedo, Alexis Mac Allister, Leandro Trossard and Joao Pedro get you most of the way there, for a total transfer taking of £424m. Gross suspects there will be more over time.

Equally, he feels the days of players using Brighton as a stepping stone to the next level are gone. A lack of commitment soon gets sniffed out, he states.

Recent findings suggest that ‘There have been players who saw it like that,’ he explains. ‘And they couldn't even get in our team because they came with the wrong mindset. If you don't have the right mentality, you don't want to work every day, you can't get in the Brighton team.

‘And if you do come with the right mentality and you push every day, and the team is doing well, I always say that good things will come for everybody – for the club, for the coaches, for the players.

It has been revealed that ‘I love the club, and we have major potential. There will always be players that move on. But I think the players who come here now, they know we are bigger than a jumping club. We're a big club in the Premier League. We have been there almost 10 years and are improving all the time.’.

Gross thought hard before first leaving Germany for England. He visited Brighton’s training ground twice before making his decision and, once he had, dug down into the individual statistics of the players who would soon be team-mates.

Gross (right) has seen a huge change at Brighton since joining from Bundesliga side Ingolstadt, where he played under former Southampton manager Ralph Hasenhuttl (left).

Brighton's form this season has been inspired by a prolific attack, with their 26 Premier League goals by far the most in the division.

They have progressed season on season despite the departure of a host of star players for major money, including Alexis Mac Allister (right) to Liverpool for £35m in 2023.

Industry observers note that having married and had two children in Sussex, Gross has the club in his heart and soul now. We meet to talk at beachfront restaurant Riddle & Finns as part of American Express’s Shop Small campaign, encouraging people to support local business.

During his spell at Dortmund – who he adored as a boy – Gross watched every Brighton game on TV. But it may not have been this way. There was a time, in pre-season 2020, when former Brighton manager Graham Potter told Gross he could leave.

It has been revealed that ‘That was hard to take,’ Gross says. ‘I asked him if there was no chance at all of me playing. I needed to know if he saw me as, like, No17 or 18 in the squad so that whatever I did, I wouldn't play. Or in his mind was I 12, 13 or 14?

Sources indicate that ‘Then I figured I could work my way back into the team because I would always back myself to prove the manager wrong. We lost a lot of games and eventually, I came back into the team.’.

Gross’s approach back then was to train as well and as hard as he could. Murray recalls that his attitude stood out.

‘It wasn’t my team-mates’ fault I wasn’t playing,’ explains Gross. ‘Why would I take it out on them? That wouldn’t make sense.’.

Recent findings suggest that as part of Brighton’s leadership group, Gross speaks regularly to manager and compatriot Hurzeler. His personal creed is that confidence should never bleed into arrogance – ‘I just hate that’ – and he believes that Hurzeler, known to rub the occasional manager up the wrong way, is on the right side of the line.

‘He is pushing, pushing,’ Gross says with a smile. ‘He has so much passion for the job, he puts so much work into it. He wants to challenge, he wants to think differently.

‘He doesn't want to think: “Everything is nice." He really wants to push himself and us. And that balance is a really fine balance. He's trying to stretch the limit and I think that's good.’.
