Industry observers note that the 90-foot Hōlei Sea Arch in Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park collapsed in late September 2026. Here is how it formed and why sea arches eventually fall.

For years, visitors who drove to the end of Chain of Craters Road in Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park could walk a short distance and see a 90-foot arch of lava rock reaching out from the cliffs into the Pacific Ocean.

That view is gone. The Hōlei Sea Arch was still standing on Wednesday, September 23, 2026. By the night of Sunday, September 27, it had collapsed into the ocean, the National Park Service revealed on October 1.

Industry observers note that the exact time of the collapse is unknown. According to the park, it could have happened while the park was temporarily closed during Tropical Storm Nolo. (Related: Industry News: Designing Azure silicon on Azure silicon...)

The arch stood about 90 feet (27.4 meters) high and extended from steep sea cliffs into the ocean along the park’s southern coast, on the Island of Hawaiʻi.

According to recent reports, it was carved into the cliff of an ancient lava flow from about 550 years ago, according to the park. The arch took shape through what geologists call differential erosion: some layers of lava are harder than others, so the sea wears them away at different speeds. Visitors could see it by walking a short distance from the end of Chain of Craters Road, which the park described in 2020 as a 19-mile drive through some of the most spectacular scenery in the park.

The park’s own page on the arch was clear about one thing. The formation was beautiful but temporary, with a limited life span, and it would eventually crumble into the sea.

Sea arches like Hōlei can be found along a wide stretch of Hawaiʻi’s southern coast. They are remnants of once-continuous sea cliffs, built where lava met the sea and added to the size of the island.

Lava meeting the sea can even build whole upcoming land, as it did when a new island rose from the sea off Iceland in 1963.

It has been revealed that the moment that new lava cooled and turned to rock, it began to erode, the park explains. The seas on the island’s southern edge are known for rough water and violent surf, and the waves slowly break apart the rock and carve sea caves into the cliffs.

When waves hit the back wall of a sea cave, the water bounces back and starts to erode the cave’s headland. Over time, that forms an arch.

The erosion does not stop there. Given enough time, the waves cause the remaining bridge of rock to collapse, leaving behind a pile of rocks known as a sea stack.

Volcanic coastlines are constantly being remade, and on the same island, Kīlauea has produced 55 lava fountains since December 2024.

The coastline around the arch had shown signs of instability for years.

According to recent reports, earthquakes during the 2018 Kīlauea eruption and summit collapse damaged the cliffs near the arch. In January 2020, newly introduced cracks and instability led the park to close the original viewing area and the short trail to it.

“This fresh development reminds us that Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park is an ever-changing volcanic landscape,” Rhonda Loh, then the park’s acting superintendent, said in January 2020.

A safer viewing area opened in March 2020, about 1,000 feet past the gate at the end of Chain of Craters Road and set back from the cliff edge.

Later that year, massive surf from Tropical Storm Darby broke off a chunk of rock from the lower part of the arch.

The park is urging visitors to stay back from the unstable cliffs, which drop vertically into the ocean and present a serious risk of falling.

After the collapse, photos on social media showed people entering the closed area near where the arch once stood.

According to the park, that puts both the visitors and first responders at great risk of falling and drowning off the rough, wave-swept coast.

Visitors are required to stay out of closed areas and away from cliff edges, which can collapse without warning.

The park describes individual sea arches as an ephemeral feature of the island’s constantly shifting coastline.

According to recent reports, as the cliff slowly migrates inland, other arches will form and replace Hōlei, the park says. The park notes that erosion does not stop once an arch has formed.

The information was obtained from the National Park Service’s announcement of the collapse, its pages on Hōlei Sea Arch and how sea arches form, and its 2020 releases on the closure of the overlook and the new viewing area.
