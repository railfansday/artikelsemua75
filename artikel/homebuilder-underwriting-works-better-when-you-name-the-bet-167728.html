Homebuilder underwriting works better when you name the bet

In underwriting, the money question is narrower and harder than it looks: what, specifically, is this deal depending on? Almost everything else in underwriting is a way of finding that one thing and then testing it.

I once spent several weeks going back and forth with a builder about a deal he was presenting. He was being sloppy about his comp analysis – overstating his market position, understating the competitive set. I knew it, and I kept pushing on it. He kept defending it. We went in circles. It has been revealed that after a few weeks of this, he finally lost patience. “Come on, Scott,” he said. “Even if you’re right about the comps, four years from now there will be half the projects in this submarket that there are today.”. I stopped. That’s an entirely different argument, I told him. And a completely different bet. Why didn’t you say so? Where’s the pipeline analysis? Let’s look at it.

According to recent reports, he was right, as it turned out. The supply argument was sound. And I had spent weeks pressing him on a number that – even if I’d won the argument – wasn’t the one that mattered. He hadn’t named his real bet. I hadn’t asked what it was. We were both analyzing the wrong thing.

That failure – his and mine – is the most common and most expensive mistake in underwriting. Not a specific bad assumption about costs, or an aggressive absorption rate, or a pricing premium that didn’t materialize. Those are the visible problems. The underlying one is simpler: not knowing what the deal is actually depending on, or not communicating it.

According to recent reports, every project depends on one thing being true – sometimes two, rarely three – that, if wrong, makes the deal fail to perform regardless of how well everything else turns out. It’s a premium spot with no comps – how hard can the price be pushed? It’s a rough neighborhood – with great access and it looks to be transitioning – do people take the same risk to buy as to rent here? Before there is a there there, how fast can we sell in our newly introduced MPC? Will the bond market keep buying bonds early in the life of our deal? How much competition will we have five years from now? One of these is carrying most of the weight.

Good underwriting means identifying that assumption first, then allocating effort accordingly. If the real bet is that this corridor will have half the supply in five years due to land-use and infrastructure constraints, you should be spending far more time on a detailed pipeline analysis – project by project, timing by timing – than on the precision of your current comp spread. The comp analysis matters, but it’s not the question. The question is whether the supply thesis holds.

If the real bet is that buyers will pay a premium for your product, the work is different. Have you comped the house in exhaustive detail – not category-level, but feature by feature? What do buyer-preference surveys actually show about the specific features you’re banking on? Is there evidence from comparable markets that buyers pay more for those features, in that amount? Do you know how to sell it effectively? Generic confidence in your product is not the same thing as a tested value proposition. If you can’t answer those questions specifically, you’re not underwriting the real bet – you’re hoping it works out.

Industry observers note that the problem is that most pro formas don’t reveal what they’re depending on. Effort gets distributed roughly evenly across the model – costs, absorption, pricing, schedule – with each assumption tweaked modestly in the optimistic direction. The result looks rigorous because it covers everything. But covering everything is not the same as testing the thing that matters. When several mildly aggressive assumptions sit next to each other in a spreadsheet, no one can see that one of them is critical and uncertain and the rest are secondary – which is not to say unimportant.

When I walk builders through this kind of underwriting, I sometimes hear “if we did all that and were hyper realistic as you suggest, we’d never buy anything.” I could not disagree more, and this attitude contains within it two related mistakes.

According to recent reports, one, why would having very realistic underwriting cost you deals? The only thing costing you deals is a return threshold higher than the market will allow. Is it better to have aggressive underwriting and unrealistic hurdles? Is self-deception really necessary? Two, and this is key, aggressive underwriting and unrealistic hurdles can cause you to make poor choices on deals, and deal risk.

Industry observers note that as an example, if you consistently underestimate your development schedules and costs, you have a bias against finished-lot deals – because you incorrectly think you get a sufficient premium for self-development. Realism is the path to better choices about what to do, not a hindrance to doing anything.
