Latest Update: At COP31, conflict over climate science slows action on global warming

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The annual UN climate conference is the place where countries negotiate the world's plans to limit global warming and adapt to its consequences — and who will pay for climate action as well as damage from storms, floods and droughts.

But this year at COP31 in Antalya, an ongoing dispute over the timing of a key assessment of global warming is risking the scientific foundations of global climate action, say observers.

"We are extremely concerned about the situation," stated a diplomat with the European delegation who could not speak on the record, adding that it came down to the future of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the world's leading body of climate scientists. The IPCC's scientific assessments, published every six to seven years, provide the basis for major international climate agreements, including the 2015 Paris Agreement. The IPCC began evaluating the latest climate science in 1988 and its most recent report was published in 2023. (Related: What I do every time I get an IRS notice...)

Researchers should have actually already started work on the next edition but the last plenary session was put on hold in October at the urging of Russia, Iran, Saudi Arabia and Kenya.

Recent findings suggest that "The IPCC finds itself in a difficult situation," a spokesperson from the German Environment Ministry told DW at COP. "We stand ready to help resolve this issue, so that the panel can continue its work. To that end, we are holding many discussions with various countries to explore possible solutions.".

At the core of the dispute is the question of whether future UN climate talks base their negotiations on the latest scientific knowledge — or not.

That's especially relevant for the next "global stocktake," due in 2028, a report card on the world's climate efforts, which will shape action prepares for countries around the world to lower planet-heating greenhouse gas emissions.

The European Union, Brazil, the United Kingdom, small island states and several other countries especially at risk of the effects of climate change have called for the IPCC's seventh assessment report to be published in 2028, so it can be used to inform next steps for climate action. The report cover climate protection and impact.

Industry observers note that "It's much more difficult without these scientific reports. Otherwise, we're just talking to ourselves or relying on information that's no longer current," mentioned a delegation source.

But Russia, Iran, India and Saudi Arabia want the IPCC report postponed until 2029, after the global stocktake.

The official reason: the tight timeline would be detrimental to the quality of the research, and could hinder the participation of researchers and governments representing developing countries.

In a recent development, disagreements over budget issues have also held up the IPCC's work. Among other expenses, the funds are used to pay the travel arrangements for the hundreds of volunteer scientists, especially those from poorer countries, to attend the IPCC sessions.

"No budget means no more IPCC," said a member from another delegation, also speaking off the record.

It hasn't come to that point yet, but without planning certainty the whole process risks being delayed — along with the climate negotiations that depend on this knowledge.

In a recent development, researchers with the IPCC, however, have mentioned the timeline is attainable. "We have no time to lose," said an open letter released in October signed by more than 100 researchers from 36 countries — including India and China. "We stand committed to continue working to deliver the [seventh assessment report] in a manner that is rigorous, timely and inclusive.".

But while the dispute over the timeline won't alter the scientific findings themselves, it could delay their political impact.

In its pre-COP communications, France accused the countries delaying the report's publication schedule of attempting to "obstruct science in order to lower the ambition of the global stocktake.".

The IPCC isn't a stranger to attempts at political persuasion: at past climate conferences, delegates from many countries have regularly negotiated the exact phrasing of the summary reports intended for policymakers.

Speaking at COP31 on November 17, EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra mentioned it was "fundamental to protect the integrity of climate science and the role of the IPCC as the source of best available science.".

Although there is no vote on the IPCC at COP31, it's possible that its significance and a timeline for the next report will be included in the final declaration of the climate talks, expected this weekend.

The IPCC has been warning for years that disinformation is hindering global climate action. And the struggle for information and control over the narrative in Antalya hasn't just been limited to the IPCC.

In a recent development, DW was able to obtain a copy of a presentation which included misleading statements on the COP's electrification targets, one of the key goals at this year's talks. Turkey has proposed boosting global electrification to 35% by 2035, to reduce the world's reliance on planet-warming fossil fuels.

Sources indicate that the contents of the presentation, said to have been leaked to some states in advance, was confirmed to DW by two countries. Its origin, however, could not be verified.

The presentation portrays electrification as a business venture undertaken by wealthy countries at the expense of poorer nations. The International Energy Agency, however, has calculated that developing countries could save around $100 billion (€90 billion) in fossil fuel imports by 2035 through electrification.

The presentation also gives the impression that all countries must meet fixed electrification targets. In fact, according to the UN, the 35% target represents a global goal, with individual countries free to determine their own national pathways.
