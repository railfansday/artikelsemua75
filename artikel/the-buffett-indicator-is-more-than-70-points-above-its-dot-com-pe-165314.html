Sources indicate that in a 2001 Fortune article, Warren Buffett publicly mentioned the idea of gauging the stock market's valuation by comparing the country's total equity market capitalization to its GDP. This concept was introduced just after the technology bubble burst. (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

Industry observers note that before that, at the dot-com peak in the spring of 2000, the Buffett indicator was just under 150%. This was a level that suggested the stock market was extremely overvalued. That proved to be the correct view; Over the following decade, the S&P 500 (^GSPC +4.73%) produced a negative single-digit total return. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

Relative to what was going on at the turn of the century, the current Buffett indicator of 222%, more than 70 percentage points above the dot-com record, is in the stratosphere. It's not far off the highest reading ever from three months ago in August. Investors have definitely been concerned.

Here's how to interpret the Buffett indicator as 2026 nears its end.

If you perfectly mistimed your investment allocation and bought an S&P 500 exchange-traded fund, such as the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY +4.71%) in early 2000 when the Buffett indicator was around 150%, as mentioned, your portfolio suffered by losing money in the subsequent decade. The stock market was performing well prior to the housing market meltdown, however, a generational event that led to a severe economic crisis. It's impossible to have predicted this would happen.

Industry observers note that nonetheless, investors might be skeptical that buying stocks is the right move today. With the Buffett indicator at 222%, what does this mean for the equity market over the next 10 years?

All else equal, history says that high starting valuations result in lower forward returns. The tech bubble is the clearest example. This is true even though past trends aren't guaranteed to repeat.

Sources indicate that according to research from J.P. Morgan (JPM +1.97%) Asset Management, the bank believes that U.S. large-cap stocks will produce an annualized total return of 7.5% in the next 10 to 15 years. That's not alarming, but it's significantly below the 14.7% yearly pace of the prior decade (as of Nov. 18). And considering above-normal inflation, the real projected return is lower.

Sources indicate that but investors should understand the Buffett indicator's limitations. It ignores revenues that businesses collect outside the U.S., as this doesn't get captured in the GDP figure that's in the denominator. The biggest companies, especially those that operate in the technology sector, have substantial foreign operations, which naturally skews the Buffett indicator higher.

It has been revealed that investors shouldn't be deterred. The right response to this information is still to build a diversified portfolio. If you can't help but be worried, then maybe it's best to increase your cash position. This will enable you to take advantage of opportunities that come up. And of course, always focus on buying high-quality names.

According to recent reports, if you're someone who's staring at a multi-decade time horizon until retirement, it's recommended that you stay optimistic and bullish over the long term. Over any 20-year rolling period, the S&P 500 index has generated a positive return.

Waiting for a cheaper valuation seems like the right move. However, you shouldn't try to time the market. When the Buffett indicator was first thought of, a reading over 120% might have been considered expensive. Today, it's plausible that a Buffett indicator of over 200% is normal.

Industry observers note that the nature of the monetary landscape is worth exploring. Since 2000, the M2 money supply in the U.S. has ballooned more than 400%. Furthermore, the U.S. federal debt has increased 7x during that same period. I think these are underappreciated tailwinds. There's no way that all of this liquidity, which supports risk assets and nominal earnings growth, hasn't had a profound impact on the stock market. Looking ahead, that trend will continue to be a constant propellant.

Be mindful of the Buffett indicator. But don't let it distract you. Time should continue to work in long-term investors' favor.
