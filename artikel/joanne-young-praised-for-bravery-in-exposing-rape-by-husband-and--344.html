Woman who waived anonymity says she hopes her case will mean other victims of abuse are more likely to come forward.

A woman who was drugged and raped by her husband and strangers he had invited to their family home has been praised for her bravery in exposing the crimes.

Industry observers note that the judge in the case and women’s rights advocates said Joanne Young’s courage had been a feature of the trial. Her decision to waive her anonymity and attend court has drawn comparisons to Gisèle Pelicot, who was drugged and raped by her husband as well as dozens of strangers in France. In a statement outside court on Thursday, Young said the convictions meant “that those who find themselves facing what I have are more likely to come forward, and more likely to have faith in the criminal justice system”. (Related: Porting fairseq wmt19 translation system to transformer...)

In a recent development, saying that she now lived “with a pain that’s hard to put into words”, Young added: “Consent has to come from the person themselves. No one should be able to give it on your behalf, and no one should be able to rely on someone else’s word that you agreed. At no point did anyone ask me for my consent. I urge the government to look at this as a priority so that women are properly protected in the future.”.

All survivors of sexual abuse are granted automatic lifelong anonymity and only they are able to waive that right. Women’s rights activists appeared outside Winchester crown court for the trial of the men accused of sexually assaulting Young, brandishing banners such as “Shame must change sides” and “A drugged woman never consents”.

In a recent development, on Thursday, four men were convicted by jury of various sexual offences against Joanne Young. Her ex-husband, Philip Young, pleaded guilty in January to more than 50 offences, including raping and drugging his then wife.

Her daughter Alex has also now called for a change in the law on consent to make it clear that third-party consent cannot be used as an argument in a court of law.

She wrote on LinkedIn: “I am calling for the Sexual Offences Act 2003 to be looked at and updated. We live in a world where there is debate over whether third-party consent is enough to have sex with someone. We live in a world where someone’s partner can consent for them, and that is enough to use it as an argument in a court of law. This is simply unacceptable.”.

Jess Phillips, a former minister for safeguarding and violence against women and girls, has suggested online safety laws should be updated to prevent these cases from happening in future.

The Labour MP for Birmingham Yardley told LBC: “The ability of these men to find each other on websites that you and I could just log on to now – it just means that these cases are going to keep on coming.”.

After the verdicts on Thursday, women’s rights groups and others heaped praise on Joanne Young for her bravery. Farah Nazeer, the chief executive of Women’s Aid, mentioned: “We stand in solidarity with Joanne Young – a woman of extraordinary bravery who has chosen to waive her right to anonymity when giving evidence against her former husband, who drugged her and invited other men to sexually assault her.

Recent findings suggest that “Chemical coercion is a horrific crime, one that leaves women questioning their own sense of reality and sanity, just as the men they trust abuse them, coerce them and lie to them. The bravery of women like Joanne, and Gisèle Pelicot in France, have been fundamental in exposing this crime and the men that commit it.”.

Ciara Bergman, the chief executive of Rape Crisis England & Wales, praised Young’s “profoundly courageous” decision to waive her anonymity but emphasised that there was “no right or wrong way to react after rape or sexual abuse, just as there’s no right or wrong decision when it comes to reporting it”.

In a recent development, she stated: “Survivors of sexual violence and abuse impress us with their courage in millions of ways, whether or not they report what happened to the police … For those who pursue criminal justice, as Joanne has done, reporting to the police and giving evidence in court can call for particular kinds of courage: the tenacity to see a case through to trial, to see the defendant and their supporters in court, and to speak about deeply painful experiences in graphic detail in a room full of strangers. And the strength to carry on with everyday life while waiting for an outcome they can’t control.”.

Sources indicate that janaya Walker, a co-executive director of End Violence Against Women, said: “We extend our solidarity to Joanne Young, who has shown incredible courage in this case, including the difficult decision she has made to waive her anonymity. Whilst society may wish to think that such horrors are a total deviation from the norm, they demonstrate the horrifying reality that the greatest danger to women is often inside their own home.”.

The Labour peer Thangam Debbonaire told the BBC’s Newsnight the statement outside court was “extraordinary”, adding: “ I feel really moved by her … She is an extraordinary example, and the fact that she has done what she has done, for women, and she mentioned that so clearly, is really moving.”.

Young briefly gave evidence in person at the trial, glancing occasionally towards the defendants in the dock. At one point, Mr Justice Saini asked her to keep her voice up. Much of her evidence was given in police interviews that were filmed and shown to the jury.

James Foster, a specialist prosecutor in the Crown Prosecution Service’s Wessex complex casework unit, said: “Joanne Young’s decision to waive her anonymity and attend this trial speaks volumes about her courage and resolve … she sat in court [and] listened to the most intimate and distressing details of what was done to her while she was drugged and unaware.”.

Foster said waiving anonymity was an entirely personal decision with no right or wrong choice. He said he wanted to reassure the public that victims of sexual offences retained full entitlement to complete anonymity when reporting crimes.
