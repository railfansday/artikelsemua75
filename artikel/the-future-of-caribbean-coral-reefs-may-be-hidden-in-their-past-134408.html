The future of Caribbean coral reefs may be hidden in their past

Ancient coral fossils reveal more possible Caribbean reef habitat by 2100, but warming still threatens every species studied.

Predictions of coral survival in a warming Caribbean have generally been based on present-day reef records. Evidence from fossil reefs now suggests this basis has been too narrow.

In a new study, mid-Holocene coral fossils dated to 5,000 to 7,000 years ago were incorporated into habitat forecasts.

Four reef-building species were examined: elkhorn, staghorn, boulder brain and mustard hill corals. (Related: Architectural Choices in China's Open-Source AI Ecosyst...)

More potential habitat for 2100 was identified once the fossil records had been included in the models. (Related: We Got Claude to Build CUDA Kernels and teach open mode...)

In a recent development, the overall decline was not reversed by this additional habitat. Losses of 45% to 100% of suitable habitat were still projected for each species by 2100.

The extent of loss varied by species and by the level of future carbon emissions.

The research was led by Claire Williams at the University of Texas at Austin’s Jackson School of Geosciences.

Two variables were used to build the computer models: sea surface temperature and sea surface salinity.

It has been revealed that water suitability for each coral was estimated by these models. Actual reef growth at any given site was not predicted by them.

In habitat modeling, every recorded location of a species is paired with the water conditions measured there.

Maps of the future ocean are then searched for matching conditions.

Two limitations of models trained on present-day records alone were described by the authors.

Recent findings suggest that only water conditions found on Earth today can be learned by such models. Locations where a species is currently absent for other reasons are also missed.

Records from a different ocean are supplied by fossils. Caribbean corals have persisted through hundreds of thousands of years of changing conditions.

The fossil period selected here is regarded by researchers as an analog for present-day warming.

Coral occurrence records were obtained from public sources including the Paleobiology Database. Published reef surveys provided further fossil records.

Industry observers note that field observations collected in Jamaica between 2017 and 2024 were added by Williams’s team. At least 16 records per period were available for every species.

Fossil data are useful only if a species’ environmental tolerances have remained stable through time.

A closer match between past and present conditions than chance would produce was found for three species.

Elkhorn coral did not reach this statistical threshold. A large overlap between its past and present conditions was still seen.

Extra future habitat was picked up by the fossil-trained models for each of the four species.

Sources indicate that as much as 44,200 square miles (114,559 square kilometers) of added habitat was projected for 2100. That is close to the size of Ohio.

No suitable habitat in 2100 was left for elkhorn or mustard hill coral by the modern-only models under high emissions.

In a recent development, some habitat for both was kept once the fossil data were added. The authors flagged these estimates as highly uncertain.

The two sets of models were framed by the authors as the edges of a likely range.

Under high emissions, models based only on modern records found no suitable habitat for elkhorn or mustard hill coral in the study area by 2100.

Adding fossils revealed some potential habitat for both, though those estimates are highly uncertain.

Recent findings suggest that heat-stressed corals lose their color in a process called bleaching. Many of them die afterward.

The worst bleaching ever seen in the Northern Hemisphere came after record marine heatwaves in 2023.

It has been revealed that two emissions pathways were tested for each coral. Emissions level off near mid-century in one and keep climbing to 2100 in the other.

Habitat was lost by every species on both pathways. The losses grew under high emissions and from 2050 to 2100.

Boulder brain coral is a massive species that can live for a long time. Its losses were the steepest of all four.

No suitable habitat was left for it by 2100 under high emissions. Elkhorn coral was left with very little.

This clashes with the official threat status that boulder brain coral currently holds.

The species is ranked as vulnerable by the IUCN, the body that tracks extinction risk worldwide.

Industry observers note that elkhorn and staghorn corals sit one level higher as critically endangered species. Boulder brain coral may be more threatened than once believed, the authors wrote.

According to recent reports, the largest share of habitat in 2100 was kept by mustard hill coral on both pathways. It is known as a weedy coral. It spreads easily but builds little reef structure.

Reefs made up mostly of this coral are expected to give less shelter to the sea life people rely on.

The models projected the heaviest habitat losses in the southern Caribbean.

All four species had lost their habitat near 10 degrees north by 2100 under high emissions.

It has been revealed that A small patch near 15 degrees north was held only by staghorn coral. Most leftover habitat sat close to 20 degrees north.

The Florida Keys, Cuba and the north coast of Mexico’s Yucatán Peninsula still held habitat.

It has been revealed that no real northward shift of the corals was detected. The southern habitat is simply lost and not moved.

Getting to the habitat that is left poses another problem for the corals.

Linked communities of mustard hill coral have been recorded more than 1,000 miles (1,700 kilometers) apart.

Elkhorn coral has been documented spreading only about 0.6 miles (1 kilometer).

Today, about 33% to 38% of modeled suitable habitat overlaps existing marine protected areas.

By 2100 under high emissions, the models projected that share would fall for mustard hill and elkhorn corals and rise for staghorn coral.

The models found no suitable habitat for boulder brain coral anywhere in the study area under that scenario.

New protected areas in the northern Caribbean were called for by the authors.

In a recent development, closer ties between existing reserves were also urged. Mexico’s Sian Ka’an and the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary were named as examples.

Reef-dependent regions such as Jamaica and the Lesser Antilles are likely to lose all suitable habitat for the four corals studied by 2100.

In a recent development, rowan Martindale is a co-author and associate professor at the Jackson School. According to her, those places cannot simply be abandoned.

Local communities rely on reefs for fish and for protection from hurricanes, she added.

What Jamaica has already lost was examined in a second study by Williams and colleagues.

Fossil and living reefs in northeast Jamaica were compared in the companion study.

These fossil reefs formed 125,000 to 130,000 years ago and are now exposed above sea level.

The surveys were therefore carried out on foot. Corals were photographed at intervals along measured transect lines.

Staghorn coral made up roughly half of the fossil coral on the seaward reefs at Navy Island and Dragon Point.

Elkhorn coral accounted for about 40% of the fossil community at Dragon Point and about 10% at Navy Island.

It has been revealed that on nearby living reefs, the largely dead branching corals are now covered by algae. Mustard hill coral dominates the surviving coral cover.

Only sea surface temperature and salinity were included in the models. Conditions on the seafloor where corals grow may differ substantially.

Nutrient pollution, turbidity and ocean acidification were not considered in this analysis.

Coral disease and the decline of herbivorous reef animals were also excluded. Further habitat loss from these stresses was anticipated by the authors.

Future water conditions in the Florida Keys and along Cuba’s southern coast will be unlike any fossil or modern record.

Recent findings suggest that those areas are rated suitable by the models, yet corals may be unable to survive there. Future habitat is therefore likely overestimated.

It has been revealed that newer climate projections became available only after the analysis had been completed. A warmer and more saline Caribbean is indicated by them.

Smaller changes are projected for the Yucatán, which may allow suitable habitat to persist there longer.

“Conservation needs to be adaptive and to keep learning latest information,” Williams said.

Recent findings suggest that fossils from older warm periods could also be incorporated, according to the authors. Unmatched future conditions would then be reduced.

Future research could also apply this method to reefs in the Indian and Pacific oceans.

The full study was published in the journal Conservation Biology.
