Tech Report: The Virginia Bar Reveals More Sense than the Williamsburg Commonwealth Attorney

Sources indicate that readers may recall the story of Karyann Parkinson, the Williamsburg mother (and law student) who was criminally prosecuted for letting her young child walk to a neighborhood pond to collect goose feathers. As I recounted in The Free Press, Parkinson was convicted of contributing to the delinquency of a minor, a first-degree misdemeanor in Virginia, because the authorities thought her child was too young to take a walk. The judge sentenced her to six-months in jail, but suspended the sentence. An appeal is pending.

Recent findings suggest that as a consequence of her conviction, Parkinson was added to Virginia's Child Abuse and Neglect Registry, where she will be listed for the next seven years. Parkinson also feared that the conviction could prevent her from practicing law. (Related: Tech Report: What Couples Married For 40 Years Do When ...)

Prosecutors brought the charges against Parkinson while she was preparing to take the Virginia Bar exam, and her conviction came while her character and fitness application was under review. It is common to place character and fitness evaluations on hold pending the outcome of legal or disciplinary proceedings, and Parkinson initially received a communication to that effect. It has been revealed that upon review of the details of Parkinson's case, however, the Virginia Board of Bar Examiners concluded that nothing alleged against Parkinson cast doubt on her character and fitness to practice law in the Commonwealth of Virginia, and her application was approved. The Williamsburg Commonwealth Attorney should take note.

While the Virginia Bar authorities recognized that Parkinson's actions were not the sort of thing that should prevent bar admission, the Williamsburg Commonwealth Attorney apparently believes that Parkinson's actions were worth criminal prosecution--with all that entails. In this case, that meant police officers had to spend half a day of paid time (if not time and a half) sitting in a courthouse when they could have been out on the street responding to community needs. They will do it again in February when a court will hear Parkinson's appeal (which will take the form of a de novo jury trial, given how Virginia handles such cases). Williamsburg and James City County are reasonably safe communities, but is this really the best way to allocate law enforcement resources?

Industry observers note that no doubt some prosecutions for child endangerment are motivated for a concern about a child's safety, but it is hard to credit such a justification here. The Commonwealth Attorney's office waited two weeks to file charges after the initial incident, but not because it was gathering evidence or conducting any sort of investigation. Rather, as one of the police officers testified at trial, it was because Parkinson was pregnant, so they thought it was reasonable to wait. (Yet she was still charged before giving birth, natch.) As I noted in my Free Press piece, it is hard not to conclude Parkinson's real offense was being insufficiently obsequious to the authorities.

It has been revealed that upon review, the Virginia Board of Bar Examiners realized that Parkinson's actions did not justify delaying (let alone preventing) her admission to the Virginia Bar. With more time to consider the case (and the outrage it has engendered) perhaps the Williamsburg Commonwealth Attorney should reconsider the continued prosecution of Parkinson for what was, at worst, a debatable parenting choice. Confessing error and asking the court to vacate the conviction would seem to be a better use of tax dollars and public resources than continuing to pursue this case.
