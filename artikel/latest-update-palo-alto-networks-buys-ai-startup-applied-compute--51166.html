Latest Update: Palo Alto Networks buys AI startup Applied Compute for $4.5bn in stock

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Palo Alto Networks’ office in Singapore.

It has been revealed that palo Alto Networks has agreed to buy Applied Compute in an all-stock deal valued at $4.5bn. The startup helps companies train and run their own versions of open-weight AI models. Palo Alto confirmed the deal on Wednesday, minutes after The Wall Street Journal’s Kate Clark reported it. It has been revealed that applied Compute was founded last year by three former OpenAI researchers. Earlier this month it raised $350m at a $3.25bn valuation, the Journal reported. It also mentioned it had reached $100m in run-rate revenue. Its co-founder and chief executive, Yash Patil, will become Palo Alto’s co-chief AI officer when the deal closes. The deal still needs regulatory clearance and should close in Palo Alto’s fiscal third quarter of 2027.

“Every enterprise is going to run a mix of open and closed models, and every one of those models needs to be secured,” said Nikesh Arora, Palo Alto’s chairman and chief executive. (Related: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Faster Language M...)

Open-weight models let users download and change the weights, the numbers that set how a model behaves. Palo Alto said no large provider offers them securely today. It prepares to let companies train, adapt and run them in the public cloud or in their own sovereign environments. Applied Compute will keep serving its own customers, and Palo Alto will sell the platform to its own base.

Arora told the Journal the deal means “venturing outside of cybersecurity”. On the earnings call, he said open-weight models now carry more than 60% of token traffic. A year ago it was under 5%.

“If it works, I think this will dwarf the ability that we have in cybersecurity. This could be bigger than what we do,” Arora said on the call.

It has been revealed that palo Alto told analysts it expects the deal to add more than $1bn in annual recurring revenue by fiscal 2029. It intends to build more than 100 megawatts of inference capacity. Capital spending on that will stay under $3bn through fiscal 2030, it said.

It has been revealed that arora also addressed whether Applied Compute’s team will stay. Its staff have “millions of reasons” to stay for the next three to four years, he wrote on X. They will keep their offices and, for the most part, their independence.

The deal came with Palo Alto’s fiscal first-quarter results. Revenue rose 39% to $3.44bn in the three months to 31 October. Annual recurring revenue from its next-generation security business rose 66% to $9.73bn. Net income was $482m, up from $334m a year earlier.

Palo Alto raised its forecasts for the year. It now expects revenue of $14.52bn to $14.57bn. It sees adjusted earnings of $4.26 to $4.30 a share.

Recent findings suggest that on the call, Arora linked part of that demand to the recent run of outages. He blamed them partly on IT teams rushing to patch AI-found flaws. He has argued before that AI prices must fall for the technology to take off. He has also called slowing AI down unrealistic.
