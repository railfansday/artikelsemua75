NTT and SSC Space have signed a memorandum of understanding to study how optical ground stations in different countries could work as one network. The companies will look at whether a satellite can send data by laser to stations run on different systems and how those stations can pass the data into terrestrial networks. The agreement, signed November 11, is a study of interoperability and commercial requirements, not the launch of a joint service.

The two companies bring different pieces of the system. NTT supplies optical networking and network control expertise. SSC Space operates satellite ground infrastructure and brings experience coordinating ground-station operations. According to recent reports, A satellite can collect more data in a pass than it has time to transmit to Earth. Optical links can move more data during a contact window, but they need precise pointing and a clear enough path through the atmosphere. A spacecraft with a fast laser downlink can still miss its chance to transmit if the receiving telescope is under heavy cloud. That matters especially in low Earth orbit, where a spacecraft moves in and out of view of a given station. (Related: My Journey to a serverless transformers pipeline on Goo...)

Recent findings suggest that one answer is to spread stations across regions with different weather. If a suitable station is in view and clear, an operator can route a contact there instead of waiting for another pass over the cloudy site. NASA’s Laser Communications Relay Demonstration illustrates the approach: its optical relay connected to ground stations in California and Hawaii, both chosen for high altitude and low cloud cover. The experiments found that some thin high clouds were tolerable, but still reinforced the case for having more than one place to receive a signal.

SSC Space’s existing optical program gives the project a starting point. Its initial sites in Australia and Chile are at existing radio-frequency facilities, with access to terrestrial fiber and the option to support both radio and optical links. SSC Space lists up to 10 gigabits per second for each station. That figure describes the stations’ stated capability, not the expected performance of the proposed joint service.

In a recent development, NTT has also been working on what happens once a station receives the signal. In a separate agreement with Cailabs, it stated it would study connecting selected ground stations to nearby data centers through its All-Photonics Network and coordinating both ground station and terrestrial network resources. Unlike NTT’s separate lunar work with ispace, which includes power transmission, the SSC Space agreement addresses receiving data from spacecraft and getting it onto networks on Earth.

In a recent development, for owners of satellite imagery, an important measure is the time between an image being captured and its arrival in software that can use it. A higher peak downlink rate helps when the link is available. A reliable choice of stations matters when a satellite has upcoming imagery but the planned receiver cannot see it through the clouds.

In a recent development, NASA’s work using satellite data and machine learning to help forecast flash floods is one example of why timely delivery matters. That project does not use the proposed NTT–SSC Space network, and neither company announced an AI system as part of the agreement. The connection is upstream: the sooner a ground network can deliver useful observations, the sooner forecasting and analysis tools can process them.

The companies have not named a first mission, service start date or service availability target. The test of the proposal is whether an operator can use more than one station without building a separate integration for each site, and whether that flexibility reliably shortens the wait for data when a planned contact falls through.
