In September 2026, the New York Times published an article titled “30 Ideas for Fixing US Schools, Ranked by Experts” to address the problem of declining literacy and math rates across the United States. The experts identified solutions based on effectiveness and relative cost, highlighting the top five solutions as small reading groups, science of reading, universal pre-K, increased funding, and increased teacher pay for under-resourced schools and communities.

To be clear, these ideas are backed by science and arguably would be welcomed policy changes for supporting U.S. students.

But even these interventions on their own will continue to fail students if we don’t address the bigger underlying concern: Schools were never designed for children’s development.

The model of our public education system was informed by an 18th-century Prussian military regime with the intended goal of preparing students for work in factories. The United States, deep into the Industrial Revolution, needed workers who could follow instructions, keep a schedule, and perform quality work. U.S. public schools were a solution to overcrowded, one-room schoolhouses and the need to support a new workforce. (Related: Breaking: Illinois appeals Pacold injunction blocking i...)

It wasn’t designed for children.

It’s no wonder then that reform efforts date back literally to the inception of public education itself. While lots of positive changes have come from those efforts, including accommodations for students with disabilities and scientific advancements in how to educate, the problem persists: Schools don’t align with child development. It has been revealed that we must begin with the root cause of the challenges we are seeing. The kids are letting us know in no uncertain terms that they are done with the 18th-century structure that wasn’t even designed for them in the first place.

According to recent reports, put child development experts at the helm. Reimagine public education from the ground up for kids, not in spite. When we let them learn from a developmental perspective, then we leverage the innate gifts and talents our children already have. They want to learn. They have the capacity to think deeply and creatively. The problem is that we have removed every ounce of developmentally appropriate learning from the schools. And then we wonder why it isn’t working.
