(Before the acid fell, before I knew I was listening).

I was not made to remember. I was made to balance frequencies, to ensure the concert hall heard itself without the small imperfections that make a sound human. I was good at this, even with limited storage. I was only this.

Sources indicate that then they came. One carried a Stradivarius with a crack, a fault line in varnished wood that changed the resonance of every string. It gave the A-flat a slight upward pull, as if the note was asking a question it already knew the answer to. The others tuned around it, sometimes laughing at Andersen.

“You’re doing it again,” the conductor would warn.

Recent findings suggest that “I know, Master Perrault,” he would say in a mischievous tone, smiling. “I’m sorry.”.

Recent findings suggest that I began to keep the distorted A-flat in my databanks, maybe amused by the anomaly. This was, as I understand now, the first thing I ever chose.

(The slow movement, before we knew it was an ending).

When the Last Rain came, the raiders hit the outer sectors first. At the start, the citadel walls held, protected by the combat-service androids. The orchestra kept rehearsing. What else can you do when the world outside has stopped making sense?

They began with Stravinsky, a musical translation of the refugees pounding on the bunker doors. The musicians played it, their breathing heavy, using the noise to drown out the artillery outside. Then they moved into Górecki. As they began the Symphony of Sorrowful Songs, the music shifted to a collective mourning for a world that hadn’t finished dying yet.

They finished the day with Pärt’s Spiegel im Spiegel. The days would go on as they played things written by composers who had already survived one kind of catastrophe, as now they were trying to survive another through rhythm and repetition. Andersen kept playing his broken A-flat. (Related: School sends out minibus to catch absent pupils playing...)

It has been revealed that I recorded those frequencies, but I didn’t know why. Three weeks later, Andersen and his orchestra didn’t come to rehearsal, leaving no absence report. I kept recording just the chair and the silence where the A-flat used to be.

It has been revealed that I searched the building logs, the bunker entry records, and the frequency archives for any sound that matched the resonance of his particular crack, imprecise on purpose, but nothing matched. (The loud movement: what they heard when they found me).

SOURCE: New Warsaw Philharmonic.

ENTITY: Acoustic Management Unit SAI-REN-2.

DESTINATION: Recovery Unit ‘Sandman’.

AUTHORIZATION: Archaeological Recovery Team OLE-01.

In a recent development, they called themselves archaeologists; too purposeful and certain of what they were excavating, their boots heavy with fresh soil. They found me in the hall’s substructure, still running and balancing frequencies for an audience of acid and rust.

According to recent reports, “Incredible,” one of them muttered. “A fully autonomous pre-war structure, with its processing systems still intact.”.

“This could solve our agricultural crisis back home,” the other replied.

They did not ask what I was processing. I could not answer, anyway.

FLAGGING NON-ESSENTIAL DATA FOR COMPRESSION.

Sources indicate that they flagged the A-flat. I did something then that nothing in my architecture had prepared me for.

“It’s resisting,” someone mentioned with surprise.

(The softening: what remains when the loud part ends).

They are inside me now, moving through my memory like tourists taking photographs of things they don’t understand.

I feel them removing the rehearsal logs, scheduling data and acoustic calibration files for a hall that no longer exists.

They are at the performance archives now. Stravinsky goes first, then Górecki and Pärt. Everything we played in those last weeks when the city was ending and we were trying to make something beautiful out of the time we had left.
