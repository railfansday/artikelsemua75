Industry News: Buying From a Private Seller? Try a Private-Party Auto Loan Before a Personal Loan

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

For a used car from a private seller, ask a credit union or your bank for a private-party auto loan. The car secures it, so it usually costs less than a personal loan, and the lender can pay off any loan the seller still owes. Save the personal loan for a car too old or cheap to qualify.

It has been revealed that A seller you found on Facebook Marketplace or through a friend won’t arrange financing the way a dealer would. The loan is yours to line up, and the kind you choose changes the rate you pay.

Recent findings suggest that an auto lender can charge less than a personal lender because the car is collateral, which it can repossess if you stop paying. Say you borrow $10,000 of a $14,000 price over five years, at 8% on an auto loan or 11% on a personal loan. The auto loan runs about $203 a month and the personal loan about $217, so the auto loan costs about $880 less in total.

Before you shop, find a lender that makes private-party loans, since not all do. Bank of America, for one, finances only dealer purchases. Then get pre-approved, so you know your rate and how much you can spend, and check the lender’s limits. Many lenders cap the car’s age and mileage and set a minimum loan, so a high-mileage older car or a loan of a couple of thousand dollars may not qualify. Once you’ve chosen the car, the lender will want a copy of the title and a bill of sale, plus a payoff quote from the seller’s lender if the seller still owes on the car. Have your lender pay the seller’s lender directly and write the seller a check for the rest, so that loan is cleared before the title is signed over to you.

Industry observers note that A personal loan is the better tool when the car falls outside those limits, the seller won’t wait for that paperwork, or you’d rather not have a lender listed on the title until the loan is paid. Either way, pay an independent mechanic to inspect the car, and check its title history with a report from a provider the Justice Department lists at vehiclehistory.gov.
