📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, start as a virtual assistant by selling one service you’ve done in a past job, such as managing an inbox and calendar or keeping the books, instead of offering help with anything. Price it by the hour or as a flat monthly package, and pitch it first to people who already know your work.

Business owners hire a virtual assistant to hand off a specific task. An offer that names the task and its price is easy to accept, while “I can help with anything” leaves the owner to work out both. (Related: Breaking: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons in a...)

According to recent reports, your past jobs tell you which service to sell. A former executive assistant can manage an owner’s inbox and calendar, an office manager can keep a small company’s books, and someone from a call center can answer customer emails and chats. (Related: Supermicro sued by shareholders over claims Nvidia exec...)

Recent findings suggest that base your price on what employers pay for the same work. In May 2025, the Bureau of Labor Statistics put the median employee wage at $21.53 an hour for customer service representatives, $23.23 for secretaries and administrative assistants, and $24.36 for bookkeeping, accounting, and auditing clerks. Set your rate above the figure for your kind of work. As a contractor, you pay self-employment tax, both the employer’s and the employee’s share of Social Security and Medicare, and nobody pays you for sick days or hours spent finding clients.

Charge by the hour for work that comes and goes. For weekly tasks, sell a flat monthly package that spells out what it covers. Say a former office manager keeps the books for three local contractors, about two hours a week each, for a flat $350 a month per client. That’s about $40 an hour and $1,050 a month.

Sources indicate that pitch former bosses, coworkers, and clients first, since they’ve seen your work. Then look for clients through local business groups, like your chamber of commerce, and freelance marketplaces. Put each client on a short contract that lists the tasks, the rate, when you’re paid, and how either side can end it.

Industry observers note that the Federal Trade Commission warns about fake virtual assistant jobs that pay with a check. The fake employer asks you to deposit it, keep part as your pay, and forward the rest to another person. Then the check bounces, and your bank wants the full amount back. A real client never asks you to forward part of a payment.
