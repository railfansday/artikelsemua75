It was an unprecedented year for El Niño, the weather-changing ocean pattern defined by warmer-than-normal surface waters in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific. By October 18, the ocean area that researchers use to monitor El Niño’s severity climbed to an average temperature of over 30° Celsius (86° Fahrenheit) for the first time in history. From there, it kept on rising.

It has been revealed that in September, the region’s daily average sea surface temperature reached the most extreme level ever recorded: more than 3.03 degrees Celsius above the 30-year average (dashed line). The World Meteorological Organization predicts that sea surface temperatures may peak at 3.7 degrees above that average by January. The two previous strongest El Niños on record emerged in 1997 and 2015.

Industry observers note that all that hot water has fueled the most active Pacific hurricane season on record. By mid-October, 331 units of cyclone energy had accumulated, beating out 2018’s record of 318 units. It has also unleashed heat into the atmosphere, affecting distant circulation patterns and leading to an extraordinarily quiet Atlantic hurricane season.
