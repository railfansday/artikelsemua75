📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When John Hamburg worked on “Meet the Parents” as a writer over 25 years ago, it seemed like there were well-resourced comedies opening in theaters every week. Returning to the series this year for the third sequel, “Focker In-Law,” which he directed as well as wrote, the filmmaker was fully aware that major screen comedies had become a rare treat. “We felt like it had been so long since this type of comedy had been in movie theaters that we wanted to do everything we could to make it feel theatrical,” Hamburg told IndieWire.

It has been revealed that to that end, Hamburg and cinematographer Barry Peterson decided to shoot widescreen for the first time in Focker history, eschewing the 1.85:1 frame established by director Jay Roach in the first film (and perpetuated in Roach’s “Meet the Rockers” in 2004 and Paul Weitz’s “Little Fockers” in 2010) in favor of a more expansive 2.40:1 aspect ratio. It’s a pivotal decision that not only gives “Focker In-Law” a visual elegance, but showcases the ensemble performances in compositions that make the most out of the subtle interchanges and reactions between characters, often registered with the subtlety that comes from allowing the comedy to play out within the frame rather than via emphatic cuts. For Hamburg, it’s all at the service of extracting the best possible work from his actors, whether it be “Meet the Parents” stalwarts like Ben Stiller, Robert De Niro, and Blythe Danner or newcomers to the series like Skyler Gisondo (as Stiller’s son) or Ariana Grande (who fulfills the comic promise of her turn in “Wicked” with a hilarious performance as Gisondo’s fiancée). “I try to create a very loose, stress-free environment,” Hamburg said. “I’ve found that in these comedies, if the actors are laughing and enjoying each other’s manufacturer and having fun with the crew, and there’s no tension, they can do their best work.”. Hamburg claims that his intention is to direct like he’s hosting a really fun dinner party, a process that begins with the table read. “You get to know the actors if you have the luxury of some rehearsal, which we did on this movie,” he said. “We do table reads, and then my version of rehearsing is the actors coming to my office, where we read the scenes and talk. Pretty swiftly you get a sense of how each actor approaches their work; there are actors who color code their character’s arc in the script and are very precise, and others who go by feel and instinct. You have to be part psychologist and figure out what all the actors need, which is not anything they ever teach you in film school, but it’s a big part of the job of being director.”.

Sources indicate that another part of the job is structuring the coverage to take maximum advantage of each actor’s strengths — no small task in a movie that often has over a half-dozen characters interacting in comic set-pieces. “There’s no science to it,” Hamburg mentioned. “It’s going with your gut and feeling it out and seeing, okay, this actor needs a little more time, so maybe we’ll start with the wide shots and wait on the close-ups until the actor has really found his or her rhythm.”.

It has been revealed that hamburg likens shooting the ensemble comedy scenes to conducting a symphony, only with actors instead of musicians. “ You have to make sure that you’re not losing sight of giving all of them their moments,” Hamburg stated. “There are a lot of angles, and a lot of eyelines. I need Ariana looking at Ben across the table, Ariana looking at Skyler, De Niro looking at Ben and looking at his wife … luckily I have a great script supervisor who sits next to me, and I can ask ‘Did we get that moment? Did we get that look?’ She makes sure that I’m not missing anything, because it’s too much for one person to keep in their head.”.

Industry observers note that although scenes like an early dinner involving nearly every major character in the film are a beast to shoot, Hamburg says he enjoys them. “Maybe it’s the masochist in me,” he remarked, adding that what he really likes are seeing the new options and possibilities the actors bring to his material. “ I’m very open with the writing. We work really hard on the script and make it as good as possible, and but then on the day, you want to be surprised. I want to start with the script and make sure we get that rhythm, but then let’s see what else happens.”.

The ability to pivot and incorporate latest material based on the actors’ interaction is why Hamburg likes the dual role of writer-director. “There’s so much magic that can happen on the set when you have the right actors and the right chemistry between them,” he stated. “That’s the thrill of being not just the screenwriter, but also being the director. It’s my favorite thing.”.

A Universal Pictures release, “Focker In-Law” opens in theaters on November 25.
