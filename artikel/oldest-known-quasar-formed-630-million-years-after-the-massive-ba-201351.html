Oldest known quasar formed 630 million years after the massive bang

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that only four months after setting its own record, the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Euclid space telescope has outdone itself yet again. In a study published today in the journal Astronomy and Astrophysics, ESA researchers announce the discovery of the most distant—and therefore oldest—quasar ever observed. The object now classified as J1504+6252 formed just 630 million years after the substantial bang, making it 40 million years older than the quasar Euclid spotted in July. Euclid’s newly introduced image is equivalent to viewing J1504+6252 as it existed about 13.1 billion years ago, when the universe was only five percent its current age.

Recent findings suggest that A quasar is the result of a galactic core’s supermassive black hole inhaling cosmic gas and dust while simultaneously spewing tremendous amounts of energy. During this comparatively brief phase in a galaxy’s lifecycle, the nucleus can actually shine brighter than the entire system itself. But even such a luminous object like quasar J1504+6252 can be hard to spot when it is located deep within space-time.

However, Euclid excels at finding these and other elusive objects. The ESA launched its space telescope in 2023 to examine the mysterious realm of dark matter and dark energy. Despite estimates that these forces account for about 95 percent of the universe, astronomers still know very little about them. Identifying subjects like quasars allows researchers invaluable opportunities to learn more about dark matter and dark energy, as well as their roles in the early, formative eons of the cosmos.

Industry observers note that J1504+6252 is so young and gargantuan that it’s confounding astronomers. Based on their initial assessments, it’s unclear how a quasar with a mass equivalent to about 360 million suns even formed so early in cosmic history. It also has the largest redshift on record. Redshifting occurs when light waves stretch towards the infrared spectrum as they travel through the universe. The greater the redshift, the older its source is. In barely 1.5 years, Euclid has flagged 31 quasars, including 12 beyond a redshift of 7.

It has been revealed that J1504+6252 is the first quasar to possess a redshift beyond 8. Despite its age, J1504+6252 is devouring material at a comparatively slow pace. This implies it attained its size during unprecedentedly fast growth spurts early in its life—a feature that challenges existing models of black hole formation. It has been revealed that astronomers have nicknamed the quasar Dún Aonghasa to honor its age and location. The actual Dún Aonghasa is a prehistoric clifftop fort in Ireland overlooking the Atlantic Ocean. Like the ancient structure itself, J1504+6252 is situated at the edge of the visible cosmos. That said, astronomers expect Euclid to find multiple more quasars beyond redshift 8 in the years to come.
