The best part of five hours after the final whistle blew at the Etihad Stadium and about 150 miles north east, Noni Madueke scored an equaliser to ensure the day ended with the equation ultimately unchanged. Manchester City will still go to Arsenal on Sunday six points behind the reigning champions. They could reduce the gap to three, though the evidence of recent weeks is that it is likelier to stretch to nine.

That would surely end City’s title challenge. The theory had been that it would take a potential punishment for their 114 guilty verdicts to do that and, while City are appealing, a sizeable sanction may be still to come. Perhaps that is fitting: the title could be decided on the pitch, rather than in the courts. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

The temptation is to attribute a slide of three defeats and a draw in four games to off-field matters. Enzo Maresca has denied that is a factor, and whatever his players say in the future, that is overly simplistic. (Related: GTA 6 players report game-breaking crashes on PS5 and X...)

Recent findings suggest that rather, there are similarities with another side who spiralled from a 100 percent start into a wretched run: Liverpool won their first five matches last season, and then lost four in a row, while City began with seven straight victories a year later. In each case, the initial results came with evidence of flaws which did not halt them: until, suddenly, they did.

Industry observers note that neither looked solid defensively or always had the right balance in midfield even when winning. In City’s case, Maresca has lamented a lack of clean sheets, with none in their last nine matches in all competitions, with 26 goals already conceded in the campaign. A new manager does not seem to know his best back four. His habit of making half-time substitutions, with nine in the last nine matches, can show a decisiveness or an inability to find the right formula before then. Some of that is understandable, as Maresca gets used to a squad that was revamped in the summer.

Sources indicate that the Italian started by impressing with his tactical acumen. Three months into the campaign, however, his team look too susceptible to the counter-attack and offer too little control in midfield. Perhaps it is unfair to criticise Maresca for being too over-reliant on Erling Haaland for goals; it should be a broader concern that, beyond Haaland, Elliot Anderson, Iliman Ndiaye and Rayan Cherki, not too many others are enjoying a fine season personally.

In a recent development, maresca returned to the Etihad Stadium aware of pertinent precedents. He has raised the subject of Manchester United after Sir Alex Ferguson and Arsenal after Arsene Wenger. Neither David Moyes nor Unai Emery mounted a title challenge after replacing a legend. When City touched stratospheric heights under Guardiola, there was always the probability they would decline after him, regardless of the scale of their funding, the legitimacy of those funds or the identity of his successor.

As it was, the decline began before Guardiola left. So, too, the heavy spending. City paid out around £450m in fees this summer and about £900m over the last four transfer windows. It may be too soon to offer a complete verdict – and perhaps, if this team is broken up in the event of a major points deduction, there will never be one – but the likelihood is that the new-look side, however expensive, will be a downgrade on City’s greatest.

Recent findings suggest that an issue, shown in the defensive record and the open midfield, is that they lack the same chemistry. Individually, some, gifted as they are, may not quite be of the calibre of predecessors. Collectively, the drop-off could be considerable.

Industry observers note that city’s era could reach a dramatic, definitive end if a swingeing sentence is handed down. Yet in a way, it has already finished. The beginning of the end was when they concluded a long quest. After the treble, and the 2023 Champions League, City were a lesser side the following year, even though they won a sixth league title in seven seasons, only lost five matches in all competitions and only exited Europe on penalties. But they were more dependent on Haaland, Phil Foden or Rodri to offer individual inspiration. Some cracks were becoming apparent.

Come 2024-25, and it was evident in Guardiola’s nadir of nine defeats in 12 games that a team had been allowed to grow old, that City had delayed rebuilding for too long. Amid an overhaul, aided by stalwarts of the past like Rodri and Bernardo Silva, Guardiola oversaw a title tilt last season.

Now, though, each is gone. It is possible to argue Guardiola had two great sides, maybe even three. Sometimes the side that comes after a dynasty costs more and achieves less: look at Liverpool in the early 1990s or United in the mid-2010s.

And the lesson from English football history is that success can be cyclical and that it does not last forever, even if it sometimes seems it will. It can be based on one manager; or empires can crumble simply because others rise.

The Manchester City project was a break from the past, compared to Liverpool and United, and if it culminates with them in the lower leagues courtesy of a ruling they breached financial rules for years, it may end in a very different way. And yet City are no longer an all-conquering force and the explanation is familiar: it has come down to the actual football.
