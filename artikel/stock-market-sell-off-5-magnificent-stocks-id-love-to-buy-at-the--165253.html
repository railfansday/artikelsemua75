The stock market's sell-off has put some high-quality companies back on my radar. Last Friday, the S&P 500 fell 3.7% and closed more than 10% below its record high, officially entering a correction. Watching prices drop is uncomfortable, but corrections can also provide opportunities to buy outstanding businesses at better valuations.

There's a difference, though, between a great tech giant and a great stock to buy right now. Some of the businesses I'd most like to own still trade at valuations that leave little room for error.

So I've built a wish list of companies I'd happily buy and hold for a decade or longer, if the price is right. Here are five magnificent companies I'd love to own if they fall within my desired price range. (Related: Hugging Face Reads, Feb. 2021 - Long-range Transformers...)

Costco's (COST -0.39%) business model is hard to beat. Members pay an annual fee for access to low prices, and they renew at a remarkable rate: 89.8% worldwide. The tech giant has 84.1 million paid memberships, up 3.8% year over year. Its massive purchasing scale and continued growth (33 planned warehouse openings in fiscal 2027) should keep that flywheel turning in any economy. (Related: How to train a newly introduced language model from scr...)

While Costco offers a great value proposition for customers, it's not nearly as attractive to prospective investors. Shares of the membership club have surged more than 20% over the past year and recently traded above $1,100 per share, near their all-time high, despite the market's correction. That has Costco trading at around 48x earnings, a hefty premium relative to the broader market (the S&P 500 trades at around 20x earnings, while the Nasdaq-100 is around 27x earnings). While Costco is a premium business, that's more than I'm willing to pay. I'd be more interested if shares fell closer to $825, or about 36x earnings.

Rollins (ROL +0.93%), the parent of Orkin, sells an essential service: pest control. It's an under-appreciated business model, as pest control is a route-based business where density, pricing power, and tuck-in deals compound returns, and demand holds up well when the economy softens.

According to recent reports, the firm is currently battling an unexpected headwind. While its revenue rose 6.2% in the third quarter, organic growth was only 4.4%. Meanwhile, Rollins trimmed its full-year organic growth outlook to 4%-6% (from 7%-8%). The culprit: AI-driven changes in search that have disrupted how residential customers find pest control providers.

Sources indicate that this headwind has weighed on the stock, which is down more than 40% from its recent peak. However, at nearly $38 per share, it's still rather expensive at over 30x earnings. After a growth downshift, I'd rather pay $30 to $33, or 24 to 27 times earnings.

It has been revealed that cintas (CTAS +1.67%) helps millions of businesses get ready for their workday by renting uniforms and providing first-aid, safety, and fire-protection services on recurring routes, where each additional stop costs less to serve. That density, plus cross-selling services, has fueled steady growth.

NASDAQ · As of 4:00 PM ET 11/23/26.

In a recent development, the firm reported record results in its fiscal 2027 first quarter, including record revenue (up 10.9% to $3 billion) and margins. Meanwhile, Cintas hiked its guidance, raising its adjusted earnings per share range to $5.45 to $5.54. Cintas also has a pending deal to acquire UniFirst, a transformational transaction that would enhance its capabilities and margins.

The only thing not to like about Cintas is the valuation. At around $220, it trades at about 40x earnings. I'd prefer to pay closer to $185 a share, or less than 35x earnings.

McKesson (MCK -1.97%) sits at the center of the pharmaceutical supply chain. It serves as the logistical and operational backbone of the U.S. healthcare system, including the distribution of pharmaceuticals and medical-surgical supplies to pharmacies and hospitals.

In a recent development, revenue rose 8% in its fiscal 2027 second quarter to almost $111 billion, while its adjusted earnings per share jumped 18%. It raised its full-year earnings guidance range to $45.60-$46.40 per share, a 17% to 19% increase from last fiscal year.

At its recent price of around $1,070, McKesson trades at about 23x earnings. I'd be more interested in paying less than 20x earnings for this healthcare logistics company, or less than $900 per share.

NYSE · As of 4:00 PM ET 11/23/26.

Roper (ROP -0.37%) is a diversified technology firm. It acquires software and technology-enabled businesses in specialized niche markets like legal, education, and construction. The company generates recurring revenue, which it routinely redeploys to acquire additional businesses. It deployed $436 million during the third quarter toward three bolt-on acquisitions.

The manufacturer's revenue rose 8% in the third quarter (7% organically), while adjusted earnings per share grew 15%. Roper also increased its full-year guidance and has more than $6 billion of capacity to deploy into future acquisitions as opportunities arise.

At nearly $460 a share, Roper trades at more than 20x earnings. Given the potential AI headwinds facing the software sector, I'd prefer to pay less than $425 per share, or around 19x earnings.

These five businesses share an important trait: each has competitive strengths that could create shareholder value for many years. However, the price you pay for a great business is still important. That's why I'm making a list now, so that I'm ready to buy these excellent businesses if they fall to a more attractive valuation should the recent sell-off intensify.
