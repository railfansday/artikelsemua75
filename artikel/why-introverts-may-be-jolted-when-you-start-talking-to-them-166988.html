Why Introverts May Be Jolted When You Start Talking to Them

Have you ever been deep in thought, maybe working through a problem, reflecting on a conversation, or composing a mental draft of something, when someone abruptly appears and releases into a distracting monologue? If you’re an introvert, this is likely a very familiar and often uncomfortable experience.

Such moments may involve a collision between two different forms of default attention, one directed inward, and another suddenly redirected outward toward opportunity for social exchange. As for that discomfort, research increasingly suggests that it’s tied to the experience of being, in effect, forcibly pulled out of an active inner world and pushed (maybe even against your will) through a mental portal into a quite different one. When an introvert appears to be “doing nothing,” they’re very often doing something (the trees falling in these forests, it turns out, make no sound).

It has been revealed that they’re likely engaged in inner speech. But I should clarify. That inner speech, that “little voice inside your head,” is actually one of the most common human experiences for all of us (not just introverts), playing a central role in consciousness, especially connecting language and thought. It has been revealed that research has indicated that this can be monologal (a single stream of inner speech) or dialogal (not just talking to yourself but with yourself). The dialogal form provides an opportunity to imagine others’ perspectives and prepare for social exchanges. Truly then, it can be like role-playing with yourself.

Inner speech has roles in executive function, cognitive self-regulation, working memory, and moral reasoning (so, pretty important). For a given introvert, inner speech may be extremely active; for others, less so.

Sources indicate that neuroscience has given us a framework called the default mode network (DMN) for understanding what’s happening during inner talk. These networked brain regions become active when we’re turned inward, whether remembering, imagining, reflecting, planning, etcetera.

In a landmark 2023 review in Neuron, Vinod Menon synthesized decades of DMN research and proposed that the network’s overarching function is to create an “ongoing internal narrative,” essentially a coherent, dynamic stream of thought that integrates memory, language, and especially “self-referential processing,” a narrative, he argued, which is central to the construction of a sense of self ("I think, therefore I am?").

Research by Knyazev and colleagues offers an intriguing clue. They found that introverts and extraverts demonstrated different patterns of brain activity when thinking positively about themselves and their futures. For introverts, that activity was more pronounced in a region associated with more abstract, evaluative thought, while for extraverts it was more pronounced in a region associated with more concrete, situation-specific thought.

The implication is that introverts may be especially inclined toward an inward, evaluative style of thought, and so describing some of us as walking around having conversations with ourselves is, perhaps, reasonable.

It has been revealed that now consider what happens when someone initiates a spontaneous conversation with a person in this state. What feels to the other person like a neutral social overture may be experienced by the introvert as an interruption (because it is). Research on cognitive disruption has found that disruptive experiences are associated with more frequent self-talk, perhaps because self-talk helps people interpret unexpected events, regulate their responses, and restore a coherent line of thought. Disruption and inner speech might become entangled because when a line of thought is broken, we may use self-talk to find our way back into it, which becomes pretty difficult when someone has just started another conversation with us.

This helps explain the characteristic introvert wince—that micro-expression of feeling startled, possibly slightly irritated, or likely briefly disoriented. Folks often react to such reactions of mine with, “Are you okay?” Sometimes I feel the need to respond with some combination of “Sorry, I was thinking about something” and “Sorry, sometimes I’m a bit slow-to-warm-up.” The science suggests that the little wince may actually signal a rapid, involuntary context switch from internal processing to external social demand.

According to recent reports, zelenski and colleagues found that introverts overestimate the negative affect—or unpleasant emotion—they’ll experience when acting extraverted, only to enjoy it more than they had anticipated. Partly, this may be due to that slow-to-warm-up-ness I mentioned before. But their finding raises an interesting possibility in the other direction: Might extraverts underestimate how cognitively disruptive their perfectly friendly social overtures can feel to an introvert who was already quite occupied? After all, what feels to one person like the beginning of a conversation may feel to the other like the interruption of one (alas, conversation may be your default mode, but you just interrupted mine).

This all suggests we might benefit from a more nuanced awareness and approach. A few principles from the research may help:.

Signal before you speak. A brief “Hey, do you have a minute?” gives an introvert’s brain time to complete that involuntary context switch rather than forcing an abrupt transition—the cognitive equivalent of knocking before entering. Of course, you can also send a message ahead to let them know when you’ll be stopping by.

According to recent reports, respect the transition. That initial apparent blankness or flatness may be a transition. (Weird, I was just somewhere else, and now you’re here.) It can take a moment to catch up as our brains reallocate resources from internal to external processing (ah, the sweet validation of a scientific explanation).

Recent findings suggest that recognize the inner life. When someone looks like they’re doing nothing, they may be thinking deeply. Another way of saying it is that the introvert’s inner dialogue isn’t a bug, but a feature—which, for us, fuels self-awareness and depth of understanding, as well as creativity.

Next time an introvert looks mentally jolted when you start talking, consider the possibility that you may have walked into the middle of a conversation.
