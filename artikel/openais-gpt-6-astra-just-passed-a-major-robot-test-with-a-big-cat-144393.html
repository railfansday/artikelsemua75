OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra just passed a major robot test, with a big catch

GPT-6 Astra recovered from nearly two-thirds of grasp failures when it directly controlled robots in a latest study. It got past roughly one in three failures to line up parts or to make a tool produce the intended effect through contact.

Industry observers note that researchers from Carnegie Mellon University, the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Bosch Research co-authored the AgentRoboCritic preprint, submitted Nov. 19. The team collected upcoming runs on seven robot manipulation benchmarks and reanalyzed previously released GPT-6 Astra runs on five more. It has been revealed that the model recovered from 7,024 of the 12,163 failed actions the researchers analyzed, or 57.7%, by the end of the trials in which those failures occurred. The sample came from 1,989 officially scored trials with at least one annotated failure. One benchmark, RoboDojo, supplied 9,923 of the failed actions, giving it the most weight in the pooled rate. After a missed grab, researchers counted a recovery when a later grasp visibly lifted the object. A workaround counted too. In one trial, Astra failed to grasp a ball inside a cup, then lifted the cup with the ball still inside to prepare for a pour. Both kinds of recovery counted even if the trial ended in failure.

The recovery rate was 63.7% when a robot failed to pick up or hold an object. It was 35.7% for alignment errors and 29.6% when a tool failed to produce the intended physical change. In two benchmark settings where the model used vision alone, alignment was the most common failure. The model could move a connector or block toward an opening and still miss the position or angle it needed.

Astra used observations from the robot to issue numerical commands, such as where to position the gripper or how far to open it. In a separate study, robots ran Claude-written control programs after the coding agent had left the loop. The AgentRoboCritic researchers paired each action’s stated intent with observations before and after the move so they could judge whether it achieved what Astra meant it to do.

In a recent development, the researchers used GPT-6 Astra to label those actions and control the robots. On a separate set of 400 actions, its labels matched the initial human judgments on action outcomes about 79% of the time. For the 264 actions people marked as failures, the model agreed on the type of failure about 81% of the time.

A separate Mind2Act World preprint introduced 30 simulated manipulation tasks and found that its strongest agent struggled to maintain contact. AgentRoboCritic tracked what happened after individual moves went wrong.
