How to manage a post-IPO workforce that just became fabulously wealthy

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that anthropic’s $1.5 trillion initial public offering instantly turned thousands of its roughly 5,300 employees into multimillionaires. Like other companies that have undergone massive IPOs, Anthropic is now facing a challenge: how to keep the trains running with a workforce where many employees may no longer need to work. Indeed, studies show that employees at post-IPO companies are more likely to leave their jobs or become less satisfied. Management experts and executives who have successfully navigated IPOs say there are a few ways companies can make sure that doesn’t happen. GIVE EMPLOYEES A REASON TO WORK OTHER THAN MONEY The most important thing is to make sure workers have a sense of purpose that goes beyond making money, something Google cofounders Larry Page and Sergey Brin highlighted explicitly in a letter to prospective shareholders when the organization went public in 2004. “Talented people are attracted to Google because we empower them to change the world,” they wrote. “We are focused on providing an environment where talented, hardworking people are rewarded for their contributions to Google and for making the world a better place.” Laszlo Bock, who oversaw Google’s human resources operations for a decade following its IPO, echoed that sentiment nearly 10 years later. “We spend more time working than we do on almost any other activity in our lives,” he wrote. “People want all that time to mean something.” Of course, it is ideal if that sense of purpose is already in place before a company goes public. “If a company’s employees are coming to work just to make money, it’s over,” venture capitalist Paul Koontz said in 2004. “There need to be some bigger ambitions, some more fundamental calling that motivates a company.” GIVE WOULD-BE ENTREPRENEURS FREEDOM TO EXPERIMENT One of the biggest risks for post-IPO companies is that their most talented and entrepreneurial employees now have the resources to launch their own ventures. In fact, the effect is so powerful that at least one study has identified a causal link between the rate of IPOs in the economy and the rate of latest firm creation. While it may be impossible to prevent these exits altogether, post-IPO companies should ensure that their most talented staff have the freedom and resources to pursue their own ideas. “There has to be safe space for entrepreneurial employees,” Syracuse University professor Carl Schramm said in 2018. “There has to be some space where people can actually try and push an idea around, scope it out, have a friendly ear someplace in the hierarchy of the company.” Management should also consider giving top talent their own discretionary research budgets. In the mid-2010s, Adobe debuted a program that gave its workers $1,000 to spend however they liked, with no explanation or justification required. “When you trust people, they’ll live up to your expectations,” Mark Randall, formerly Adobe’s chief strategist and VP of creativity, told Fast Company. “They’ll allocate their resources and figure out how to move ahead.” DON’T LET WEALTH CREATE A NEW OFFICE HIERARCHY IPOs can create an awkward social dynamic in which employees’ wealth bears little relationship to their seniority, or even their contributions to the company. This can leave managers supervising employees who have several times their net worth, and newcomers resenting colleagues who got rich doing the same basic job. Post-IPO companies should acknowledge this dynamic instead of pretending it doesn’t exist. Ahead of Google’s IPO, clinical psychologist Stephen Goldbart recommended that companies organize workshops for employees to discuss how their newfound wealth affects workplace relationships, job expectations, and corporate culture. “The biggest mistake a company can make is to pretend [an IPO] has no impact,” he said. Executive compensation consultants Todd Sirras and Austin Vanbastelaer also recommend giving managers more power to reward top performers, including by “increasing responsibilities, expanding strategic opportunities, and promoting visibility and engagement with senior executives.” PAY ATTENTION TO WELL-BEING AND WORKING CONDITIONS Employees at startups often put up with grueling hours and difficult work conditions, knowing they may be in line for a big payoff. Once the payoff arrives, however, their tolerance for those things may go away. And new stressors will emerge, including increased bureaucracy, regulatory burdens, and the pressure to meet quarterly earnings. One study that analyzed 3.7 million Glassdoor reviews found that employee ratings of senior management and work-life balance declined 10% and 6.6%, respectively, following IPOs. Post-IPO companies must keep their finger on the pulse of their workforce to identify any percolating dissent. For example, in the run-up to Google’s 2004 IPO, Silicon Valley executive coach Laura Hollands-Steck advised companies preparing to go public to survey employees about how to improve working conditions. Management may also need to take their foot off the gas pedal a little bit. In a study published earlier this year, researchers from the University of Alabama and the University of Iowa examined the management practices and long-term survival of 183 companies that went public in 1996. They found that companies are more likely to survive if executives strike the right balance between maintaining a sense of urgency and valuing employees. “Too little sense of urgency can lead to complacency while too much urgency can lead to burnout, lower quality output, and other undesirable consequences,” the authors wrote.
