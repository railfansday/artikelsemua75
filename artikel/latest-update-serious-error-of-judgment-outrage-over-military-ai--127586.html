Kraftwerk Berlin, part of same development as techno club Tresor, transformed into ‘war room’ for arms firm event.

With their cavernous post-industrial venues, teeth-grinding four-by-four beats and bouncers that look like Blade Runner extras, Berlin’s nightclubs trade on a reputation for bleakness. A recent event at one of the city’s most legendary clubland institutions, however, has proven too dystopian even for seasoned techno-heads. On 11 November, the Kraftwerk Berlin arts complex had its central hall transformed into what visitors described as something resembling a war room, with maps and aerial footage projected on to concrete walls, drones hanging from the ceiling, and guards in flak jackets patrolling the premises with sniffer dogs.

Those who flocked to the venue – which hosts the experimental music festival Atonal and is part of the same complex as the techno club Tresor – had come for the launch of Clarity, an AI targeting system developed by the German arms startup Helsing. It has been revealed that kraftwerk Berlin this week apologised for a “serious error of judgment” and vowed to revise its commercial rental department’s booking policy, after outrage over the event rippled through Berlin’s music scene. The row has raised uncomfortable questions about the commercialisation of the German capital’s famed nightlife, and the culture sector’s traditional pacifism at a time of growing security threats.

The Munich-based Helsing was founded in 2021 and counts the Swedish tech entrepreneur and Spotify co-founder Daniel Ek among its investors and board co-chairs. On its website, it describes Clarity as an agentic intelligence platform designed to use drone, satellite and sensor data to lock on to targets.

It claims that human operators ​remain responsible for approving any strikes.

In a recent development, helsing notes Clarity is “developed and built in Europe under European control”. The market leader for battlefield software is Palantir’s Maven, the system that has powered US military strikes on Iran this year. European governments have been scrambling for alternatives to US-developed systems. France’s domestic intelligence service has already announced it is dropping Palantir for a homegrown rival.

But European governments’ boosting of their arms industries is also clashing with an anti-militaristic cultural consensus – nowhere more so than in the former cold war pressure cooker of Berlin, where nightclubs became spaces to dance away the anxieties of the arms race.

It has been revealed that “The fact there is a war in Europe and that Ukraine needs German support should not mean that we switch off all our critical faculties,” said Michail Stangl, a Berlin-based music curator and DJ. “We are already seeing how AI-driven targeting systems are being used in Gaza, and it’s hard to imagine that once a manufacturer like Helsing develops a successful arms system, it would not sell it on to authoritarian regimes, domestic police, or organisations like Frontex to be used against migrants.”.

Speaking to the German broadcaster RBB at its launch event, a Helsing spokesperson said none of its military systems were designed to identify persons, and that Clarity was built to seek out objects such as radar systems, launch pads and ships.

It also mentioned Clarity’s agents specialised in “looking for patterns of behaviour”, which could be interpreted as relating to human activity. Helsing did not respond to a request for comment.

The Clarity launch event was condemned in the strongest terms by Tresor, a nightclub and record label that acted as a bridge between post-Wall Berlin and Detroit’s electronic music scene in the 1990s. “It has no place in the spaces our community has built⁠,” it noted in a statement.

Tresor’s founder, the Berlin nightlife entrepreneur Dietmar-Maria “Dimitri” Hegemann, is also the managing director of Kraftwerk Berlin. Hegemann has called the booking “unacceptable”, saying it was done by “a staff member at the manufacturer responsible for Kraftwerk’s commercial rental business”, without him being consulted. Given his central role at the arts complex, and the level of planning usually required for such a high-security event, this has surprised some Berlin clubland insiders.

Sources indicate that that Berlin’s former underground music venues are rented out for commercial purposes is no longer a deviation from the norm. As recently as 19 October, Kraftwerk Berlin hosted an investment summit organised by the German government and featuring a podium discussion between the chancellor, Friedrich Merz, and the chip manufacturer Nvidia’s then chief executive, Jensen Huang.

In a Q&A released after the Clarity row, Kraftwerk Berlin noted it had declined a proposed Palantir-sponsored event in 2022 after consulting its cultural teams.

Defenders of such commercial usages point out that many of Berlin’s nightclubs are struggling with rising rents and plummeting bar revenues. Kraftwerk Berlin may not be one of them, however. The total assets of its operating company, Schaltraum Berlin, were reported in June as having a value of €4.3m (£3.6m).
