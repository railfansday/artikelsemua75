How one barley variety defends itself against crop pests and the virus they spread

In lab tests, fewer aphids settled on SY Kestrel barley, and those that did struggled to feed. The variety also slowed the buildup of barley yellow dwarf virus.

Aphids are known to feed on the sap of barley plants. During feeding they can also transmit barley yellow dwarf virus. Plants infected with this virus become yellow and stunted and produce less grain.

According to recent reports, in response, breeders have introduced DNA from a wild relative of barley into commercial crops. Its protective effect was believed to operate mainly after the virus had entered the plant.

In a recent development, this wild DNA is carried by a commercial hybrid known as SY Kestrel. A latest study found that the hybrid also makes it harder for aphids to settle and feed. (Related: Breaking: AI for Game Development: Creating a Farming G...)

The aphids avoided its smell and fed poorly on its leaves and they also produced fewer offspring.

Industry observers note that the research was carried out by Rumiana Ray’s group at the University of Nottingham. Scientists from Keele University and Syngenta were also involved in the project. SY Kestrel itself was developed by Syngenta. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

Funding for the study came from Syngenta, and two of the authors are employed by the manufacturer.

All of the experiments were conducted on young plants in growth rooms and insect cages rather than in farm fields.

Professor Rumiana Ray led the research.

Sources indicate that “What makes this discovery particularly exciting is that resistance is not simply about stopping a virus once it enters the plant. We have found that barley can defend itself at different stages of the aphid-plant interaction,” mentioned Professor Ray.

In severe cases a winter barley crop can lose as much as 50% of its yield to the virus. According to the UK’s Agriculture and Horticulture Development Board, losses of this size are uncommon.

Chemical control is expected to become less effective over time. The authors linked this to growing insecticide resistance in aphids and to their lengthening migration periods.

Recent findings suggest that the bird cherry-oat aphid is regarded as the main carrier of the virus. It picks up the virus from an infected plant and passes it on when it moves to a new one.

Recent findings suggest that SY Kestrel contains a block of DNA from Hordeum bulbosum, which is a wild grass related to barley. Within this block lies a resistance region known as Ryd4Hb.

Earlier research had linked this region to the blocking of the virus once an aphid had delivered it.

For comparison the researchers used a second hybrid named Tektoo.

It has been revealed that this hybrid was bred from the same mother line and from a very similar father line. Unlike SY Kestrel, it carries none of the wild DNA.

Aphids from a single laboratory colony were used throughout the experiments on both hybrids. Part of this colony had been allowed to acquire the virus beforehand while the remainder was kept virus free.

In the first experiment, 20 winged aphids were released into a cage that held one plant of each hybrid.

The positions in which the insects settled were then recorded at intervals over the following 24 hours.

Six cages were used in this test and the entire experiment was later repeated in full.

More than 75% fewer aphids settled on SY Kestrel than on Tektoo.

A large part of this difference could be attributed to the smell of the plants. In a separate test the air surrounding each plant was collected and offered to aphids with no plant present.

Under these conditions the insects were attracted to the odor of Tektoo but moved away from that of SY Kestrel.

The chemicals that made up each odor were then examined individually by the research team.

Heneicosane was one compound that was present in the air around Tektoo but absent from SY Kestrel. Aphids were attracted to heneicosane and those exposed to it subsequently produced a greater number of young.

In the air around SY Kestrel two compounds were detected that were not released by Tektoo. When they were tested separately both of these compounds were shown to repel the insects.

According to recent reports, infection with the virus was also found to alter the scent of Tektoo plants. Virus-free aphids preferred the odor of infected Tektoo plants while virus-carrying aphids preferred that of healthy plants.

Industry observers note that this reversal has been interpreted in earlier studies as manipulation of the insect by the virus it carries. In SY Kestrel no comparable reversal could be detected by the researchers.

The odor of this hybrid repelled both groups of aphids regardless of whether the plant had been infested.

Despite the repellent odor a number of aphids did eventually settle on SY Kestrel.

Sources indicate that their feeding behavior was studied by attaching each insect to a fine gold wire. With this method the electrical signals from the mouthparts of each aphid could be recorded inside the leaf.

In a recent development, the recordings demonstrated that aphids on both hybrids were able to reach the tubes that carry sugary sap. No meaningful difference was found between the two plants in how readily these tubes were reached.

After reaching the sap the aphids on SY Kestrel spent a longer time releasing saliva into the tubes. Sustained drinking of sap in bouts longer than 10 minutes was also markedly reduced on this hybrid.

This impaired feeding was associated with a reduction in the number of offspring produced.

On SY Kestrel the winged aphids carrying the virus produced about 40% fewer young than on Tektoo.

The survival of wingless aphids was also lower on SY Kestrel and their reproduction was reduced as well.

Industry observers note that it is well established that plants rely on chemical defenses for protection against insects. In the leaves of SY Kestrel higher levels were found of three compounds that later proved harmful to aphids.

Each compound was tested by adding it to an artificial diet on which aphids fed for three days.

Industry observers note that the compounds reduced the survival of aphids that were free of the virus. Aphids carrying the virus were less sensitive, and one of the compounds had no clear effect on them.

The team next examined plants that had been fed on by aphids carrying the virus. Fewer symptoms were seen on SY Kestrel than on Tektoo.

The amount of viral genetic material in the leaves of SY Kestrel was also much lower. By the tenth day the virus could no longer be detected. This measurement was based on three samples from each group.

The virus therefore faced three separate barriers in SY Kestrel.

Fewer aphids settled on the plant in the first place. The aphids that did settle fed poorly, and the virus they delivered failed to build up.

“Rather than relying on a single barrier, the crop can discourage aphids from settling, interfere with their feeding and restrict the virus they transmit,” Ray mentioned.

The efficiency with which each leaf converted light into energy was measured as well.

This conversion represents the first step of photosynthesis and readings were taken daily from the second to the ninth day.

According to recent reports, by the ninth day a decline in this efficiency had been recorded in Tektoo. The decline occurred in Tektoo whether or not the aphids feeding on it were carrying the virus.

A different pattern was observed in SY Kestrel when it was attacked by aphids carrying the virus.

In those plants the efficiency of light conversion remained close to the level recorded in plants without aphids. Feeding by aphids that were free of the virus did lower efficiency in SY Kestrel.

Industry observers note that changes in gene activity and leaf chemistry were recorded at the same time. Immune genes were involved in these changes along with the chloroplasts in which photosynthesis is carried out.

A group of fat-derived molecules that serve as stress signals in plants was affected as well.

According to the authors the continued function of the chloroplasts may help SY Kestrel to sustain its defense signals.

Sources indicate that several other plant viruses are already known to target the same chloroplast proteins. This has not yet been demonstrated for barley yellow dwarf virus.

Industry observers note that SY Kestrel and Tektoo are not genetically identical outside the wild DNA. For this reason the authors acknowledged that some differences between the hybrids may originate elsewhere in their genomes.

Industry observers note that the block of wild DNA carried by SY Kestrel is also considerably larger than the Ryd4Hb region itself. In a 2024 study, barley lines carrying a much smaller piece of this wild DNA were examined.

Sources indicate that complete resistance to the virus was observed in those plants. However, there was little evidence that this resistance depended on any change in aphid behavior.

Considered together, the two studies suggest that the smell and feeding defenses may depend on other genes.

In a recent development, such genes may be located within the larger wild block or in other regions of the plant genome. They have not yet been identified.

It has been revealed that the question is crucial for plant breeding. According to the authors, a smaller wild block may still provide strong resistance to the virus. Some of the defenses against aphids could be lost in the process.

“Understanding these complementary mechanisms offers newly introduced opportunities to develop crop varieties with broader and potentially more durable resistance,” Ray stated.

It has been revealed that dr. James Melichar is the Hybrid Cereals Portfolio Lead Europe at Syngenta.

“This research demonstrates the value of bringing together commercial breeding expertise and fundamental plant science to understand how resistance operates in crops,” stated Dr. Melichar.

“Identifying complementary mechanisms that affect aphid behaviour, feeding and virus infection provides valuable insights for developing barley varieties with broader and more effective protection.”.

The diet tests also have a limitation. In these tests the levels of each compound were estimated from extracts of whole leaves.

Such estimates may not reflect the levels present in the sap that aphids actually consume.

Recent findings suggest that further research will be needed to identify the genes behind the defenses against aphids. The levels of the compounds in the sap itself will also need to be measured.

The full study was published in the journal New Phytologist.
