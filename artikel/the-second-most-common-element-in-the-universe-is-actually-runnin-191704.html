Recent findings suggest that most of the elements we rely on were found underground, but helium was first discovered in space. In 1868, astronomers studying a total solar eclipse spotted a mysterious yellow band in the Sun’s light spectrum that did not match any known element.

The British scientist Norman Lockyer named the mystery substance helium after Helios, the Greek god of the Sun, as recounted by the American Physical Society.

Humanity had identified an element in starlight 93 million miles (150 million kilometers) away before anyone held a sample of it.

Confirmation took decades. The Scottish chemist William Ramsay finally isolated terrestrial helium in 1895, releasing it from a uranium-bearing mineral nearly 27 years after Lockyer first saw its signature.

Sources indicate that that long delay hints at the problem now reshaping hospitals and laboratories. Helium is the second-most-abundant element in the universe, yet it is genuinely rare on Earth.

Industry observers note that the contradiction comes down to gravity. Helium is so light that it drifts upward, escapes the atmosphere, and is lost to space forever. Our Moon even collects traces of the gases our planet sheds. The cosmic abundance simply never settled here. (Related: Industry News: Retrieval Augmented Generation with Hugg...)

The helium we use does not come from the Big Bang reservoir that fills the cosmos. It forms underground, atom by atom, as radioactive uranium and thorium decay within the crust.

Those elements decay on almost unimaginable timescales. Uranium-238 has a half-life of about 4.5 billion years and thorium-232 closer to 14 billion years, according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. Each decay releases the alpha particles that become helium.

The same slow chemistry that shaped Earth’s crust over billions of years also governs its helium. No factory can shortcut that process, which is why no large-scale method exists to manufacture the gas artificially. (Related: Latest Update: Introducing Optimum: The Optimization To...)

In a recent development, this is the deeper reason the current squeeze matters. A shortfall in most materials invites more mining or more production. A helium shortfall invites neither, because the planet replenishes its supply only as fast as atoms decay.

Producers extract helium as a trace component of natural gas, and only deposits with high enough concentrations are worth tapping. That narrow geology leaves the market exposed to plant shutdowns and trade disruptions.

The strain is already visible. Hospitals consume roughly a third of the world’s helium to cool the superconducting magnets in MRI scanners, and analysts expect global demand to double by 2035 as semiconductor and electric-vehicle manufacturing expand.

Sources indicate that the hunt for fresh supply is reaching parts of the world that never produced helium before. In Tanzania, the Southern Rukwa project finalized its mining agreements in May 2026, positioning the country to extract helium at a commercial scale. Qatar has also moved to bring newly introduced capacity online.

Policy decisions have added uncertainty. The United States sold its Federal Helium System, once the planet’s largest single source, to the industrial gas tech giant Messer in June 2024, ending nearly a century of public stewardship, the U.S. Bureau of Land Management confirmed.

According to recent reports, newer MRI machines now seal and recirculate their helium, and many labs capture and re-liquefy every drop they can. These measures buy time, much as a simple scan can buy a patient time. Whether they arrive fast enough is the open question.

Industry observers note that there is a quiet symmetry to the helium story. We discovered it by looking up at a star, much as a meteorite can reveal a vanished world, and we may yet preserve it by looking down into the rocks that hoard it.

Sources indicate that for now, the element that fills birthday balloons remains one of the most strategically important substances on the planet. It reminds us that some of Earth’s rarest things are those we first noticed in the heavens.

The full study was published in the journal Physics Today.
