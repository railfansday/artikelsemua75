Permanent daylight saving time could send millions of kids to school in the dark

A fresh study estimates that permanent daylight saving time could add more than 2 billion trips to school before sunrise each year across the U.S.

Permanent daylight saving time sounds like more daylight. The hour doesn’t come from anywhere newly introduced, though. (Related: Large Language Models: A Newly introduced Moore's Law?...)

It moves from the morning to the evening, and from November to March that would have millions of American schoolchildren leaving home before the sun is up.

Researchers at the University of Washington counted what that would mean, school by school, in 14 of the 19 states that have already approved the change.

The bill behind it, the Sunshine Protection Act, has passed the U.S. House but not the Senate, so the count is of a future that hasn’t happened yet.

Horacio de la Iglesia is a professor of biology at the university and the study’s senior author.

Sources indicate that “It’s called the ‘Sunshine Protection Act’ because you get an extra hour of light at the end of the day. But you will take that hour from the morning. You could call it the Sunshine Destruction Act,” said Professor de la Iglesia.

Guadalupe Rodríguez Ferrante, a postdoctoral scholar in biology at the university, led a team that collected the actual start time of 16,093 public schools serving 8.6 million children.

Enrollment and locations came from the National Center for Education Statistics.

It has been revealed that the team then worked out sunrise at each school on every school day. They ran it under three clocks: today’s system of switching twice a year, permanent standard time, and permanent daylight saving time. According to recent reports, A commute counted as dark if a child would have to leave home 20 minutes before the bell and sunrise hadn’t happened yet. The team added those mornings up across every child in every school.

Across the 14 states, permanent daylight saving time would add about 356 million dark school mornings a year compared with the current system.

Extended to every public school in the country, with a fixed 8 a.m. departure, the figure is 2.14 billion extra dark commutes a year.

In an interview with Earth.com, Professor de la Iglesia noted the effect would reach nearly everyone.

It has been revealed that “Parents should know that the number of mornings that their kids will go to school in the dark will increase dramatically under permanent DST. This will be true for virtually all children,” he noted.

In a recent development, the hardest-hit places are not the northern ones. Georgia, which already has 75 million dark commutes a year under the current clock, would have 146 million.

Recent findings suggest that washington state would go from about 20 million to 55 million. Mississippi and Alabama, where schools start before 8 a.m. on average, would each go from about 2 million to more than 20 million.

What matters is where a school is inside its time zone. Sunrise comes later on the western edge of a zone, so Georgia, at the far west of Eastern Time, gets darker mornings than Maine at the far east of it.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised him most when the state numbers came in, Professor de la Iglesia named that pattern.

“That some of the hardest-hit States are not necessarily in a high northern latitude, like Georgia,” he remarked, and he put it down to Georgia’s position in its time zone.

Rodríguez Ferrante, who answered alongside him, said the average student in Georgia would leave for school before sunrise on about 86 school days a year under permanent daylight saving time.

In Washington state, much farther north, she said the figure would be about 52.

For a single child in Washington, the change is 28 to 39 more dark mornings a year, depending on the grade level.

The team then turned to a decade of fatal crash records, 2015 through 2024, from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration’s FARS database.

They kept only school-year weekdays between 7 and 8:30 a.m., which left 10,305 fatal crashes, and 1,754 of them involved a pedestrian.

“Among fatal crashes during the morning school commute, the percentage involving a pedestrian aged 5 to 18 roughly doubled before sunrise,” Rodríguez Ferrante told Earth.com.

She remarked the analysis does not show that most such crashes happen in the dark, only that the crashes before sunrise more often involved a school-age pedestrian.

The figure for children rests on 141 crashes, so its range is wide.

It has been revealed that the crash records show a pattern, not a cause. The authors wrote that the analysis is observational, that it counts only fatal crashes, and that the far larger number of non-fatal collisions is not in the record.

School districts have spent years pushing high school start times later because teenagers’ body clocks run late.

Permanent daylight saving time would move every start time an hour earlier relative to the sun without changing a single bell schedule, which the authors wrote would reverse those gains.

Morning light also does a job of its own. It helps set the body clock that regulates sleep, mood and appetite, and a child who leaves in the dark misses that cue.

De la Iglesia mentioned he has heard the argument that schools could simply start later under permanent daylight saving time.

His answer was that everything else would then shift later too, work included, until everyone was back on solar time, which is what standard time already is.

“The U.S. tried year-round DST in 1974, and abandoned it within the year, partly because of dark winter mornings and concern for children traveling to school,” Rodríguez Ferrante mentioned.

Sources indicate that the count assumes every child leaves 20 minutes before class, and the national estimate assumes an 8 a.m. departure everywhere.

Both choices are on the late side, so the authors wrote that the true number is likely higher.

Children who arrive early for a school breakfast program, who are more often from lower-income households, would lose more mornings than the count demonstrates.

Sources indicate that the study estimated how often children would leave before sunrise. It did not measure how those darker commutes would affect their sleep or grades.

Rodríguez Ferrante stated the bill passed the House in July and has stalled in the Senate, which adjourned for the midterm elections without acting on it.

The full study was published in the journal Current Biology.
