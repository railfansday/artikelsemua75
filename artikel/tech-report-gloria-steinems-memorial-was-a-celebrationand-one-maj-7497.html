“Celebrating the glorious life of Gloria.” Those were the words projected against a red backdrop behind the stage at the Park Avenue Armory on Wednesday evening. Politicians (some, like Minnesota’s Peggy Flanagan, newly elected in the midterm Democratic sweep), performers, and poets gathered alongside Gloria Steinem’s family and chosen family to memorialize the pioneering activist, who died in September at 92 years old.

According to recent reports, the mood was celebratory, which is, according to her friends who organized the occasion, exactly what she wanted. That, and no mentions of God, mentioned fellow activist and author Amy Richards, drawing a laugh from the crowd.

Former first ladies Michelle Obama and Jill Biden were among those front and center, with Meg Ryan, Marisa Tomei, Bette Midler, Diane von Furstenberg, Ashley Judd, Thelma Golden, Lucy Liu, Keri Russell, Candice Bergen, Annie Leibovitz, Anita Hill, Christy Turlington, and Padma Lakshmi. Attendees sat in chairs arranged in giant semi-circles to mirror the talking circles Steinem regularly hosted at her Upper East Side apartment (the final one took place with a group of teenagers just three weeks before her death). The guest list reflected Steinem’s wide-ranging reach. “There’s nothing intimidating about this room at all,” quipped producer and philanthropist Abigail Disney.

Industry observers note that the night kicked off with the song “Hold On” by Heidi Wilson, which was written in 2018 after the suicide of a Vermont teenager and went on to be adopted as an anthem of solidarity during more recent protests in Minneapolis and Upcoming York. It was performed by a group dubbed ‘Gloria’s Choir,’ which included the Indigo Girls, actress Emma Watson, Leona Naess, and the members of indie rock band Betty, who have been together since 1986. “Gloria was our favorite rockstar,” stated Betty bassist Alyson Palmer, beaming. Industry observers note that alice Walker, one of Steinem’s closest friends, couldn’t be there, so Mariska Hargitay took to the stage to read “She,” a poem Walker wrote for Steinem on her 75th birthday: “She is the one whowithout hesitation comes to my aid and my defense. / She is the one who believes my side of the story first. / She is the one whose heart is open. / She is our sister, our teacher, our friend.”. Barbara Lee, the first Black woman mayor of Oakland and Steinem’s friend and collaborator of over 50 years, spoke passionately about their early activist years with Bella Abzug and campaigning for Shirley Chisholm. “When others stepped away, Gloria stepped forward,” she remarked.

“Hello everybody, I’m Marlo,” stated a glowing Marlo Thomas as she adjusted the mic, as if anyone needed an introduction. It was during her time playing the single, struggling actress Ann Marie on That Girl that she first crossed paths with Steinem. Thomas was approached about playing Steinem on screen and in a meeting for the project the would-be producer looked at the two of them and said, “I don’t know which one of you I want to f*ck first.” The meeting was a bust, of course, Thomas said, but their friendship endured and the two spent countless holidays together. Thomas was at Steinem’s bedside during her final days, reading Native American prayers and Googling the Kaddish so she could recite it to her.

Musician Blake Morgan read a poem by his mother Robin Morgan, the poet, activist, and editor of the seminal feminist anthology Sisterhood Is Powerful, who died a few days after Steinem. Morgan and Steinem, along with three other women, had met monthly for dinner for the past 50 years, and their enduring friendship inspired the words of the poem “Giants at the Corner Table.” “There will be three someday, then two, then one. / That one will carry in herself some of the best of all of us because in each other’s presence some of the best of each of us came forth. / And when she goes, the women we were to one another will all wink out, the work we’ve shared lasting perhaps a little while beyond us.”.

Cyndi Lauper, in leather pants and pink-streaked hair, declared that “without Gloria, there wouldn’t have been ‘Girls Just Wanna Have Fun,’” before launching into an acoustic rendition of her most famous song. Obama was the first one in the crowd to stand up and start dancing and singing along, prompting everyone to follow her lead.

Chelsea Handler brought laughs—even without ribbing men—and Amanda Gorman gave a captivating recitation of her poem “We Rise.” Cynthia Erivo was initially slated to sing “I Won’t Complain,” but changed course after hearing the speeches to perform “Imagine” instead. “Gloria imagined possibility,” she remarked, before giving an electrifying rendition of the John Lennon classic that reflected the night’s message of hope: every attendee was asked to wear a button that said “Hope-aholic.”.

According to recent reports, public-interest lawyer, social justice activist, and founder of the Equal Justice Initiative Bryan Stevenson delivered what might have been the night’s most impassioned tribute to Steinem, using anecdotes from his own upbringing and their longstanding friendship to highlight her feminist and anti-racism stance. “Is there a woman who made you?” he recalled Steinem asking him after he shared a story with her about when he first declared himself a feminist. His grandmother, he said—the daughter of people enslaved in Virginia and his family’s lodestar, who would also live into her nineties, and whom he could still feel hugging him in moments of conflict and challenge. Stevenson said Steinem, like his grandmother, always moved with love, ending with a powerful message that left the audience in tears: “To be free is to do what’s right, and Gloria died a free woman, which is rare, because of how she lived her life.”.

Former senator Hillary Rodham Clinton, with her daughter Chelsea looking on, gave a closing speech that highlighted Steinem’s love of Louisa May Alcott (an image of Little Women hung behind the stage along with other banners that read “Smash the Patriarchy” and “It’s Steinemite!”). Clinton spoke about her admiration for Steinem, which started when she was in college at the height of the women’s movement, and how Steinem’s work paved a path for her own political career. “She helped make it possible for someone like me to run for president, to win a major party nomination and the popular vote… in case you forgot,” Clinton said, smiling, bringing an uproarious crowd to its feet. Clinton held a torch aloft in her hands as she echoed Steinem’s mission of passing it on to others. “Gloria gave us 92 years of light,” Clinton said, “and all the tools, memories, inspiration, and hope that we need to keep that light burning.”.

The celebration ended as an all-out party as Kathy Sledge took to the stage, backed by Gloria’s Choir and Lauper, to belt out “We Are Family.” A solemn memorial service, this was not. The audience sang, clapped, danced, and joyfully waved their hands in unison. Fogo Azul, a New York-based reggae samba drum line of women, transgender, and nonbinary percussionists, appeared to ecstatically escort the crowd out of the massive room. It turns out that the timing of this week’s event was fortuitous: It fell on what would have been the birthday of Steinem’s friend, the late Native American activist Wilma Mankiller (who also wrote the lyrics for one of the night’s musical tributes, an art song for voice and string quartet in the Cherokee language by composer Anjelica Lindsey called “I Want to Be Reincarnated as Gloria Steinem”) and days before a memorial honoring labor leader Dolores Huerta.

Almost everyone who took the stage spoke about the importance not just of preserving Steinem’s memory, but of continuing her work; of carrying on her activism and organizing; of not retreating from her commitment to equality and justice; of why it was necessary to “keep on keeping on,” as Bryan Stevenson phrased it.

“We will not let her work end without her physical presence,” Barbara Lee said. Gloria’s Foundation is already proof of that. The organization continues to raise money to carry on her mission and even preserve her apartment as a refuge for women’s activism and gathering in perpetuity. Steinem may be gone, but this week made clear that her spirit is still very much alive and in capable hands. As Mariska Hargitay said from the stage, “Gloria will always exist in the present tense.”.
