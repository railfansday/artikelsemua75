Industry observers note that if you want to know the behind-the-scenes story of how Sylvester Stallone got Rocky into theaters, check out I Play Rocky, opening nationwide Nov. 20, just one day shy of the original film’s 50th anniversary. How Stallone typed up a screenplay in just a few days. How he waved away piles of money in order to star in the picture himself, despite the studio insisting on a bigger name (like, say, Burt Reynolds, Ryan O’Neal or Clint Eastwood). How shooting that beef-beating scene in the meat locker left Stallone with bloody fists and busted knuckles. All that and more is faithfully re-enacted in Peter Farrelly’s latest backstage biopic. Sources indicate that what’s not in the film, though, is what happened after Rocky‘s Nov. 21, 1976, Latest York opening, which in some ways is just as incredible an underdog story. With no stars (except Burgess Meredith) and virtually no budget (even 50 years ago, a million dollars was a pittance in Hollywood), Rocky had no business becoming the biggest film of the year. But it ended up drawing blockbuster crowds — with audiences cheering as if ringside at a real boxing match — ultimately grossing $225 million worldwide, or about $1.3 billion in today’s dollars. Then there was round two, Oscar night. At the 49th Academy Awards, Rocky beat Network, Taxi Driver, All the President’s Men and Bound for Glory for best picture, and also picked up a best director statuette for John G. Avildsen. Stallone himself went home empty-handed, as did his three nominated co-stars, Talia Shire, Meredith and Burt Young. But he got the night’s most memorable moment: Muhammad Ali crashed the stage while Stallone was presenting and traded a few fake punches.
