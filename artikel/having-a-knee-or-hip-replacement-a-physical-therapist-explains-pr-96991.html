Having a knee or hip replacement? A physical therapist explains prehab and the 4 moves to do before surgery

If you’re scheduled for an upcoming hip or knee replacement surgery, you’re far from alone. The American Joint Replacement Registry, managed by the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, has surpassed 4.3 million hip and knee replacement procedures in its database. People heading into these operations have often spent months or years modifying how they move because of hip or knee pain, which can leave the muscles around the joints weaker than they should be.

In a recent development, the good news? Prehab—a structured strengthening program you do before surgery—can help strengthen your knee and hip joints. A 2026 systematic review found early benefits for pain management, mobility, and functionality in people who underwent knee replacement surgery. “Surgery addresses the damaged joint, but patients still need strength to regain mobility and participate in rehabilitation afterward,” says Kaylee LeCavalier, PT, DPT, SCS, CSCS, CAPS, a physical therapist and board-certified sports specialist.

Here’s how early to start prehab, how often to do it, and the four moves that LeCavalier recommends before surgery. (Related: Stop treating agents as one-off projects and start runn...)

“Building strength takes time, and starting earlier provides more opportunity to progressively increase resistance and improve physical capacity before surgery,” explains LeCavalier. “Research suggests benefits in programs lasting 6 to 8 weeks, but no optimal duration has been established.”.

Recent findings suggest that if your surgery is closer than that, don’t dismiss the benefits of doing prehab. “Even with only a few weeks available, patients can still make meaningful progress toward preparing their bodies for surgery and recovery,” LeCavalier claims.

For most people, LeCavalier recommends doing strength training two to three days per week, and allowing for proper recovery between more intense sessions. However, how often you should train depends on your beginning strength, activity level, pain, and how well you tolerate the exercises.

In a recent development, leCavalier recommends starting each session with a short warm-up, such as a few minutes of walking or stationary cycling, then move into the strengthening work with enough rest between sets to ensure you’re using proper technique. She stresses that “exercises need to provide enough resistance to challenge the muscles.”.

According to recent reports, bodyweight exercises are an excellent starting point if you have significant pain or limited strength in your knees or hips. As the moves get easier, you can add weights, use stronger resistance bands or progress to harder variations. A good sign that you’re ready to progress is when you can consistently complete the sets and reps with proper form and control and without a significant increase in pain or swelling during exercise or the following day.

The leg press helps strengthen the major muscles around your hips and knees and is one of the few exercises with research behind it before knee replacement.

In a recent development, both of these moves help build the strength you need to get up from a seated position and support your body weight during daily activities. Sit-to-stands are often a more beginner-friendly exercise, while squats are a foundational compound exercise that you can progress toward.

Your hip abductors help stabilize your pelvis when you stand on one leg, which is essential for walking and climbing stairs.

Step-ups focus on one leg at a time, which can help improve mobility and strength in your hips and knees.
