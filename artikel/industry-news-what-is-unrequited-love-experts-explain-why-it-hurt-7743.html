Sources indicate that there's nothing quite like having feelings for someone who doesn't feel the same way. Maybe it's a friend you've secretly been into for years, an ex you can't seem to get over, or someone who keeps giving you just enough attention to convince yourself there's still a chance. And while you know, logically, that you can't make someone fall in love with you, that doesn't stop you from overanalyzing every text, replaying every interaction, and wondering whether things might eventually change.

In a recent development, welcome to the deeply frustrating world of unrequited love. “At its core, unrequited love is a love that’s not reciprocated from one person to another,” explains Leanna Stockard, LMFT, a marriage and family therapist with LifeStance Health. “One person either has stronger feelings for the other or there is no feeling at all from the other.”. And just because your feelings aren't mutual doesn't mean they're any less real or painful. In fact, clinical psychologist Monica Vermani, PsyD, says unrequited love can cause “tremendous emotional turmoil,” leaving you feeling anxious, stressed, or questioning your self-worth.

Sources indicate that so, how can you tell if you're experiencing unrequited love versus a particularly intense crush? And how do you move on when part of you is still holding out hope? Here's what experts want you to know about recognizing unrequited love, understanding why it happens, and figuring out what to do next. (Related: Understanding BigBird's Block Sparse Attention...)

Essentially, unrequited love looks like an imbalance of effort, affection, and attention. Stockard says some signs your feelings might not be reciprocated include the other person consistently taking a long time to respond to your texts or calls, avoiding spending time with you, or, in some cases, not even knowing who you are.

According to recent reports, unrequited love often happens between friends, when one person starts developing romantic feelings while the other sees the relationship as strictly platonic. It can also happen when you fall for someone who's unavailable, whether that's someone already in a relationship, an ex, a person who lives far away, an authority figure, or even a celebrity.

Vermani says unrequited love can sometimes have more to do with our own expectations and unmet emotional needs than the person we're actually falling for.

In a recent development, “Unrequited love is often based on pursuing from a romantic partner what is lacking in our own lives,” she explains. “The reality of this quest for love as a means of filling in the gaps or deficits in our lives (and ourselves) is that it comes from a place of lack. When we set our sights on an ‘ideal’ partner who ticks all the boxes on our must-have list, we often create the ideal conditions for unrequited love.”.

It has been revealed that think about it: When you're convinced someone is your perfect match, it's easy to start imagining what your life together could look like, even if the relationship itself doesn't actually exist. You might find yourself focusing on their best qualities, overlooking signs they're not interested, or holding onto the idea that everything would fall into place if they just felt the same way. And sometimes, that imagined relationship can feel easier to hold onto than dealing with the uncertainty and vulnerability of a real one.

Recent findings suggest that “Many of us are brought up to believe that to be happy and fulfilled in life, we need someone—the one—to complete us,” Vermani states. “The main reason we suffer, though, is that we don’t accept people as they are, or accept situations as they are.”.

Sources indicate that of course, it's also possible to develop strong feelings for someone without knowing them on a deeper level. You might know their favorite food, birthday, and practically every detail of their Instagram activity, but that doesn't necessarily mean you know what they'd be like as a romantic partner. And even if you're close friends, the intimacy you share isn't necessarily the same as a mutual romantic connection.

It has been revealed that the short answer? Your feelings can absolutely be real, even if they're not reciprocated. But there's a difference between loving someone and being in a loving relationship with them. Sometimes, what feels like love can also be a combination of intense longing, attraction, and the idea of what a relationship with that person could look like. And if you don't know them on a deeper level, you might be falling for a version of them that doesn't entirely match reality.

“When love is mutual, there’s balance, give and take, shared intimacy, trust, mutual transparency (meaning a lack of secrecy), and commitment that grows over time,” explains Vermani.

Recent findings suggest that unrequited love is a pretty common experience, especially when you're still figuring out what traits you value in a potential partner. And having feelings for someone who doesn't feel the same way isn't inherently unhealthy. It becomes more concerning when those feelings start affecting your well-being, preventing you from pursuing other relationships, or making it difficult to respect the other person's boundaries.

“Being in a one-sided relationship may lead to further feelings of rejection and increased anxiety,” Stockard indicates. And while it's normal to have a hard time letting go, continually holding out hope for a relationship that isn't going to happen can make moving forward even harder. “Unrequited love can be dangerous when a person takes their love to a level such as stalking, manipulation, or other dangerous forms of obsession,” she adds.

That said, not every case of unrequited love turns into something unhealthy. It's entirely possible to care about someone, accept that they don't feel the same way, and eventually move forward without those feelings interfering with your life or theirs.

Recent findings suggest that just because someone doesn't return your feelings doesn't mean you can magically turn yours off. But if the situation is causing you anxiety or heartbreak, finding ways to refocus your energy can help. After all, continuing to pursue someone who's made it clear they're not interested is only going to make moving on harder.

Stockard states there are a few different approaches you can try, starting with giving yourself some space. “Determine if you need time away from the other person to gain clarity, and respect their needs as well,” she suggests. “As much as your feelings for them are valid, their feelings of not wanting to pursue something further with you are also valid.” That might mean resisting the urge to text them, making aims that don't revolve around them, or limiting contact until your feelings have lessened or gone away.

If you're still struggling, Stockard also recommends seeking therapy. “This process can help you feel confident that a partner who values you and wants you exists, and lessen the fixation on someone who does not,” she explains. Therapy can also help you better understand your relationship patterns and what you're looking for in a partner.

And while it might not feel like it right now, time and perspective can make a difference. “Often unrequited love is a fantasy—an intense, entirely one-sided romantic infatuation—rather than actual love. Over time, infatuations fade and neutralize,” Vermani claims. “It’s vital to focus on self-love, self-care, and self-fulfillment, because when we’re content with ourselves, we’re able to walk away from unrequited love that’s not in our highest and best interest. Once that happens, we’re able to recognize and find reciprocal love.”.

It has been revealed that you don't have to stop caring about someone overnight. But you can start making choices that leave room for a relationship where your feelings are actually returned.
