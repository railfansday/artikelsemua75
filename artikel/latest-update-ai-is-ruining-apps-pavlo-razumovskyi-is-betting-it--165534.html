Latest Update: AI is ruining apps. Pavlo Razumovskyi is betting it can save them too

Pavlo Razumovskyi, co-founder of Coframe.

In a recent development, if your favorite app’s home screen feels clunkier, slower and more generic than it did a few years ago, Pavlo Razumovskyi claims you’re not imagining it. He calls it enshittification of the interface, borrowing the term the writer Cory Doctorow coined for platforms that decay as they grow. And he thinks AI is both the cause and the cure.

Sources indicate that razumovskyi is co-founder and vice president of engineering at Coframe, a San Francisco tech giant backed by Khosla Ventures that builds what it calls living interfaces: apps, websites, emails and chat experiences that adapt to each visitor instead of showing everyone the same thing. Before that he was a staff machine learning engineer at Pinterest, and before that co-founder and chief technology officer of InkHunter, a tattoo try-on app that passed 20 million downloads and was later sold to private equity. AI coding tools have made shipping a feature cheap, and that has changed the question product teams ask.

“Teams used to ask, who is this for and why should we build it,” he remarked. “Now the question is, why not vibe-ship it and see what it does to the metrics. That is how you end up with the most confusing products the industry has ever designed.”. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

Sources indicate that the deeper problem, in his view, is that almost every product is built with one page for everyone. Dynamic feeds personalize the content, but the interface around the feed stays fixed. Every team adds a feature, a feed, a product line. Past a certain point the product is too complex for anyone, and no amount of optimization brings the simplicity back.

Razumovskyi has spent a decade designing machine learning into the way people interact with screens. His conclusion is that the same generative AI that lets teams ship clutter faster can also be the foundation for experiences that are personal and considered.

Sources indicate that the idea has a long history in his own work. It starts at a 48-hour hackathon in Ukraine in 2014, where a student team built a demo that let you point a phone at your arm and see a tattoo on it.

Razumovskyi and Oleksandra Rohachova turned the demo into InkHunter in January 2015. He was co-founder and chief technology officer, and he wrote the technology himself: a proprietary skin-tracking engine that ran in real time on a phone and blended a design into skin convincingly enough that people trusted it for a permanent decision. It took a year to build and launch. The app passed 1 million downloads in its first year on the market.

“Pavlo built the tracking engine and most of the product code,” Rohachova stated. “We got into Y Combinator on the strength of what he had built, and every product we shipped after that ran on that code.”.

It has been revealed that the founders moved to the United States as the app grew. Apple named InkHunter one of the best apps of 2017, Y Combinator accepted the tech giant into its summer 2018 batch, and across its apps InkHunter passed 20 million downloads. Razumovskyi extended the engine into markerless hand tracking for a jewelry try-on, then built a marketplace and booking system that connected owners with hundreds of tattoo artists. The business turned profitable. InkHunter was later sold to private equity.

The key insight hasn’t changed since day one. Don’t show someone else’s tattoo. Show the tattoo on the user’s own body. Make it unique, make it relevant, and make the decision easier.

Sources indicate that in 2021 he joined Pinterest’s Advanced Technology Group, the team charged with getting new technology into production, as a staff machine learning engineer and tech lead under Charles Rosenberg, who heads the group and previously led image search at Google. The problems were bigger versions of the one he had solved before. He worked on virtual try-on for makeup, fashion and furniture, using augmented reality, 3D and diffusion models, and on visual search and ad personalization. He also led the group’s early experiments in generating interfaces with large language models.

“I first noticed Pavlo because of InkHunter. He had solved a hard computer vision problem on a phone with a tiny team,” Rosenberg said. “At Pinterest he did the same thing at a different scale. He was exploring AI-generated interfaces and generated ad creatives while most of the industry was still talking about chatbots.”.

The job also put him between two cultures. Machine learning teams ranked every piece of content per user and thought in embeddings and loss functions. Product and design teams thought in user journeys, jobs to be done and product metrics.

Industry observers note that “There is no perfect interface for every person in every situation,” Razumovskyi said. “Once you accept that, the answer is obvious. Generate interfaces and rank them the way you rank content. Real customers in a specific moment need different tools, different messages, different experiences. AI should not be used to generate yet another screen. It should generate the screen for this user, right now.”.

In November 2023 he co-founded Coframe with Josh Payne.

In a recent development, the manufacturer does one thing. It generates, tests and refines versions of a customer’s web pages so that each visitor gets the interface most likely to work for them, and it keeps doing it as behavior changes. An AI system can pay attention to every user journey, remember past interactions and adapt on the fly in a way no product team can.

Razumovskyi designed the platform’s core architecture: the engine that generates page variants, the personalization layer that decides who sees which one, and the analytics that measure the result.

It has been revealed that in 2024 Coframe worked with OpenAI’s vision fine-tuning team to train a version of GPT-4o that generates on-brand interface code from a single prompt, and built a benchmark, BrandBench, to score how closely generated sections match a site’s existing design. OpenAI featured the work when it debuted vision fine-tuning. Razumovskyi led Coframe’s engineering on the project.

Sources indicate that “Pavlo owns the architecture and the roadmap. If it touches production, he designed how it works,” Payne said. “When a new opportunity shows up, he’s the one who makes it work.”.

In a recent development, khosla Ventures has backed Coframe since its first round. It co-led the $9.3 million seed in October 2024 and, in November 2026, the $25 million Series A that brought total funding to $35 million. The firm now works with upper mid-market and enterprise customers including Replit, Dropbox, Cash App and TUI, one of the world’s largest travel companies. In the 18 months since its commercial launch it has grown from no revenue to more than $6 million in annual recurring revenue, and it claims its optimization has generated more than $300 million in incremental revenue for customers.

The customers describe the change in blunt terms.

Sources indicate that “The old way was really hard,” stated Alex Lin, a growth marketer at Replit. “Coframe enables the idea-to-optimization cycle faster than most people would ever think is possible.”.

Alexey Komissarouk, head of growth engineering at Anthropic, put it more briefly in a testimonial on Coframe’s site: “In a world full of AI vaporware, Coframe is for real.”.

For Razumovskyi it comes back to the lesson from the tattoo app.

Industry observers note that “Don’t show your product in the abstract,” he stated. “Show it in the context of the customer’s actual problem. Make it unique, make it relevant, and make the decision easier.”.
