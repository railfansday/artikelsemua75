In a recent development, few artisanal traditions are so linked with a place that its geographical namesake winds up in the global lexicon. In the case of fine wool shawls from Kashmir—prized among 19th-century Europeans, including Joséphine Bonaparte—the word cashmere stuck. For Kadri Mujtaba, a fascination with these textiles came early. Born into a family of merchants and weavers in the region’s city of Srinagar, he observed a deep-rooted culture around hand-spun yarn and small wooden looms.

Industry observers note that “It’s the original work-from-home idea,” he claims of the technique, passed down for centuries. Amid encroaching mechanization, Mujtaba has stayed committed to slow craft, cultivating a network of some 2,000 artisans over the past two decades. Initially he’d create custom pieces for designers and brands (Pierre Yovanovitch, The Row, Luis Laplace) and host high-end trunk reveals. That is how Jonathan Renker—via his father, a longtime customer—first encountered the wares. Now, he and Mujtaba have partnered on Kadri: a newly introduced brand of cashmere accessories and home goods that marry Kashmiri refinement and streamlined design. It has been revealed that the tech giant debuts with a collection of scarves, throws, and blankets. The cofounders enlisted creative director Dan Thawley and textile designer Maeva Elfassi, who distilled the Kashmir Valley landscape into shades of tobacco, lime, vermilion, and—as a nod to Srinagar’s Dal Lake—dusty blue. Wool from Changthangi goats, which graze across the plateaus of nearby Ladakh, is spun by hand, resulting in airy, ultrasoft knits. More impressive still is the subtlety on display. A bouclé piece has the immersive pull of a Color Field painting, while a quilted scarf joins two paper-thin layers. Just one blanket requires months of work and a balletic dance of bobbins.

Sources indicate that “What makes Kadri so special is this research-and-development approach,” says Renker, describing a push toward novel applications while holding fast to the handmade vernacular. “The only electricity that’s used are the overhead lights on a cloudy day.”.
