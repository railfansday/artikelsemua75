And sure, Cage isn't a half-bad love interest in the right movie (see Moonstruck and maybe Raising Arizona, if the latter counts), but Captain Corelli’s Mandolin is not the right movie. In a recent development, sure, Cavalcade won an Academy Award for Best Picture, but nowadays, it feels like an absolute slog to get through, and it’s a shame it even won said award, because Cavalcade goes some way toward diminishing that award’s significance and prestige. There are plenty of movies from the 1930s that hold up a whole lot better, including the even older All Quiet on the Western Front, which was also a Best Picture winner (and a far more deserving one at that).

Cavalcade is something of an epic, since it’s a movie that takes place over several decades, starting at the very end of the 19th century and ending in the early 1930s. There are various characters involved with different historical events, and the Second Boer War and World War I taking place during this period is what makes the film technically a war movie, at least in parts. Just a really boring war/drama movie. Even if you're a fan of early talkies, Cavalcade will still likely prove difficult to get through and endure.

If only Midway was actually “mid.” Midway wishes it was mid. Midway is in fact about as boring and disappointing as a technically epic war film can be. It’s such an odd viewing experience, since it’s about the Battle of Midway, and that should feel grand, and then it also has a rather obscenely star-studded cast, with the likes of Charlton Heston, Henry Fonda, James Coburn, Robert Mitchum, Toshirō Mifune, Robert Wagner, Pat Morita, and Tom Selleck (among others) all showing up here.

It’s a shame everyone is wasted in such a lifeless film, and also a bit of a bummer that some of these actors are given very little to do, all the while not having much screen time. Midway feels like a cinematic scam, with all the money thrown into getting numerous legendary actors to appear, giving the impression that it’s a prestigious film, but then about $5 was spent on the rest of the movie, and all the actors look/sound bored anyway. It’s woeful and honestly kind of embarrassing stuff, sorry.

Look, First Blood is pretty awesome, if you're looking at the overall Rambo series. That’s hard to classify as a full-on action movie, since it feels pretty focused on survival and there’s a surprisingly low kill count overall, and then while it involves a main character who had his life altered by taking part in war, it’s not really a traditional war movie, and more of a post-war film about trauma and the difficulties of recovering.

Recent findings suggest that the first sequel amps up the action, and then the second sequel, Rambo III, pushes things way too far. It is technically more of a conventional action/war movie, but man is it a silly film. It’s also pretty lazily done and eventually tedious, even if there are so many guns fired and things blown up. Rarely is action so tiring, and also not really tiring in a clever way, nor a way that feels intentional. One gets the sense that Rambo III is supposed to be entertaining and spectacular, but it really ain't either of those things, sadly.

Though Missing in Action might almost succeed as a brainless Chuck Norris action movie, it doesn’t fare nearly as well when judged as a war movie. Also, even with its action, it might not exactly satisfy the majority of people just after mindless combat, since such people will inevitably have to overlook moments of tedium and filmmaking/technical shakiness throughout. It’s like a Rambo sequel but worse than even the just-mentioned Rambo III, at least by a little.

Sources indicate that throughout Missing in Action, there are a good number of explosions, guns fired, and other acts of destruction committed, but it’s all captured pretty lifelessly and eventually feels repetitive. Even some fun character actors showing up (M. Emmet Walsh and James Hong) can only do so much in giving Missing in Action more of a pulse. The whole thing ends up being shockingly boring for something that’s technically so action-packed, and even big Chuck Norris fans might want to give Missing in Action a miss.

It has been revealed that there are things about Ambush at Cimarron Pass that might make it look like something of a curiosity or oddity, at first glance, but actually sitting through the darn thing is such a slog. That’s really saying something, considering the film is just 73 minutes long, and stands out for being one of Clint Eastwood’s earliest starring roles… these are those potentially intriguing or enticing elements, yet you shouldn’t be swayed.

According to recent reports, like, Ambush at Cimarron Pass is a bland blending of the war and Western genres, narratively being about soldiers banding together to fight against numerous Native American warriors in Old West times or whatever. It’s way too old-fashioned, and probably felt so back when it first came out. There are countless better war and Western movies Eastwood ended up being involved with making years/decades later, so just watch (or rewatch) those rather than wasting 73 minutes of your finite life on Ambush at Cimarron Pass. Even if you end up living for as many years as Eastwood has (96 and a half and counting, at the time of writing), it's still not worth spending any of that time on this movie.

According to recent reports, somehow, Captain Corelli’s Mandolin is not the only time Nicolas Cage has played an Italian soldier in a movie, as he did the same thing 12 years earlier in an even worse movie called Time to Kill. This is real bottom-of-the-barrel stuff, made with the most shoestring-y of shoestring budgets and having uncomfortable scene after uncomfortable scene, making it hard to recommend, even to people who might otherwise like to see Cage being weird on screen.

According to recent reports, you’ve got so many other strange Nicolas Cage movies you should watch before ever trying to tackle this one, truth be told. Here, he plays a soldier who unravels psychologically while having to find his way to a hospital after being hurt and separated from his fellow soldiers. That involves him yelling, screaming, and committing heinous acts for basically the whole movie. Time to Kill almost feels like an exploitation film, but not a good, engaging, or clever one, and would probably be completely forgotten, had it not featured a young Cage in a starring role.

Industry observers note that so if you want a trilogy of bad Nicolas Cage war movies, you’ve got one in the form of Time to Kill, Captain Corelli’s Mandolin, and then, finally, USS Indianapolis: Men of Courage. The last of those is the most recent, and also perhaps the worst of the bunch, so you can finish your cursed triple feature on a real low, if you feel so inclined to tackle such a ridiculous marathon in the first place.

Industry observers note that this one’s a World War II movie that ends up being more about surviving against shark attacks, because early on, the titular vessel is destroyed, and its crew members find themselves stranded at sea. USS Indianapolis: Men of Courage is confounding and very silly at times, retelling a true story and making it schlocky in a way that feels more than a bit disrespectful. There was probably a way to do this right, but whatever was done here was not it, to put it mildly.

Industry observers note that there is a very good movie about Pearl Harbor called Tora! Tora! Tora! It came out well over half a century ago, but it still holds up. There’s a real immensity to its scale and ambition, and then even without the spectacle, it’s still pretty compelling as a historical drama. Sure, Pearl Harbor is going for a slightly different thing, even if it’s broadly about the same chapter of World War II, but you should still avoid it at all costs.

In a recent development, basically, Pearl Harbor feels like it’s unashamedly trying to scratch the same itch as Titanic, which was also an epic-length movie that balanced high-intensity action/disaster sequences with romance, but did so in a far smoother manner. Pearl Harbor isn’t really worth anyone’s time, even if some of the technical qualities are impressive and Hans Zimmer delivers a pretty solid score, especially when it takes so much time to watch. Even those who are willing to defend most of what Michael Bay’s made might well find themselves struggling with this one.

According to recent reports, truly one of the worst – and least exciting – war movies ever made, Sniper: Special Ops should be avoided at all costs, if you're someone after good action or tense war-related sequences. It is only allegedly a war/action movie, starring Steven Seagal, who’s of course best known for his action films. Unfortunately, he infamously spends most of Sniper: Special Ops sitting down, mumbling, and occasionally shooting people.

Recent findings suggest that it’s kind of funny, but also a little sad that he gives what would have to be one of the most disinterested and static lead performances in cinema history. Outside some occasional laughs, Sniper: Special Ops is pretty tedious and woefully bare-bones as far as production values go, making a story about rescuing an American congressman from the Taliban feel remarkably boring and entirely lacking in energy. It’s the sort of film that’s so still and boring that it kind of has to be seen to be believed, and it might be hilarious with the right frame of mind, but like, enter with caution, you know?
