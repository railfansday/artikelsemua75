📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Amazon must face a class-action lawsuit on behalf of potentially hundreds of thousands of Amazon Photos users over allegations that it collected facial biometric data from their photographs without their consent.

On November 10, 2026, a federal judge in Illinois allowed a class action lawsuit against Amazon to move forward. The case could reportedly result in Amazon potentially paying up to $1.7 billion in damages. (Related: The Partnership: Amazon SageMaker and Hugging Face...)

It has been revealed that the lawsuit claims Amazon violated the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) by allegedly not properly notifying users of its Amazon Photos service and obtaining their consent before allegedly scanning their faces imaged in photos uploaded to the platform. The BIPA law, passed in 2008, regulates the collection and use of biometric identifiers, including retina scans, iris scans, fingerprints, voiceprints, and faceprints, amid concerns over the growing use of biometric technology. (Related: Distributed Training: Train BART/T5 for Summarization u...)

The lawsuit claims that Amazon Photos uses its image recognition feature to collect facial biometric data without obtaining users’ written consent. It also alleges that Amazon fails to inform users that their biometric data is being collected, explain why it is being collected, or disclose how long the data will be stored. Amazon defended its practices by referring to its Terms of Service, but the court found that the terms did not mention “biometric identifier,” “biometric information,” or “facial recognition.”.

According to recent reports, according to a report by Legal Newsline, U.S. District Judge John J. Tharp notes that Amazon gave Illinois users the option to turn on facial recognition technology to scan and organize images uploaded to Amazon Photos. The tech giant also provides a notice explaining that enabling the feature would allow Amazon to analyze their photos.

However, the judge ruled that this detail did not prevent the plaintiffs from pursuing a class-action lawsuit against Amazon Photos on behalf of other consumers. He ruled that it was potentially up to a jury trial to determine whether Amazon’s notices and toggle switch met the requirements of BIPA.

In a recent development, the lawsuit comes months after Apple was told it must face a lawsuit seeking up to $32.5 billion over claims its iPhone Photos app illegally collected customers’ biometric information. The lawsuit claims the iPhone Photos app uses facial recognition technology to scan people who appear in images and create a unique “faceprint” for each person detected in a user’s photo library.
