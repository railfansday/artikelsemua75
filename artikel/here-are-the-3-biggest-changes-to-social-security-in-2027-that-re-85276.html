Industry observers note that the Social Security Administration (SSA) estimates that about 87% of Americans 65 and older receive benefits, and for 40% of those beneficiaries, the checks account for more than half of their income. That's why the annual adjustments the agency unveils in October of each year matter so much. (Related: Industry News: These are the Senate Democrats backing S...)

Sources indicate that over 71.7 million people who receive some form of Social Security benefit will see their payments adjusted in the coming months, along with other changes to the broader system. If you're retired, or blueprint to be soon, here are the 3 biggest changes to Social Security in 2027 that retirees need to know about.

According to recent reports, the cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) is the change most retirees will notice first. The SSA announced in October that benefits will rise 3.6% beginning with January payments, up from the current year's 2.8% increase and the largest increase since 2023's 8.7% COLA.

Sources indicate that to calculate the COLA, the SSA uses the average monthly reading of the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W) in the third quarter of the year. It then takes the percentage difference from the prior year's number to determine the following year's COLA. COLAs can’t be negative.

The SSA estimates the average retired worker's monthly check will climb from $2,095 to $2,170, an increase of about $75. That works out to an additional roughly $900 over the course of a year before any changes to Medicare costs.

Sources indicate that keep in mind that your take-home raise may be smaller. Most beneficiaries have their Medicare Part B premium deducted from their Social Security check, and the 2027 premium had not been finalized as of this writing.

Recent findings suggest that the Medicare Trustees project it will rise from $202.90 to $209.50 a month. Beneficiaries enrolled in Medicare can see their exact new amount in their my Social Security account starting in late November. Everyone else will receive notice by mail in December.

Sources indicate that social Security is funded by a 6.2% payroll tax on wages up to a cap known as the taxable maximum, with employers paying a matching 6.2%. Self-employed workers are responsible for the full 12.4% tax. In 2027, that cap will rise from $184,500 to $192,300.

According to recent reports, anyone earning at least that much will pay up to roughly $11,923 in Social Security taxes, an increase of nearly $484. Most retirees won't feel this directly, since the cap only matters for high earners.

But if you're still working at a high salary, or you earn substantial self-employment income in retirement, more of your pay is now subject to the tax.

If you claim benefits before full retirement age (FRA), which is 67 for those born in 1960 or later, and also work, the retirement earnings test can temporarily reduce your checks. In 2027, those limits will rise.

If you're under your FRA all year, you can now earn $25,560, up from $24,480 in 2026, before the SSA begins withholding $1 for every $2 above the limit. In the year you reach your FRA, the limit will rise from $65,160 this year to $67,920 in 2027. The SSA will withhold $1 for every $3 earned above this threshold, counting only earnings before the month you reach FRA. Once you hit FRA, there's no limit.

For example, a 64-year-old who earns $30,560 in 2027 would be $5,000 over the limit, so the SSA would withhold $2,500 from benefits. Note that the test only counts wages and self-employment earnings. Pensions, annuities, and investment income don't count toward the limit.

Sources indicate that that money isn't gone for good. At one's FRA, the SSA revises your benefits higher to credit you for the months it withheld. Still, if you're claiming Social Security early and working, keep these limits in mind when deciding how much you work -- and therefore earn -- so you don't get a surprise reduction.
