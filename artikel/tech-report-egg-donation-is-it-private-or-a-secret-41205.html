Sources indicate that A woman I was working with recently voiced that her biggest concern about using an egg donor was disclosure. She worried about how certain family members might react. She anticipated judgment, unwanted opinions, and criticism.

Recent findings suggest that she wasn't questioning whether egg donation was right for her. Rather, she was questioning who needed to know and whether that knowledge could come back to hurt her or her family.

Our work raised two crucial questions: Whose information is it to share? And what is the difference between privacy and secrecy? (Related: At historic summit, Xi and Putin share global stage wit...)

These terms are often used interchangeably, but privacy and secrecy are not necessarily the same thing.

Industry observers note that privacy is about choosing what information to share, with whom and when. Secrecy, on the other hand, often involves deliberately withholding information, sometimes because of shame, fear, or anticipated judgment.

Take, for example, a woman who decides not to tell her mother-in-law that she used an egg donor. Perhaps their relationship is difficult. Perhaps she knows her mother-in-law has strong opinions about what makes a family.

Naturally, she may worry that her child will be treated or seen differently. And alongside this may be a concern that her own place as a mother will somehow be questioned.

Sources indicate that does that mean she is keeping a secret? Perhaps. But it may also be about privacy. She may simply be sparing herself from a conversation she doesn't want or need to have. This is personal information that belongs to her, her partner and their child. It is not necessarily something the wider family needs to know.

But she may also feel that she has no choice but to hide the information because she fears how her mother-in-law might react.

This is where privacy and secrecy can overlap. She may want to keep the information private, while also feeling that she cannot safely share it. Both can be true at the same time.

And keeping something secret does not automatically mean that someone feels ashamed or that their decision is wrong.

While parents have every right to decide who knows about their fertility treatment, donor conception also involves another person: the child. And the child's relationship with this information is different. For the child, it is part of the story of how they came into the world.

According to recent reports, research supports telling donor-conceived children about their origins early, using age-appropriate language and making it part of their developing understanding of themselves, rather than a “one and done” significant conversation when the child is older (Ilioi et al., 2017; Duff & Goedeke, 2024). There can be something deeply reassuring about growing up with this knowledge, rather than having to make sense of it for the first time later in life.

In a recent development, as the child grows, the conversation also grows. Questions change, upcoming feelings emerge, and things that seemed unimportant at five may take on a different meaning at fifteen.

But what happens when parents tell their child about their conception and then ask them not to tell anyone else?

It has been revealed that parents may have understandable reasons for wanting to keep donor conception within the immediate family. They may want to protect their child from judgment, insensitive comments, or rejection.

But imagine a child who has always known they were conceived through egg donation, yet has been told not to mention it to grandparents or friends.

Perhaps a friend asks why they don't look like their mother. The child may want to explain but hesitate, remembering that their parents don't want anyone to know. In this way they are caught between wanting to talk about themselves and worrying about upsetting their parents.

In a recent development, A child may also begin to wonder: Why can we talk about this at home but not outside? Is there something wrong with how I was conceived? This is where privacy may begin to feel like secrecy. Privacy gives a child a choice about what they share, while secrecy may leave them feeling responsible for keeping something hidden.

As children grow older, they develop their own feelings about their genetic origins. Some may want to talk openly about being donor-conceived, others may prefer privacy. What matters is that the child is gradually given the language, understanding and freedom to decide what they feel comfortable sharing.

For example, they might say, “My parents needed help from an egg donor to have me.”.

Or equally, “I don't really want to talk about that.”.

According to recent reports, both are reasonable responses. The important difference is that the choice belongs to them.

According to recent reports, it is OK for parents and children to have different feelings about disclosure. What is vital is that parents recognize their own anxieties and try not to pass them on to their child. The child should feel free to develop their own feelings about their origins, without carrying the weight of their parents' worries.

This can be a complex challenge for parents to come to terms with. And it doesn't have to be neatly resolved. It can just be.

Not everyone will understand or approve. Some may have deeply held beliefs about genetics and family relationships. Others may ask intrusive questions or make insensitive comments. You cannot necessarily prevent those reactions, but you can decide how much access those individuals have to your private lives.

Depending on your family and community, it may also be crucial to prepare your child for the fact that people have different ideas about what makes a family. Not everyone will agree with your views, and they don't have to. This too is part of diversity. There are many different ways to be human.
