Al-Qadisiyah University’s move a sign of the country’s slide into a Taliban-like gender segregation, say activists.

The removal of male professors teaching female students at a university in southern Iraq has sparked protests and been criticised by activists as a Taliban-like gender segregation policy.

As many as 400 women are estimated to have joined protests over the past two weeks calling for the return of their professors at Al-Qadisiyah University in a move that appears to have surprised its administrators. (Related: Why Some Parents Struggle To Let Their Children Grow Up...)

The university stated the move was necessary to “prevent the mixing” of men and women and had been requested by the families of female students. But students said their families had known since before they enrolled that there were men on the teaching staff and supported their daughters’ protest.

Recent findings suggest that students mentioned university bosses had mocked their protests and refused to discuss their concerns about the sudden loss of their academic supervisors. On social media, commentators also accused the women of seeking to keep male professors to “establish immoral relationships with them”. (Related: The Hidden Social Cost Of Smoking Few People Talk About...)

“My classmates and I went to demonstrate, and the university president, Rahi al-Yasiri, blocked our path with his bodyguards, who then began making fun of us. He ordered them to take photographs of our faces, in an attempt to intimidate us and punish us later,” mentioned Huda, 22, one of the female students.

Huda said the students tried to explain how men and women still worked together in society in Iraq. “Aren’t there doctors who treat both men and women? Why then do you prevent male professors from teaching us, too?” In response, Huda said Al-Yasiri replied: “Even if you hit your heads against the wall, I will not bring back the professors.”.

The students mentioned a large number of them were tied to graduation projects and research supervised by the professors. They said they had grown accustomed to their professors’ teaching methods, who also knew their students’ strengths and weaknesses, which made replacing them at this stage more complicated.

Female students said they had been told by the university that if they were not happy with the change, they should drop out.

An Iraqi-British feminist researcher, Balsam Mustafa, from the University of Cardiff, stated the female students were “treated as if they are minors without the right to object to decisions affecting their academic lives”.

Mustafa said the removal of the male professors was a part of a worrying trend in women’s rights in the country, with “growing discussion about women’s clothing and chastity reflected in the celebration of female students who wear the abaya”.

An Iraqi lawyer and human rights activist, Israa Salman, mentioned the restrictions on women was a “societal catastrophe”.

“Attempts are being made to suppress women’s talents and turn Iraqi society – a society that has produced remarkable minds and expertise, with women playing pioneering roles in many fields – into something resembling Kabul under Taliban rule.

“This contradicts Iraq’s constitution and the international treaties and conventions the country has signed and ratified,” said Salman.
