Why the Most Politically Savvy Colleague Gets Things Done

A department head needs budget for a project. She knows the formal process: Write the proposal, present it at the monthly meeting, and then wait for feedback. She also knows how decisions are actually made in her organization. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

So in the week before the meeting, she has three separate conversations with people who will make or break the decision. First, she talks with the finance director, who worries about cost overruns at the end of every quarter. Then, she talks with the operations head, whose team always gets dragged into projects that sometimes go nowhere. Next, she talks to the program lead who will "volunteer" to manage the project. (Related: Director of Machine Learning Insights...)

Recent findings suggest that by the time she presents, the decision has effectively been made. People raise issues briefly as a formality because they already feel assured, and some even answer their own questions because they like the solution they were shown in advance.

Industry observers note that there are a couple of ways to look at this approach. It's definitely an effective approach. Some people would call it clever, but not everyone would mean that as a compliment.

Sources indicate that the resourceful style is one of the "dark side" personality styles: everyday patterns that can help or hinder us at work, depending on how strongly they show up and in what form. Its most extreme form resembles what psychologists call Machiavellianism, a cynical, self-serving approach to other people.

People who do this as a regular course of action share this way of viewing the world, navigating work through relationships, and many of them are transactional. They understand how power structures and networks truly exist in reality, separate from the official org chart or chain of command. They quickly pick up on who makes decisions, or who suggests the ideas that get picked up more often than not.

They tend to be strategic, focused on outcomes, and can be very adaptable when the route to a goal changes or latest obstacles emerge.

Researchers have studied the useful side of this for decades under the name "political skill." Ferris and colleagues (2005) developed a measure of it with four parts: social astuteness (reading people and situations), interpersonal influence, networking ability, and apparent sincerity (coming across as genuine).

In a recent development, A meta-analysis by Munyon and colleagues (2015) found that political skill was linked to higher productivity, career success, a better reputation, and greater job satisfaction. It predicted task performance even after accounting for broad personality traits and general intelligence.

When the researchers looked at the four aspects separately, networking, influence, and apparent sincerity predicted performance. Social astuteness on its own did not. Reading the room is a useful start, but the rarer skill is reading a room and influencing your place in it.

Sources indicate that at their best, resourceful people do exactly that. They get around obstacles that stall everyone else, and find the person who can make a decision or make the obstacles go away. They can get other people to support them to make unpopular decisions, and if they get the desired results, some people will respect and admire them for it.

According to recent reports, this can be done proactively and prosocially, or in ways that are counterproductive and manipulative. Organizations and even small teams are political whether or not anyone likes it, and someone who can find ways of making things work is necessary for any high-functioning team.

Sources indicate that the dark side emerges when people become collateral damage. Consider a colleague who builds trust with a peer during a brutal restructuring, learns that the peer is burned out and looking for another job, and later uses that confidence to edge them out of a key role when budgets tighten.

As I discussed in a post on destructive behavior in the remote office, a manipulative person can work this way with surgical precision, especially when much of the work happens across different and poorly organized communication channels.

It has been revealed that however, it is possible to operate in some gray areas while still maintaining respectful and mutually beneficial relationships. Arguably, it's a more successful strategy in the longer term.

The research on Machiavellianism shows the cost of being too ruthless. A meta-analysis by O'Boyle and colleagues (2012) found it was linked to more counterproductive work behavior, meaning behavior that harms colleagues or the organization. Its link to lower job performance was small and inconsistent, which fits the idea that this style can deliver short-term results while eroding the reliability and trust that long-term work requires.

Industry observers note that it helps to compare this style with more combative, rule-breaking tendencies that are compulsive instead of calculated. Combative people tend to push boundaries, take visible risks, and ask forgiveness rather than permission. Resourceful people usually leave the rules intact and work the system instead. They do not need to skip the approval process when they have already lined up the approvers.

That can make the resourceful style harder to spot, and its destructive form harder to challenge. Nothing improper has technically happened. The damage is a loss of trust amongst teams.

As I argued in "The Surprising Upside of Your Dark Side," the question to ask of personality styles is: How do you use them?

Industry observers note that people who read power well will always have an advantage in certain types of work. Whether others experience them as a valuable ally or a threat depends less on how well they play the game than on what they are trying to accomplish.
