Tech Report: ‘Just a Guy With a Boat on His Head’: He’s Running 52 Marathons in 52 Weeks—While Carrying a Canoe Every Step

Industry observers note that getting to the Philadelphia Marathon on November 22 proved an endurance event of its own for Travis Meier. The 38-year-old Minneapolis resident flew into Hartford, Connecticut, drove three hours into the Adirondack Mountains to pick up a canoe, then spent another five hours behind the wheel getting to Philadelphia. This was all before tackling Sunday’s 26.2 miles with the boat balanced over his head.

And yes, the canoe made it through the entire race without ever touching water.

In a recent development, “Coming from Minnesota, I can't just throw a boat in an overhead compartment," Meier told Runner's World. "It was a logistical nightmare: where to keep it, where to sneak it into the race, and how people would react to it.". (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

Despite the travel headaches, Meier came away from Philadelphia with his second personal record in as many weeks. It was another step in his latest undertaking: 52 marathons in 52 weeks, each while carrying a canoe, to raise awareness about protecting the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness in northern Minnesota.

According to recent reports, meier, who manages a distillery when he isn't hauling boats around, wasn't exactly a seasoned runner when all this started. His introduction to the sport came through the Red Bull Urban Portage, an event that involved carrying a boat through downtown Minneapolis before paddling along the Mississippi River. (Related: Course Launch Community Event...)

Recent findings suggest that “I was really bad at paddling, but I could outrun people,” he recalled. Before the event, he bought a Northstar ADK canoe off Craigslist with every intention of selling it once the race was over. Instead, it found a home on his garage wall and managed to stick around for another year. Eventually, he started entering more races with it.

It has been revealed that that eventually brought him to Minnesota's Ely Marathon in 2023, where he completed his first-ever 26.2-mile race while carrying a canoe. Since then, he's run only one conventional marathon without a boat, and what started solely as a personal experiment gradually became an opportunity to draw attention to a place he'd loved for years.

Sources indicate that the Boundary Waters, a wilderness area known for its lakes, forests, and extensive canoe routes, has been at the center of a contentious debate over proposed mining operations. Federal protections restricting mining in the surrounding watershed were rolled back earlier this year, a development that particularly frustrated Meier.

"I've been to many remote places on this earth, and very few have its caliber of perfect,” he said. “So why would someone want to mess it up?".

It has been revealed that meier had been toying with the idea of running 52 marathons in 52 weeks ever since reading about Dane Rauschenberg, who accomplished the feat in 2006. With the ongoing fight over protections for the Boundary Waters, he had his reason to give it a shot, bringing his canoe as a form of protest to May’s Taco Bell 50K. It got people asking questions, which was exactly what he wanted, and gave him encouragement to keep showing up at races with a boat on his head.

Of course, carrying a canoe for more than four hours at a time comes with some challenges, particularly when Mother Nature gets involved.

According to recent reports, during the Mankato Marathon in Minnesota, Meier battled 30mph winds along a ridge beside a cornfield. Mile 14 alone took him nearly 22 minutes, as his canoe repeatedly caught the wind and spun him around like a kite.

According to recent reports, at one point, Meier turned to a few supporters witnessing him get thrown around and asked, “When's the point of insanity?” He sent them ahead to see if the wind conditions were better ahead in the valley. Luckily, the gusts let up enough, sparing him a repeat trip through that nightmare.

The spectators in Philadelphia offered a different kind of entertainment, with plenty to say about the guy running a marathon with a canoe on his head.

Around mile three, Meier encountered a young girl who appeared scared by the enormous object approaching her. He knelt down, giving her enough confidence to return his high-five.

"Grandmas confess their love to me, and I don't know why," he mentioned of his interactions with spectators, adding that Philadelphia set a record for the number of phallic jokes he heard along the course.

It has been revealed that the jokes are part of the experience, and the attention is ultimately what he's hoping for. Meier stated fellow runners often approach him after a race to discuss the Boundary Waters and the larger concern of protecting public lands. He's also seen Instagram comments from people who knew little about the area before coming across his videos.

"They may not know anything, but they've at least gotten a 10,000-foot view of the BWCA," he said.

In a recent development, the running community has helped make the challenge possible, with sponsors pitching in for race entries and shoes while Meier covers the remaining expenses. He's even promised to refund their donations if he doesn't complete the challenge.

"My integrity is everything,” he said, “even though I'm not a company, just a guy with a boat on his head.”.

Industry observers note that his biggest sacrifice so far has been giving up so much of his free time. Between a full-time job, travel, recovery, and the weekly races, there’s no room for a vacation abroad. He has considered tackling back-to-back marathons to create some breathing room. His body hasn't agreed to the arrangement.

It has been revealed that as for what would make all 52 races worthwhile, Meier has a specific outcome in mind: permanent legislation protecting the Boundary Waters. Here’s the good news: proposals exist in both the House of Representatives and Senate. Even so, he knows that may be a tall order. But his presence—and effort—are creating awareness.

"If it doesn't happen, which it likely won't, at least the effort started conversations about our public lands," he stated.
