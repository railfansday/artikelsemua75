The Making of Oxygene

According to recent reports, in late 1976, an album was released which re-wrote the rulebook of synthesizers and electronic music and captured the hearts and imagination of the entire world. Composed by the then unknown 28-year-old French artist Jean-Michel Jarre, the album Oxygène broke out of France to become a top 10 album in seven different countries, spending a remarkable 24 weeks in the UK album charts and selling now close to 20 million copies worldwide.

Uniquely, the album consisted of just six tracks, numbered Oxygène Parts 1 through 6, and was entirely instrumental with no acoustic instruments whatsoever, which was unheard of in 1976.

Jean Michel Jarre tells the story of the making of his ground-breaking album in his own words.

"Around 1969, I felt that the world of classical music, the world of rock and other forms of music were a bit narrow and living in their own worlds. I had the feeling it was the right time to explore other fields to perhaps see if a combination of different styles were possible. “The main difference with electronic music is that you create music in terms of sound, not just notes. It’s something else entirely. When I was a teenager, I always liked processing my guitar or organ or whatever, playing the tape backwards and changing the speed. Then the drummer in my band said, ‘You should meet with these crazy guys at Groupe De Recherches Musicales [GRM] in Paris who are working in French public radio. They’re doing the same kinds of things as you, and they have labs.’. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

“When I first went there, I was immediately seduced by what they were doing. Electronic music owes a lot to public radio stations - the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, the German lab where Stockhausen was working and, of course, the one in Paris.

It has been revealed that "Pierre Schaeffer was one of the most brilliant guys of the century in terms of what he brought to the music world. He created the notion of Musique concrète and influenced me a lot in this direction. Saying that, I felt that contemporary music had got a very elitist attitude - a bit too intellectual vis-à-vis the music and it created barriers between the audience and yourself. I wanted to be much more open to the audience.

"I stayed with Pierre Schaeffer [and Pierre Henry] for three years and I also went to Cologne to work with Stockhausen. He was totally crazy, but a real genius. He considered himself to be made of sound, biologically, so he had a kind of surrealistic approach. Crazy, but realistic at the same time. It was very interesting.

According to recent reports, "When I left GRM and through to 1976, I did production work for some rock artists, earning enough to set up a studio in my kitchen. I didn’t have much equipment, though, just a few guitar pedals and my first synthesizer, the VCS3, which looked like a telephone exchange. I realised that using a Revox tape machine to delay the sound that came out of one loudspeaker created a huge sense of space.

Recent findings suggest that “The British-made VCS3 from EMS [Electronic Music Studios, based in London] was created by a genius called Peter Zinovieff. It was a kind of poor European version of the Moog. In those days Europe was not as rich as the States, so the sound was drier. In a sense, it was a kind of punk synthesizer - there was something very harsh and rough about it. I loved it; I still take one on stage with me today.

“At the time, I was obsessed with this idea of creating a bridge between experimentation and pop melody. I liked this kind of challenge, playing around with sound design and integrating melodies. Oxygène was a new start for music with analogue synthesizers".

Sources indicate that the album was recorded on a Scully recorder with just eight tracks, and all through a home-made mixer. On top of the VCS3, Jarre had managed to add some other classic equipment including EMS Synthi AKS and ARP 2600 synths, an Eminent String Ensemble, an RMI Harmonic Synthesizer, and Farfisa organ, while an RMI Keyboard Computer was used for some sequencing.

It has been revealed that jarre continues, “I really did it in a kind of home way using what, I think, was one of the first home studios. I had just three or four synthesizers and was using a Scully eight–track and a mixture of Ampex 256 and 3M tape. The whole album was done on just one eight–track and you can hear that in the piece — it’s quite minimalist and I think that contributes to its timelessness.

“Nothing is repeated the same way. Each sound, even if appears to be the same, has a slightly different attack and release, and, as the sequences are being made by hand, by definition they are not the same. Even the sounds of the drum machines were filtered in a very subtle way to give life inside the patterns.

Recent findings suggest that ”Michel Geiss was a maintenance technician, at a famous French record firm at that time called Barclay and I met him because he was very interested in electronic instruments. He visited my home and we started to experiment. He was teaching me to use the first semi-modular synths I could put my hands on. And then step by step we worked together, Michel mainly as a kind of sound engineer for me.

It has been revealed that “For Oxygene, he was working at his record firm as a maintenance guy during the day, and he was coming to work with me at night. In those days I probably had the first home studio in France, when the serious music was done in serious recording studios with a window where God was the sound engineer behind the window, and we were just on the other side as musicians. For electronic music you need to match the mixing area with the main studio to record your own sounds.

“So, I had this home studio in a kitchen in the flat I had in Paris, and Michel joined me, using one of the first eight-track tape recorders to record my music. I only had two tapes. So, I had no choice if I was not happy with something to erase what I'd done the previous day, and this is the reason why I have no archive of material from Oxygène. It's such a shame.

Sources indicate that “And that is also the reason why if you take the master tape of Oxygène, it is made up of two different tapes. Right in the middle of part one of Oxygène, I had one piece I really liked, where suddenly in the first part you have this kind of brassy, quite epic brassy moment. This is coming from another tape that I recorded another time.

It has been revealed that “I thought to myself that it's really matching very well. So, I did a very strong edit, quite dramatic edit, by cutting the two tapes together, even the colours are different. One is pink, one is black. It's totally crazy. And whilst you can't hear the edit, it's quite challenging to cut an eight-track tape like this.

“With so little to work with, I had to be creative. My old Mellotron only had a few working keys, but I still managed to write Oxygene Part II on it. My primitive drum machine - a Korg Mini Pops was the sort of thing people played in pubs, but by using Sellotape I could make it play two preset rhythms at the same time, creating cool beats. Oxygène Part IV, which became iconic, was a mixture of [presets] Slow Rock and Rock, while Part VI combined Rumba and Bossa Nova.

“But when I finished the album, because Oxygène consisted of entirely instrumental music, none of the record companies thought it would work. The only people who took the risk were a small French manufacturer called Dreyfus Music. Even after it had become a hit in France, people stated it couldn't possibly be a success anywhere else. Then eventually, after it got to the top of the charts in Britain and all over Europe, the critics explained that it was successful because of its originality and because of its use of melody. It's easy to be wise after the event!

Recent findings suggest that “I made the first album back in the days of vinyl, when we were always thinking about sides one and two. The other characteristic of Oxygène is that it has sunny melodies but it’s also quite dark, like the cover artwork.”.

Studio engineer and would-be long-time collaborator Michel Geiss, has fond memories of his first encounter with Jean Michel Jarre, recalling, "Oxygene marked the beginning of my collaboration with Jean-Michel. At the time, I was working during the day at a large studio complex in Paris called Studios Barclay. I was employed there as a technician. The recording studios were located on the upper floors, while I worked in the workshop downstairs. My body was downstairs, but my mind was upstairs! I felt strongly attracted to the world of music engineering, although I wasn't particularly interested in the equipment I had been assigned to work with.

According to recent reports, "In fact, I found the right balance when I began working with Jean-Michel at his own studio around the same time. Every day, once my working day at Barclay had finished, I would join Jean-Michel in his studio, at his request, to work on his album. We would continue until I had to catch the last train home. I remember him calling me every afternoon to ask if I could come over.

"This was my first real opportunity to work in a studio as a sound and music collaborator, and I thoroughly enjoyed it. Jean-Michel would also occasionally ask for my musical opinion on various matters. I programmed some specific sounds on the ARP 2600, including the main theme sound in Oxygene IV and the sound of the waves in Part VI.

Sources indicate that "It was a close working relationship, and I felt that I had finally found my rightful place. It suited me much better than simply being an electronics specialist, which was my background before I met him.

"My main role during the recording of Oxygene was to record Jean-Michel while he played his instruments, as he needed someone to handle the recording process.

In a recent development, "At the time, he was also keen to expand his relatively limited collection of instruments in order to explore new sounds. I suggested that we take a look at the RMI keyboards, which were sold in France by Piano Center in Montreuil. Jean-Michel immediately agreed, saying, 'OK, let's go!'.

"When we arrived at the music shop, Jean-Michel discovered the two instruments I had suggested: the RMI Harmonic Synthesizer and the RMI Keyboard Computer. The latter used punched cardboard cards to store its sound information. The Harmonic Synthesizer had a truly distinctive sound and featured an arpeggiator. It would later prove to be a key instrument on the album, particularly on Oxygene IV.".

The album’s iconic artwork came about when Jarre was visiting Marquet Gallery in Paris and a piece caught his eye that he was instantly drawn to. A painting of a skull hidden within the Earth. The painting was created by young artist Michel Granger, titled Oxygène. By chance, Granger happened to be in the gallery at the same time and the pair spoke and Jean-Michel bought the painting to use as the album cover and adopted its title.

Michel Granger recalls, “I was having my first major show in Paris. On display was a 30x40cm watercolour called Oxygène and Jean-Michel bought it, telling me he’d love to use it as an album cover. Oxygène was part of a series about the damage being done to our planet. It was quite a violent image for a record sleeve.

In a recent development, “The only change I made was to the background, making it square to fit the album shape. Jean-Michel and I chose the typography and that was that. We were both similar ages and he was very passionate about music and painting. We would have long conversations in the evenings, always at the same pizzeria, then he went home to compose all night.”.

Jean Michel Jarre commented on the artwork,"What I like about this picture is that it isn’t necessarily pessimistic. It rather poses the question: where are we going?”.

With its iconic title and cover set, and after the album was mixed at Studio Gang by Jean-Pierre Janiaud, an accomplished engineer, Jarre took the album to Francis Dreyfus, owner of the Disques Motors record label, who he had worked for during his songwriting days with Christophe. Dreyfus loved the album and was convinced it could be a success.

Dreyfus pressed 50,000 copies on his own label to sell in France and released the album in December 1976. Uniquely and a brainwave of Dreyfus and Jarre, they offered the album to nationwide hi-fi shops, given the album's innovative use of stereo. The idea being that high-end hi-fi buyers would buy a copy of the album to go with their hardware purchase. By April 1977, sales in France had reached 70,000 copies despite very little promotion. Legend has it that some people returned the LP believing the iconic white noise was a manufacturing defect.

Sources indicate that finding an international label to distribute was harder. Initially no record organization was interested in the album, citing no singer, no guitars, no drummer, no proper track titles, no obvious singles, and to round it off, it was French.

It has been revealed that everything changed when Fred Haayen, the upcoming head of Polydor Records in London discovered the record in a French record store and signed it to the label. He was instrumental in persuading BBC Radio 1 to play the album in full the week of release. The only other album to ever receive such an honour was Pink Floyd's Dark Side of the Moon.

Sources indicate that the album was mostly slated by the UK's mainstream music press, with the NME calling it “another interminable sonic cruise and infuriatingly derivative.” but the public loved the album and on August 23rd 1977, Oxygène entered the UK album charts at number 19, climbing and remaining in the top 10 for the next 11 weeks. After moving to the United States to lead Polydor USA, Haayen also spearheaded the launch of the album in the US, to achieve official platinum status.

In a recent development, we’ll let Jarre conclude the story, “It’s now sold something like 18 million copies and, no matter what I do, I am defined by Oxygène. But that’s OK. It’s funny. At first, the album was played in hi-fi shops as an example of 'state-of-the-art sound'. I didn’t tell them I made it in my kitchen.

“When you’ve been lucky enough to have some kind of success with your first project, it will always be part of your DNA, no matter what you do afterwards. It’s like the first movie for Tarantino or Kubrick. For me, it’s the same with Oxygène. I love many other things that I’ve done and I’m very proud of them all, but Oxygène is something unique.”.
