Extreme heat is killing more people, and scientists have estimated how much warming is to blame

A Lancet Countdown report found that annual heat-related deaths rose 36% worldwide, with human-caused warming accounting for more than two-thirds of the recent toll.

The Paris Agreement took effect in 2016. From 2016 through 2023, an estimated 540,800 people died from heat worldwide each year, on average. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

That was about 36% higher than the annual average from 2006 through 2015.

The share of heat-related deaths attributed to human-caused warming rose from 55% to 68%.

A project based at University College London, the Lancet Countdown, produced the figures in its tenth annual report, which tracks 54 measures of how climate change affects health. (Related: Tech Report: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

It was written by 146 researchers and produced with the World Health Organization. Every death count in it is an estimate from models, not a tally of death certificates.

In an interview with Earth.com, Jennifer Israelsson, a senior research fellow at the Lancet Countdown, said the finding that surprised her most was not a number from the heat data.

It was “the sharp decline in political engagement with the climate-health agenda, despite multiple years with record-breaking temperatures, deadly heatwaves and devastating flooding events across the globe, and the intensification of hurricanes,” Israelsson noted.

Between 2006 and 2015, heat was linked to an estimated 398,400 deaths a year. Between 2016 and 2023, it was linked to 540,800 a year, with a range of roughly 369,000 to 681,000.

Near the end of that stretch came the worst year. Heat was linked to a record 710,000 deaths in 2023, and the authors attributed 71.5% of them to human-caused warming.

The researchers attributed an estimated 2.96 million heat-related deaths to human-caused warming from 2016 through 2023.

Annual heat-related deaths attributed to human-caused warming rose by an average of 7,400 per year from 2006 through 2015. From 2016 through 2023, they rose by an average of 15,600 per year.

Sources indicate that the researchers used daily temperatures and death records reported at daily, weekly or monthly intervals across 129 countries. They then used models to estimate the global toll.

The second question was how many of those deaths would have occurred in a world with the same weather minus the warming humans have added.

It has been revealed that the authors call the gap between the two the attributable toll. The attribution step is newly introduced to this year’s report, and the ranges around each figure are wide.

In a recent development, heat also keeps people from working. The authors estimate 573 billion potential work hours were lost to heat in 2025, 35% above the average for 2006 to 2015.

The lost income came to $1.07 trillion, about 0.9% of global economic output.

The losses were equivalent to 5% of GDP in countries with a low Human Development Index and 0.5% in countries with a very high index.

Industry observers note that the same decade brought real gains. Investment in clean energy reached a record $2.1 trillion in 2025, 82% more than went into fossil fuels.

The share of electricity from modern renewables rose from 5.5% in 2016 to 13.5% in 2023. Fossil fuels still supplied 80.7% of the world’s energy in 2023.

Where air pollution from fossil fuels fell, the authors counted the benefit. In 75 of 140 countries assessed, cleaner air prevented an estimated 323,000 deaths a year.

It has been revealed that air conditioning tells a mixed story. Its use grew from 32% of households in 2016 to 42% in 2024, and the authors estimate it prevented more than 360,000 heat deaths in each of 2022 and 2023.

In countries with a low Human Development Index, only 3% of households had air conditioning in 2024.

Israelsson told Earth.com the single change that would save the most lives from heat is a heat-health action plan.

That means a warning system that reaches hospitals and the public in time, plus measures for cooler buildings and safe working conditions.

She pointed to the Netherlands, which she noted has seen heat deaths fall since it adopted a heatwave plan.

Extreme drought covered 51.8% of the world’s land for at least a month in 2025, the second-highest extent on record after 2023.

Since 2016, the area in drought each year has grown about eight times as fast as it did in the previous decade.

Very heavy rainfall affected an average of 17.2% of the world’s land each year from 2016 through 2025, up from 14.8% in the previous decade.

Such downpours can increase the risk of floods, landslides and waterborne disease.

The number of people affected per storm or flood rose more slowly in countries with health early warning systems.

The annual increase was 8.6% smaller than in countries without those systems.

Sources indicate that whether the acceleration continues is the open question, and the authors wrote as much. The heat figures run only through 2023, and the drought and rainfall trends through 2025.

The authors’ answer is to have climate change formally declared a global health emergency.

Israelsson stated the WHO’s existing emergency mechanism was built for short, acute outbreaks like Ebola and “is not designed for long-term emergencies.”.

A latest instrument with the same kind of roles and responsibilities, aimed at long-term resilience, is needed, she mentioned.

“A decade after the Paris Agreement, our findings are unambiguous: the health harms of climate change are growing faster than the world’s response to them, and in many cases even accelerating,” noted Marina Romanello, the Lancet Countdown’s executive director.

The report was published ahead of the COP31 climate talks.

The full study was published in the journal The Lancet.
