Here's the Average Social Security Benefit at 62 vs. 70

By Kailey Hagen, CFP® – Nov 22, 2026 at 6:00AM EST.

The gap between claiming early and waiting is often viewed through the lens of a single check. As of December 2025, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA), the average Social Security benefit for someone age 70 is $2,274.68 per month. That's $850.28 more than the $1,424.40 monthly average for those age 62.

Over a full year, this monthly difference amounts to $10,203.36 in additional income. While many workers begin receiving benefits as soon as they reach the earliest eligibility age, the result is a significantly lower baseline for the duration of retirement. These averages reflect a snapshot of everyone presently receiving benefits at these specific ages as of December 2025, the most recent data available.

According to recent reports, these figures don't yet include the 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) that will take effect in 2027. That adjustment will add about $51 a month to the age-62 average and about $82 a month to the age-70 average. These raises are supposed to help checks keep pace with inflation, though they don't always do so and can vary widely each year. (Related: Tech Report: University releases $5m smart system that ...)

According to recent reports, the size of your checks varies in part on your age at sign-up and your full retirement age (FRA). This ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. This age serves as the anchor for the permanent reductions applied to early claimers and the credits earned by those who wait.

According to recent reports, the SSA bases your benefit on your average monthly income during your 35 highest-earning years, which it adjusts for inflation and runs through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. Social Security was designed to replace only about 40% of pre-retirement income. This share is well short of what most retirees need, so benefits usually function as supplemental income alongside savings or other sources, regardless of your claiming age.

Claiming at age 62 locks in a permanent 30% reduction if your FRA is 67. Conversely, waiting past your FRA earns you delayed retirement credits worth 8% a year. This increase totals 24% by age 70. These mechanics create the wide range of averages seen across different age groups.

Gender differences also play a role in these population averages. At age 62, the average benefit among men is $1,572.83, while the average among women is $1,285.50, a gap of $287.33 per month. By age 70, the gap reaches $505.54 per month, with men averaging $2,529.62 and women averaging $2,024.08.

Sources indicate that longevity also factors into the financial picture for most retirees. At age 65, men are expected to live about 18 more years, and women about 21 more years, according to the SSA. If you live an average lifespan, you tend to receive roughly the same total over your lifetime regardless of when you claim.

According to recent reports, these figures are snapshots of current beneficiaries, not a guarantee of what any one person will receive. While the averages provide a benchmark, your own benefit depends on your unique work history and when you choose to claim.

According to recent reports, claiming Social Security at 62 can be advantageous if you have a short life expectancy or if you are unable to work and don't have enough personal savings to cover your costs on your own. It might also make sense if you're married and your spouse has earned significantly more than you throughout their career. Claiming early may enable them to delay their own application until they qualify for larger checks.

On the other hand, you might prefer to put off your Social Security application until 70 if you have a family history of longevity, are still working well into your 60s, or have enough personal savings that you don't need your checks right now. If you're the higher-earning spouse, delaying your application could increase your future household benefits as well.

Recent findings suggest that it's also fine to choose a claiming age that's somewhere in the middle, like your FRA. This helps you avoid the early claiming penalty without requiring you to wait until 70 to start receiving checks.
