One longtime Minnesota farmer has spent years grappling with the loss of several of his calves killed by gray wolves, also known as timber wolves, roaming around his property in the middle of the night.

"We can walk right outside of our door and hear the howling of these timber wolves, and it'll send shivers down your spine because you have seen what they can do, and they're the top-of-the-line predator," Shayne Isane told Fox News Digital. "There's nothing that hunts them. No other animals they are afraid of.".

Farmers across the Great Lakes region are grappling with gray wolves killing their livestock, reigniting a fierce debate over wildlife management. (Courtesy of Jonathan Miller and Shayne Isane).

Sources indicate that isane, who has farmed for nearly four decades, said he is one of many farmers struggling to defend his livestock from gray wolves. Despite losing thousands of dollars and fearing for the safety of his nearly 300 cattle, Isane said he is unable to handle the threat himself.

Sources indicate that "Even if we see them attacking our livestock, we can't shoot or try to help out our animals," he said. "It's just against the law. We can't do it, only in the case of the threat of human life, which is a tough sell for us, because when you see them very close to your livelihood, your families and your livestock, it's a huge concern.".

According to recent reports, as President Donald Trump moves to reconsider federal protections, Isane mentioned he is one of many farmers hoping to gain more control over the growing threat. The blueprint could affect states across the country, including Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, California and Colorado.

Industry observers note that the Interior Department has until Dec. 3 to determine whether gray wolves and their subspecies, Mexican wolves, have met the recovery criteria under the Endangered Species Act. If the criteria are met, the department must begin the process of removing or reducing federal protections.

In a statement to Fox News Digital, a department spokesperson noted the department had no updates on that decision but "recognizes the importance of effective wildlife management and the challenges wolf depredation can pose for livestock producers.".

One of Isane's largest losses came when three of his young calves were killed in a single night. He said the wolves often drag away or dispose of the carcasses themselves, making it difficult to confirm the attack to state investigators.

In Minnesota, a farmer must have the carcass of their livestock in order to confirm a suspected wolf attack and report the loss within 48 hours to receive reimbursement.

"To lose one animal or two animals is $3,000 a piece, and so that really can affect our bottom line," he remarked.

A Minnesota Department of Agriculture spokesperson told Fox News Digital its program "provides 100% reimbursement of verified losses at full market value" and pointed to its Wolf Livestock Depredation Grant.

"While we can assist farmers with livestock prevention and loss, wolf management does not fall under the work of the Minnesota Department of Agriculture," the spokesperson wrote.

But Isane said keeping the livestock alive is only part of the "constant battle." He said wolves can cause "added stress" to the herd. "They're constantly being harassed by the wolves," he said. "We see it in weights. They lose weight, they are constantly on edge, and it affects their overall health.".

Isane mentioned many deterrent methods suggested by the state have proven unsuccessful, saying wolves are able to "outsmart" them.

"They just don't work," he said.

Sources indicate that isane emphasized that environmentalists aren’t seeing the full picture. He said the wolf population had spiraled "out of control.".

According to recent reports, "The first 30 years of my life, I hardly saw a timber wolf, and now I've seen multiple ones, 50 to 100, probably in the last 25 years," Isane mentioned. "It's gotten increasingly worse.".

According to the Minnesota DNR, there were about 2,400 gray wolves in Minnesota in 2026, with no statistically significant change from last year.

"The Minnesota DNR is very confident in our wolf research and population estimates, which indicate a stable population, not one of unconstrained or rapid population growth," a spokesperson stated in a statement to Fox News Digital.

Isane mentioned he welcomes Trump's efforts to fix a problem that has "no method of controlling.".

White House deputy press secretary Taylor Rogers told Fox News Digital the Biden administration’s "radical environmental policies" left ranchers defenseless as "predators wiped out their livestock.".

Recent findings suggest that "As a result, the national cattle herd reached a 75-year low, driving up prices for consumers," Rogers wrote. "As promised, President Trump is reversing these harmful policies and is fighting for America’s ranchers.".

Shawn Regan, C3 Journal editor and Manhattan Institute senior fellow, noted states can effectively manage recovered wolf populations without them being listed under the Endangered Species Act.

"It's not the best tool for continuing to manage recovered populations into the future," Regan remarked. "There's a reason why the act has a delisting process to allow for transition of those species to state management.".

Delisting gray wolves has long been part of Trump's agenda.

During his first term, the administration removed federal protections for the species across most states. Six environmental groups, represented by environmental law group Earthjustice, sued the Trump administration in 2021 over the decision.

Sources indicate that A federal judge overturned the decision in 2022, restoring protections across most states except in the Northern Rockies, where states still have control. The 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals is at the moment weighing whether that ruling should be upheld.

Some environmental groups involved in the lawsuit told Fox News Digital Trump’s executive order could reverse years of recovery efforts and urged the administration to focus on other issues facing farmers.

"The administration is offering up wolves as a bargaining chip while failing to address the real economic pressures burdening American farmers and ranchers," Sara Amundson, president of Humane World Action Fund, remarked.

Eric Clewis, senior Northern Rockies representative for Defenders of Wildlife, echoed this, saying, "Framing wolves as the villain distracts from policies actually squeezing the ranching industry, particularly because wolves account for less than 1% of total livestock loss.".

Collette Adkins, a senior attorney and director of the Center for Biological Diversity’s carnivore program, worries the implications could stretch even further, saying removing federal protections could "erase decades of recovery work.".

She added that the "tiny fraction of livestock operators" who have conflicts with wolves can help by doing "a better job" learning to live with them peacefully.

Environmentalists have advocated for years against delisting, arguing it could lead to culling, trapping and trophy hunting and reduce populations they believe have not fully recovered.

Independent Women’s Forum Center for Energy and Conservation Director Gabriella Hoffman told Fox News Digital there is a possibility that the move to delist gray wolves may only be a short-term solution once it heads to Congress.

"Do we have to worry about lawsuits potentially blocking this from going into effect?" Hoffman, who also recently investigated wolf attacks in Wisconsin for a conservation documentary, noted. "Congress not following through with their duty to codify this permanently and ensure this doesn't go back and forth?".

Hoffman noted delisting could actually prevent the species from becoming endangered again.

"Leaning too much on emotions would undermine the science and also I think put the wolf in further danger and make it even more imperiled than it is if you have a management system in place," she stated.

She added that wolf attacks on livestock are a sign of thriving conservation efforts.

"While it is problematic that wolf depredation or attacks by wolves is increasing, that's actually a positive sign, as counterintuitive as it sounds, of their status as a species," she said.

Jonathan Miller, a Michigan dairy farmer since the 1970s who switched to raising a herd of roughly 100 cattle, mentioned protective fencing has created a "balance" with wolves.

"It's very effective to keep them away," he noted.

He said the attacks haven’t hurt his business financially, but managing the fencing has added some extra challenges.

"It's a lot of extra work management trying to move the fence, keep it electrified so that it's effective," he said.

Earlier this month, Miller found the carcasses of two of his healthy sheep, which he blamed on his "lack of management" in putting up the barrier.

"You hate to see a wolf take one of your animals, but we lose animals, and it's just another factor," he mentioned.

When the family lost their first calf to a wolf in the early 2000s, Miller remarked it "kind of changed things.".

It was the first time he laid eyes on the predator that "probably only weighed about 60 pounds, but was massive enough to drag a calf across the yard.".

Sources indicate that miller mentioned wolf sightings in people's yards have become less common since locals began handling the issue themselves. However, he supports Trump's push for state management rather than "local people illegally controlling the numbers of the wolves.".

He hopes the move can help farmers and wolves finally coexist.

"I don't want to eliminate the wolves," he said. "I love to see wolves. I try to get pictures, but there has to be control.".
