In July, Tesla posted a video of a 93-year-old San Diego resident named Lois using a red Tesla equipped with Full Self-Driving (FSD) Supervised software to make road trips to see her family and run errands.

“I don’t have to wait for someone, and it’s been a wonderful experience,” Lois remarked to the camera after a video montage of her visiting her grandkids and smiling at the beach.

It has been revealed that tesla has promoted FSD Supervised, which can change lanes and make turns, as a way for seniors to keep their independence. The manufacturer is now rolling out a fresh version of the software. Reposting another video in March of an 87-year-old woman testing out an FSD-equipped Cybertruck, the company said “FSD Supervised can give you back your freedom.” Elon Musk has also said that Tesla self-driving “enables safer mobility for senior citizens” on X. (Related: Bus manufacturer apologizes after driver licks handrail...)

But Tesla’s promise comes with a catch: FSD Supervised still requires a driver to monitor the road. Tesla added “Supervised” to the name after California regulators accused it of misleading customers about what the software could do. That raises a safety dilemma for older drivers who are turning to FSD Supervised as their driving abilities decline. Tesla itself has cautioned that none of FSD Supervised’s features can replace an attentive person at the wheel, which is why some legal experts question marketing it to older drivers. Lawyers say the pitch could expose Tesla, too: a federal jury has already found the tech giant partly liable for a fatal crash involving Autopilot, its more basic driver-assistance system. (Related: Tech Report: Rockstar accused of tracing furry artwork ...)

“A technology that requires a capable driver to supervise it should not be marketed as a magical mobility solution for drivers who aren’t capable of safely driving without it,” Angel Reyes, a personal injury attorney based in Texas, told Fortune.

Sources indicate that FSD Supervised could be dangerous for older drivers because it depends on them being able to spot and react quickly to hazards. A study by University of Iowa researchers last year found drivers ages 75 to 84 were more likely to be responsible for crashes involving right turns compared to drivers ages 45 to 64, which the researchers remarked may be related to reduced physical flexibility or worsened eyesight. The same age group was also more likely to be at fault in crashes at traffic signals, which may be due to declining vision or slower reaction times, the study’s lead author said.

Recent findings suggest that “The seniors who would benefit most from a car that drives itself are often the ones least able to supervise one,” Brian White, a personal injury trial lawyer who deals with car crashes, told Fortune. “If someone is relying on it because of slower reactions, vision loss or memory problems, those same limits make them a poor backup for the software.”.

According to recent reports, tesla has faced some scrutiny over its automated-driving tech. Fortune previously reported its robotaxi fleet in Austin had been involved in 14 crashes over 800,000 miles driven, or one crash for every 57,000 miles, which was about eight times the average crash rate of American drivers recorded by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. Traffic signals are also where FSD has drawn federal scrutiny: the NHTSA also started investigating Tesla last year over instances of its FSD-engaged cars running red lights and “driving against the proper direction of travel on public roadways.”.

The agency initially identified six reports of crashes involving an FSD-engaged Tesla running a red light and colliding with other cars in the intersection. Four of those crashes resulted in one or more reported injuries. The investigation will look at “whether there was prior warning or adequate time for the driver to respond to the unexpected behavior or to safely supervise the automated driving task.”.

Sources indicate that white noted there’s a liability risk for Tesla if its FSD Supervised marketing makes older drivers put too much trust in it. He cited a potential legal precedent for this: A Miami jury ordered Tesla to pay $243 million last year after finding its driving-assistance tech Autopilot partly responsible for a fatal crash. A federal judge upheld the verdict in February, though Tesla has appealed.

“A company that markets a feature in a way that leads people to trust it too much can share responsibility,” he noted.

Tesla did not respond to Fortune’s requests for comment.
