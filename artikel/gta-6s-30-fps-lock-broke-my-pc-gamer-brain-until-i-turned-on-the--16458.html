GTA 6’s 30 FPS lock broke my PC gamer brain — until I turned on the one TV setting I swore I’d never touch

If you’ve ever bought a new TV and invited a tech nerd like me into your living room, you already know I’m grabbing your remote, diving into the picture settings, and disabling motion smoothing.

For movies and prestige TV, it is a crime against humanity — turning a cinematic masterpiece into an episode of "Days of Our Lives" shot on a camcorder. I hate it. Tom Cruise hates it. You should hate it too. (Related: Latest Update: Should I Quit My Job? A Decision Framewo...)

And yet, here I am, playing Grand Theft Auto VI with motion smoothing cranked on, and refusing to turn it off.

Industry observers note that to understand how I ended up committing TV heresy, you have to understand the shock to the system that is GTA 6’s frame rate. Coming straight from my monster PC rig playing games like Resident Evil Requiem at up to 214 FPS, dropping back down to a strict 30 FPS console lock feels like wading through treacle. (Related: Industry News: Judge blocks construction of Trump’s arc...)

But on top of that, as I’ve seen (and you’ve seen on Digital Foundry’s test), that 30 FPS lock isn’t an actual lock. It can dip into the mid-20s during heavy scenes. Coming from super fluidity, that 30 FPS is brutal to adapt to.

So out of sheer desperation (and because we’re stuck waiting for the PC port), I broke the golden rule and toggled my TV’s smooth motion setting on.

And I hate to admit it, but it’s basically bootleg DLSS Frame Generation for your living room.

Just like PC frame generation tricks of old, your TV’s motion interpolation processor looks at two real frames from your console, guesses what should happen in between them, and inserts an artificial frame to smooth out the motion.

In a recent development, the moment I flipped it on in GTA 6, that nauseating camera stutter when cruising down Ocean Beach vanished. Panning around a crowded street or taking a high-speed corner suddenly felt dramatically more fluid — nowhere near 60 fps, and definitely not my desktop rig, but miles smoother than the choppy presentation.

Sources indicate that before the AV forums come for my head with pitchforks, let’s be completely honest about the trade-offs. There is no such thing as a free lunch in frame interpolation:.

According to recent reports, because your TV has to wait for the next frame before it can invent the one in the middle, motion smooth adds latency between pressing a button and seeing the result on screen. In twitchy, competitive games, that’s a death sentence.

But in a Rockstar open-world game — where character movement is already famously weighty and deliberate — I never noticed that extra latency.

Your TV’s processor is just guessing after all, and while it is a good guesser, there are some moments of weird shimmering around fast-moving objects and some boiling around on-screen elements like subtitles. Probably compounded by the fact GTA 6 is already running its own resolution scaling tech under the hood.

Standard Game Mode and VRR (Variable Refresh Rate) automatically lock out motion smoothing on many TVs. Depending on your set, you either have to turn on the dedicated low-latency Game Motion Plus mode (found on newer Samsung and LG sets) or sacrifice VRR to unlock it.

The silver lining, though, is that with this being a 30 FPS game, it’s not high enough to require VRR anyway.

If GTA 6’s 30 FPS cap is giving your eyes a workout and you want to test the dark arts yourself, don’t just blindly max out every slider.

If your TV has a low-latency interpolation option (like Samsung’s Game Motion Plus or LG’s TruMotion tweaks), use that before turning off Game Mode completely.

Set Judder Reduction to a middle ground (around 5-7 out of 10) rather than maxing it out. You want to take the harsh edge off camera pans without turning your car into a blurry smudge.

It has been revealed that this is optional based on personal taste. Test how your TV’s smoothing looks with GTA’s built-in motion blur turned off. For a lot of OLED owners, letting the TV handle the smoothing cleanly beats stacking two different blur effects on top of each other.

Industry observers note that do I want to play GTA 6 at native 240 FPS on my PC rig? Obviously. The second Rockstar drops the PC version, I’ll be uninstalling this hack from my brain and never looking back.

Recent findings suggest that I still wouldn’t be caught dead watching a movie with motion smoothing turned on. But until PC gamers get their turn in Vice City, breaking the ultimate TV rule is the best survival trick I’ve found for making a 30 FPS masterpiece actually feel better to play.

In a recent development, also yes, my wife has taken note of the fact that I share the same name as the male protagonist. And yes, she is side-eyeing how I treat Lucia hard.
