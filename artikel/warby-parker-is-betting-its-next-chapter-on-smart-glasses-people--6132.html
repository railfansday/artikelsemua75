Warby Parker is betting its next chapter on smart glasses people actually want to wear

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Warby Parker is one of the clear winners of the 2010s-era direct-to-consumer (DTC) wave. It and other upstart digital-first brands like Allbirds shoes, Away luggage, and Casper mattresses used e-commerce savvy to cut out middlemen like wholesalers, upending their industries and selling straight to shoppers.

While many of its peers from that era have struggled or shut down, Warby Parker retains an empire of 360 stores; its revenue increased 10% to $245 million in its most recent quarter. Still, the eyewear company is grappling with soft growth in fresh active customers—only 3.4% last quarter—and a sluggish eyewear market overall. In a recent development, in search of its next substantial growth engine, Warby Parker is planting its flag in the promising but still tiny market for smart glasses, teaming up with Google. The tech behemoth, along with partner Samsung, will provide the AI technology behind the glasses, including a high-resolution display tucked inside one lens that wearers can control with a wristband or watch. (Related: Hyperparameter Search with Transformers and Ray Tune...)

For Warby Parker, whose own innovations have included a virtual try-on and features that enable customers to choose glasses from home, the partnership could bolster its tech-forward reputation, tapping its strengths in design and retail to win eyeglass buyers of the future.

Recent findings suggest that “Glasses are the original wearable, right?” says Warby Parker co-founder and co-CEO Neil Blumenthal on Fortune’s Behind the Business YouTube series. “The combination of form and function is something we can bring to this new category and create something that has a ton of technology in it.”.

It has been revealed that the Warby Parker glasses, which will start at $395, hit the market on Nov. 12 and are taking on an early market leader in Meta, whose Ray-Ban smart glasses are designed and made in partnership with Italy’s EssilorLuxottica, the largest eyewear tech giant in the world. Meta’s glasses represented about 69% of smart glasses so far this year. EssilorLuxottica has noted it sold 7 million pairs of AI-supported glasses last year.

Google’s and Meta’s decisions to team up with established eyewear brands like Warby Parker and EssilorLuxottica demonstrate that they understand that so-called intelligent eyewear, as Warby Parker refers to it, must be aesthetically pleasing and less sci-fi-like to achieve mass appeal.

“We’ve always innovated in technology,” says Dave Gilboa, Warby Parker’s other co-CEO and co-founder. “But our north star is that someone has to want to put this on their face.”.

Recent findings suggest that indeed, the “smart” aspect of the Warby Parker glasses is subtle. The cameras on the front of the frame could pass for a screw. Wearers can start filming or take a photograph with the glasses by pressing a small button near the temple. Outward-facing lights illuminate when the user is recording, a feature that helps assuage concerns that wearers will use smart glasses to photograph or film surreptitiously.

Recent findings suggest that warby Parker commands just 1% of the U.S. eyewear market, but its history as a digital-first brand and its reputation for simple but nice-looking design have made it a desirable partner for Significant Tech, states Kate Winick, an analyst with Forrester Research.

It has been revealed that “Warby Parker is a small player, but it’s the right player,” says Winick. “It has stores in these zip codes that tech companies desperately want to reach.’.

In a recent development, that’s especially vital for Google, which has a lot riding on these glasses. The company needs Warby Parker, Winick indicates, to help it move past Google Glass, its first attempt at smart glasses, which flopped after its 2013 debut. Glass was bulky, its battery died quickly, and at $1,500 it was hard to justify. Worse, it made people uneasy: many bars, restaurants, and theaters banned it over fears of non-consensual recording. It came to embody the era’s “tech bro” stereotype so completely that its wearers earned a nickname: “glassholes.”.

Google is eager for its new version of smart glasses to prove that its technology can work at scale across other wearables. Forrester’s Winick expects tech giants to use the wearable tech featured in glasses in other devices, such as Meta’s Muse Charm, a pocket- and keychain-sized AI companion.

Recent findings suggest that at Warby Parker’s recent quarterly check-in with Wall Street analysts, where smart glasses dominated the discussion, the tech giant said smart glasses could contribute several percentage points to its fourth-quarter growth. (Google and Warby Parker have not disclosed the revenue sharing and intellectual property ownership details of their partnership.).

In a recent development, blumenthal and Gilboa acknowledge the privacy concerns smart glasses raise, but they argue the devices could also fix a modern habit: constantly reaching for our phones. “For the first time, you can have this accessory that enables you to leave your phone in your pocket to keep your attention in the real world,” Gilboa states.
