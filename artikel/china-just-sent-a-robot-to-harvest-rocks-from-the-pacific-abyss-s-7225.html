Sources indicate that somewhere in the eastern Pacific, between Hawaii and Mexico, a Chinese robot recently crawled across the ocean floor in total darkness. The water around it was near freezing, and the pressure was immense. Over long stretches, it picked up loose rocks from the seabed one by one.

The robot is called Kunpeng, after a legendary giant fish that turns into a bird. It was built by the state-owned China Minmetals and used in what the South China Morning Post, citing Chinese state media, describes as China’s first large-scale deep-sea mining trial in international waters. Industry observers note that what China was after looks unremarkable: lumpy, potato-sized rocks. But new research suggests these rocks hold something besides metal. Thousands of somethings, in fact. (Related: Industry News: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Fa...)

They’re called polymetallic nodules, and they’re at the center of a deep-sea controversy that’s been building for years.

The problem has two parts. First, the nodules contain metals that modern technology depends on, including manganese, nickel, cobalt, copper, zinc, and iron. These metals go into everything from smartphones to electric vehicle batteries. Second, most of the nodules sit in the middle of the Pacific in a region called the Clarion–Clipperton Zone (CCZ), roughly 4,000 to 6,000 meters (about 2.5 to 3.7 miles) below the surface. Life in estuaries and tidal zones near the coast is used to constant change, from daily temperature swings to human interference. The deep ocean is far more fragile, however, so mining there could do far more damage.

Each nodule starts as a bit of ancient debris on the seafloor. Dissolved metals slowly build up around it in layers, adding only a few millimeters of rock every million years. One scientist told Popular Mechanics in 2024 that the process is “like a super slow game of Tetris.”.

According to recent reports, but as you might have guessed, there’s a catch. A latest study in the journal Scientific Reports found that the nodules aren’t just part of a delicate ecosystem. Each one is itself an ecosystem, home to dozens or even hundreds of tiny animals.

It has been revealed that the researchers analyzed 244 nodules and found more than 4,000 individual animals on their surfaces alone. The average nodule hosted 17 animals, but some held as many as 245 lifeforms, from single-celled species to nematodes and small crustaceans. The secret is the nodules’ porous structure: their crevices make ideal shelters.

“The crevices in the polymetallic nodules function as small shelters and provide home to animals that we do not encounter in the soft sediment of the seafloor,” mentioned Coral Diaz-Recio Lorenzo, a co-lead author of the study from the Royal Netherlands Institute for Sea Research (NIOZ), in a press statement.

The surrounding mud held more life overall, but the nodules hosted their own distinct communities. Those communities looked alike from one nodule to the next and different from the ones in the mud. Some of the residents seem to live nowhere else. One species, a single-celled organism called Thurammina sp. 1, appears perfectly adapted to life on the nodules. If the nodules are mined, it could disappear along with an ecosystem that took millions of years to build.

It has been revealed that “Our findings point out that commercial mining may result in an irreversible loss of undescribed and unique biodiversity associated to nodules,” mentioned Sabine Gollner, the study’s senior author, who is also from NIOZ. “This needs to be considered in environmental management of deep-sea mining.”.

In other words, the rocks China’s robot was gathering may be home to species that science hasn’t even named yet.

Kunpeng is built to survive in one of the harshest places on Earth. Researchers at Harbin Engineering University gave it a sound-based navigation system that can find its position to within a single meter, even in total darkness.

According to recent reports, to reach those extreme depths, however, the robot relied on specialized deployment gear. China lowered its submersibles 5,330 meters (17,500 feet) using the Haiwei GD11000, a deep-sea winch that Interesting Engineering notes is the only one in the world capable of reaching the ocean’s deepest point. While conventional research vessels use heavy, steel-wrapped cables that risk snapping under their own weight at extreme depths, the Haiwei system uses a 13-kilometer (8-mile) line armored with lightweight synthetic fiber. Over the course of the expedition, the winch completed six deployments—including a single 48-hour dive—logging nearly 200 hours underwater.

Recent findings suggest that the trial took place inside a 72,745-square-kilometer (28,000-square-mile) exploration block—an area larger than West Virginia. China Minmetals has studied the area since 2017 under a 15-year contract with the UN-backed International Seabed Authority (ISA). Beyond that, details are scarce.

According to recent reports, the stakes go well beyond science. China already dominates global refining of cobalt, nickel, and manganese. Mining the CCZ would let Beijing secure raw materials from international waters and protect its electric vehicle and battery industries from Western trade restrictions.

Industry observers note that and China isn’t the only country with its eye on these waters. Washington is moving to license commercial mining in the same part of the Pacific. While China works within the ISA system, the U.S. is using its own domestic laws, and it’s expected to rule by mid-December on a permit application from the U.S. subsidiary of The Metals Company, a Canadian ocean-mining firm.

It has been revealed that that name may sound familiar. The Metals Organization helped fund the 2024 research by scientists from the Scottish Association for Marine Science and Northwestern University that found possible evidence the nodules produce “dark oxygen,” a rare kind of oxygen production that doesn’t need sunlight. The company and other scientists have disputed those findings. The original team stands by its work and is now back in the CCZ studying the phenomenon.

No country has started full-scale commercial deep-sea mining yet. But China’s Haiwei winch is already set to join routine survey voyages, and a U.S. decision is weeks away. Meanwhile, scientists are warning that heavy machinery scraping the abyssal plain could permanently destroy ecosystems we’ve barely begun to study.

Sources indicate that dark oxygen or not, it’s increasingly clear that these nodules belong to an ecosystem far older than the first hominins, let alone Homo sapiens. Two superpowers are now racing to dig it up. Whether the creatures living inside the rocks will be named before they’re gone remains to be seen.
