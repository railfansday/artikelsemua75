Te Araroa day 23: 500km in, have I found my trail legs yet?

21km, Pakiri Paradise to Dome Cafe.

It has been revealed that most of the others left by 7am, but I was a bit slower and wasn’t ready until 7:15. But not to worry, as it’s a running joke that no matter how much later I leave, I’ll catch everyone up soon enough! I could see Annabel up ahead as I left, but the track went into some bushes and I didn’t see her anymore. The mud began pretty much straight out the gate, and at first I was able to avoid it reasonably easily. But it became apparent that I wouldn’t be able to go around it for much longer, so 15 minutes into the day I committed to the mud and put my feet straight in. The first steps are always the worst, and once your feet are already wet and muddy it doesn’t seem so bad anymore. (Related: Deploy Hugging Face models easily with Amazon SageMaker...)

After a bit of up and down through cow fields, the track went into the bush and I caught up to Lindsay. This was interesting, as I hadn’t passed Annabel, and Annabel hadn’t passed Lindsay either. I figured I must have gone past without realising – maybe she was peeing in a bush or had gone the other way around a tree. Not too long after, there was a steep scramble up with some chains – the first (but not last) chains of TA! I don’t think I used them, but I always enjoy those steep scrambly bits. They use different muscles to the regular walking uphill ones, and it’s a mental puzzle as well as a physical challenge. (Related: Breaking: How to generate text: using different decodin...)

According to recent reports, at the top of the hill, I found Sinead and Xavier by a helipad. We had read about this helipad in the trail notes, and realised that it must be the same one that someone had posted in the TA Facebook group about using as a campsite one night. Unsurprisingly, that post got a number of comments telling him (more or less politely) that camping on a helipad is a pretty stupid thing to do! Fortunately we were not in need of a camping spot, and continued on. The track continued to go up and down, more or less steeply, more or less muddily, for a while.

What a great camp spot that would be.

Well good, because I sure was wearing some.

Sources indicate that eventually I started to hear traffic and surmised I must be getting close to the road. Shortly before the road, there was a steep, wet downhill section that was pretty boggy; and then, hilariously, a wooden bridge over a tiny stream at the bottom. This stream was barely a trickle, while the mud that came before it was at least ankle deep in places. What was the point of the bridge?! The random and pointless boardwalks continued for the last couple of hundred metres to the road. I appreciate the efforts to improve the track, but it really is futile to put a couple of metres of boardwalk in when they are preceded by several kilometres of mud.

At the road, I took a moment to enjoy my favourite hiker trash snack, mayonnaise on crackers. At this point Xavier appeared, and looked disturbed by my choice of topping. ‘Is that… just sauce?!’ My defence: plain crackers are boring, and I’m carrying the mayo for lunch anyway, so why not add more flavour and calories to my snack?

There was a short section of walking along the main road before I turned off onto a smaller one. On the map it was marked as a gravel road, but this was most definitely surfaced. Oh well. There were some decent views in places, and there was a house offering free filtered water. After not too long, I reached the next bit of track. I felt hopeful when I saw that it had steps, and gravel – maybe the next bit wouldn’tbe so muddy? The steps led up to a kauri dieback cleaning station, where I made a valiant attempt to get the mud off my shoes. It wasn’t very successful though – to make a real difference I would have needed running water. Then the track continued uphill on a gravel road/driveway for a bit. It was only 11am, but when I saw a nice grassy patch by the road I decided to stop for lunch. I wasn’t sure where the next flattish and dryish bit of ground would be in the upcoming muddy forest. I was able to have a nice lunch and dry my tent out, but it was getting pretty warm and by the time I got going again I was feeling full, sleepy and unmotivated.

The track went upwards for a bit more before turning off. I knew that this was where the 500km point was, and after missing the 300 and 400km markers I wanted to make sure I did something for this one. There wasn’t a suitable surface to draw on with my pole, so I gathered some small rocks from the road and wrote 500 with them. I took a couple of photos and headed back into the forest. The mud didn’t seem too bad at first, but became similar to the last bit of track. Up, down, up, down, mud, mud, more mud. Some of the downs were extraordinarily steep, too. Earlier in the day I had been able to enjoy the ridiculousness of slogging through mud for fun, but now my enthusiasm was wearing out. Most of the others had passed me when I was having lunch, but at the bottom of a particularly heinous descent I caught Sinead, who was standing in a stream. ‘We’re really close to the campsite now!’ she mentioned. The campsite she referred to was not where we planned to end the day, but a small one on the way at the end of this section of forest. Still, I was happy to hear we were close, and grabbed some water to filter when I got there. I knew there was a stream crossing just at the campsite, so I believed that Sinead was right and it was just around the corner. However after a bit more walking I checked my map and realised it wasn’t the stream crossing I was thinking of, and I was still about a kilometre away.

Eventually, we did arrive at the campsite. I could see Zoe sitting there with Xavier and one or two other people. Zoe set out early that morning as she was racing to meet some friends who were picking her up at the end of the day, and she must have been on a mission as I hadn’t seen her since the morning! She reckoned she got her trail legs all of a sudden – I would like to know if mine are on the way too, because this section was feeling hard!

Recent findings suggest that also there was Tim, who at first I didn’t recognise, but who I had met at the Ruakaka campsite on the day I arrived. He had been staying with friends and slack packing for a few days. In fact, we had followed him down the beach the previous day – I had seen the hiking pole marks in the sand and the figure in the distance, but not been able to work out who it was.

Recent findings suggest that lindsay and Anica soon showed up too, but there was no sign of Annabel. We were a bit worried about her, but there wasn’t much to do. At this point, it was about 2:30pm and we still had 7km to do to our intended stop at the Dome cafe. But the temptation to just stay at this perfectly pleasant spot was very strong. I knew I would be annoyed at myself if I stayed, so I washed my shoes and socks in the stream, reapplied my tape, had a snack, and got ready to continue. Someone had remarked that track conditions improved from this point, and I sure hoped that was the case otherwise it would be a rather late arrival into camp.

Finally some nice, dry track where I can actually appreciate my surroundings.

My first view of Rangitoto Island in the Auckland harbour.

Signs for improved track conditions sure seemed to be good. Across the road, the track went steeply up on a gravel forestry road. It was steep and hot, but at least not muddy. The gradient continued as the road turned into a track, but it was nowhere near as muddy as the previous sections. I was able to make good time, and when the track took a turn into some native bush, I was able to enjoy it. It was a nice bush track, although still of course rather hilly.

Sources indicate that A few kilometres from the end, I reached a trig point. Foolishly, I believed that it would be mostly downhill from here. Boy, was I wrong. There were still at least two substantial hills that just felt never ending. I caught up to Zoe at one point, and finally I came up to a viewpoint where there was a group of day hikers admiring the view, and Tim. I apologised for my smell and asked if it was far to the bottom. It had taken them 20 minutes to get up, which was music to my ears. The track from this point on was all gravelled, with steps. There was one small uphill, which felt massive to my tired legs. And finally, I was at the bottom. Zoe’s boyfriend was there, and introduced himself to me, but I was too tired to make intelligent conversation.

The track just after the trig was cool, especially when it was going downhill.

There is space for hikers to pitch tents kindly provided by the old Dome cafe, which is being turned into a house. They provide a small tent area, a sheltered kitchen area, and a hot shower! In a bath tub!! Unfortunately there was no curtain or anything around the shower, and it was a glass door out to the deck. So we had to take it in turns and make sure no one was there before heading round. In the past, I probably wouldn’t have been too keen on this arrangement, but I was so tired and desperate for a shower that I didn’t care. Then I was able to sit in a comfy chair and eat the mammoth dinner I had prepared for myself.

Annabel ended up appearing not too long after me, with Lindsay. It turned out she had got lost very early on when the track turned over a fence and she had continued straight on. She ended up not getting further than 1km on the track by 9:30, so it was very impressive that she had managed to catch up! We all agreed that this was one of the toughest days yet. The mud and the relentless up and down were physically challenging and a bit soul-crushing. Anica had actually gotten injured, and she planned to hitch the next day instead of walking.

Sources indicate that the floor lit up in the bathroom. Not quite to my taste, but it’s definitely something!

In a recent development, lindsay and I eat a well-deserved dinner. Photo by Sinéad.

The Dome cafe is just off SH1 north of Auckland, and I remember passing the DOC sign on the way up to Kaitaia. We could hear the road noise from where we were camped, and it was strange after a tough and remote-feeling day to be back so close to civilisation. I thought I would fall asleep promptly, but unfortunately the ground was not the flattest and I had managed to pitch my tent such that my head was downhill no matter which way I faced. In the end I shoved my rain jacket under my mat by my head, and was able to sleep.
