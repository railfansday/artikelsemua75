Before Raj Shamani sat in a room with some of the most influential people in the world, he had to figure out how to stop his bedroom from echoing.

Recent findings suggest that the aspiring podcaster didn’t have a studio or connections to the successful people he wanted to meet. So, he sat in his bedroom in Indore, India, pulled a blanket over his head, and pressed record—figuring the best way to learn how to build a successful business was to ask the people who already had. “I didn’t have capital. I didn’t have connections. I didn’t have the access to people who could teach me,” Shamani told Fortune. “Initially it was selfish, I just wanted to learn.”. (Related: Towers of Fortune codes (November 2026)...)

Shamani got his first taste of entrepreneurship at 16, when he began helping with his father’s chemical-trading business after a health scare. He learned to make dishwashing liquid by watching YouTube videos, then started selling it door to door in his hometown of Indore. He eventually developed his own cleaning product, Jadugar Drop, before turning to public speaking and content creation.

Recent findings suggest that he didn’t get podcasting right on his first try. Or his second. One show lasted only a few days; another fizzled out because he couldn’t find guests. But none of that seemed to shake his confidence. In December 2020, he debuted Figuring Out.

Shamani called himself “extremely delusional,” believing he can succeed despite knowing he’s not the most talented person in the room, doesn’t have the best connections, and doesn’t have the deepest pockets.

According to recent reports, “I don’t have all of this,” he remarked. “But what I have is I can outwork everybody, and that selfawareness helped me.”.

It has been revealed that his team now records about 200 episodes annually, but publishes only around 150, scrapping the dozens that don’t make the cut. While other podcasters compete to land the biggest names, Shamani notes his team approaches each conversation like a product, built around a problem its audience wants solved.

“We weren’t competing for the biggest guest, we were competing for the most valuable question,” he remarked.

Another finding was less flattering for the man behind the microphone and camera. When Shamani spoke more than his guest during the first seven minutes, viewers were more likely to leave. So he began opening with a short question and letting his guests take over.

It has been revealed that the firm has also turned to AI to help take its podcast beyond India. Using multilingual audio tracks and dubbing tools, the team distributes conversations in Hindi, English, and Spanish, then creates clips tailored to different countries, regions, and even American states. Shamani said the share of his audience outside India has soared from roughly 3% to 22%, with a surprise jump in Australia.

That same technology helps decide what gets recorded in the first place. Before Shamani sits down with a guest, his team uses AI to study audience interests, select topics, vet potential interviewees, and prepare research briefs running 30 to 40 pages, along with roughly 100 suggested questions.

Recent findings suggest that but once the camera starts rolling, Shamani leaves the calculations behind. “I need to be the most human version of myself,” he remarked. “After and before, it’s all science.”.

Some of Shamani’s biggest business decisions have come from the people sitting across from him. After interviewing the founders of Indian consumer goods brand Mamaearth about the importance of “founder energy,” he had his first three employees live with him for a year. Those early hires now lead major departments at his firm.

Olympic swimmer Michael Phelps, meanwhile, taught him to visualize success even when things go wrong, a practice Shamani now uses before interviews.

Figuring Out Media has since expanded into educational courses, investments, and a social media distribution business called Relevance Media. A consumer goods division is also in the works, part of Shamani’s ambition to build a company that can survive long after he stops appearing in front of the camera.

“There’s a creator and a manufacturer around him, and then there’s a company which happens to have a creator,” he stated. “I think I’m building the latter one.”.
