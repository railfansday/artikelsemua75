4 ways I gave my employees more time off without sacrificing results

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A decade ago, I couldn’t have told you the last time I took significant time off from work. I wore “busy” like a badge of honor. As the business owner, I set the tone for the people on my team. Pushing ourselves to do more and work harder was woven into the fabric of our culture. Today, things could not look more different. Every summer, I take a monthlong vacation to travel with my family. When I work late nights or several days in a row without breaks, I make it up to myself with extra time off. My team gets a four-day workweek and generous PTO, and on top of that, we close our business for six weeks throughout the year and take time off together. And we’ve managed to make this shift without losing sight of high expectations and accountability. What changed? I did. After years of neglecting my health in service of my work, I came across two books that upended my perspective: Dying for a Paycheck by Jeffrey Pfeffer, and It Doesn’t Have to be Crazy at Work by David Heinemeier Hansson and Jason Fried. They revealed me what my firm’s culture could look like if I took responsibility for changing it. For years now, creating better work-life harmony for me and my team has been my priority. To me, “harmony” means that work and life work together instead of against each other. But of course, it hasn’t been easy. My biggest challenge has been figuring out how to balance caring for my people with creating a sustainable future for my company. I certainly haven’t found all the answers, but several years into this journey, my team and I are able to prioritize our lives outside of work and get stuff done—including writing and launching a newly introduced book, just this year. While it’s true that my company and team are significantly smaller than they were in the early days, I believe it’s possible for teams of any size to make shifts that support better work-life harmony. I want to share the biggest things we’ve implemented with the hope that they will spark some ideas of your own. 1. Break weeks Years ago, I took off two separate weeks during a busy time at work. I didn’t have a vacation planned; I just needed a break. When I returned after a week of doing whatever I felt like at home, I felt rejuvenated and inspired. I wanted that same feeling for my team, so I proposed a company-wide “Break Week.” I told the team we would close the business and use the time to recharge. We loved it. Being off at the same time allowed us to disconnect in a way we never had. No one felt like they were falling behind, and we returned with renewed energy. Now, we enjoy six Break Weeks throughout the year: one week at the end of the first three quarters and three at the end of the year. I see them as a way to guarantee that each person on my team—including me—is taking off at least that much time every year. I’ve realized that offering a benefit doesn’t guarantee that people will use it, so implementing a policy like Break Weeks shows my team that I’m serious about time off. You might wonder what our clients think about our closing our business so often, and at first, I worried about that too. I also worried about missing out on revenue. Not only have our clients embraced Break Weeks, but some have even implemented their own version of them. And when we have to turn away revenue, we work to make it up in other quarters. If we can’t, we see it as a reasonable trade-off. 2. Four-day workweeks  Break Weeks were a huge step toward making sure that my team prioritizes their lives outside of work, but for me, it was just the start. When I first proposed the four-day workweek, I knew I’d find it tough to commit to. But as the leader, I needed to model it. I told my team that if I ever had to work all five days, I’d make up for it another time, and I did. Today, I empower each person on my team to choose the day that works best for them to take off each week, and they can change it as needed. To make this work, team communication is critical. We maintain a time-off calendar and share frequent updates on deliverables and deadlines. Once again, I learned that it’s a leader’s job to create a structure that holds everyone accountable to using benefits; otherwise, it just doesn’t happen. 3. Human time off For years, I proudly touted our “unlimited” paid time-off policy. But, of course, there’s only so much time you can take off and expect to remain on payroll. To create better-defined guidelines, I decided to make a distinction between two types of time off: “human” time and vacation time. Human time covers doctor or personal appointments, sick time, mental health days, etc. My team knows that they can take time off for these things without my approval. If human time affects someone’s ability to get their work done, I expect them to communicate that with the team and create a initiative. Vacation time, on the other hand, means that you are completely unplugged and unavailable. I ask that my team runs their vacation intends by me before they finalize them so that I can ensure that we’re still meeting the needs of the business. Since I implemented this distinction, I’ve noticed that not only is there more clarity and communication about time off, but the team is taking more of it and working together more proactively. 4. Sabbaticals  This year, I implemented sabbaticals as a way to honor the amount of time my team has chosen to give me and the company. Depending on the person’s tenure, these sabbaticals range from four to six weeks and are fully paid. Each person earns a sabbatical every five years. Because those on my team have been with me for 9, 14, and 15 years, respectively, we are in the process of figuring out the sabbatical schedule so that the needs of the business are still covered. I’m excited to see what we learn and the impact of this new benefit. None of this works without accountability and open communication. We evaluate ourselves and each other’s performance with 360-degree reviews every quarter. We strive to give feedback within 24 hours of an problem’s arising, and we don’t shy away from difficult conversations. If someone isn’t delivering on their results and their time off is affecting their performance, it’s my job to speak up. Since implementing these changes, I’ve noticed my team and I are approaching our work with more energy and enthusiasm. In busy seasons, we’re willing to sprint because we feel taken care of. While some benefits look different for me as the business owner, I’m still taking off more time than ever before. These benefits might sound expensive or over-the-top, but I believe nothing has greater ROI than building a culture where work-life harmony thrives.
