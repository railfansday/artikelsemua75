'Critical' 1970s-era Fujitsu UK police system could remain unsupported for three years

We first asked about the support contract several days prior to the publication of this article. The Register has filed a Freedom of Information request to compel the government to clarify the matter.*The UK’s Home Office has been unable to provide evidence to show how the Police National Computer (PNC) is getting vendor support for three years or more after it missed another deadline to decommission the aging crime-fighting system first introduced in 1974. The PNC was first launched in 1974, when Tom Baker began his iconic role playing Doctor Who, and has seen the tenure of 26 UK Home Secretaries, surviving multiple efforts to replace it. (Related: Show, Don't Certify: How to Prepare for Your First Data...)

Industry observers note that A notice in December 2024 said that the Home Office was "focused on the development and adoption of the Law Enforcement Data Service (LEDS) products to replace existing PNC capability across all police forces and other non-policing PNC consumers, enabling the retirement by March 2026 (the point at which PNC support will expire)." We understand that PNC support refers to the Fujitsu support contract for the mainframe, which is housed in north London. Support for the Software AG database on the mainframe OS (BS2000) ran out at the end of 2024. So the database holding the crucial police data remains out of standard support and potentially will stay that way until 2027.The decommissioning date for the PNC had already been pushed back by the need to delay and "reset" the LEDS in 2020. The Home Office launched its successor, National Law Enforcement Data Service, in 2016, and planned to finish the new system by 2020 at a cost of £671 million.

The Home Office told The Register this week that it needed more time, and it aimed to begin decommissioning the system from March 2027, raising questions over how it would be supported.

A Home Office spokesperson mentioned: "We are modernizing policing systems to adapt to evolving challenges, including rolling out the LEDS to replace the Police National Computer.

"While LEDS is already operational, we are continuing to run the legacy Police National Computer system to support the operational needs of all UK police," they said.

Officials said the PNC would remain available throughout the transition and would only be disconnected when police forces have confirmed that they no longer need it.

According to recent reports, earlier this month, in a written response in the House of Lords, Lord Hanson of Flint, a junior minister, noted the PNC might be required until early 2028. Some LEDS services were already live and the program was "targeting completion of the remaining development, adoption and readiness activity in early 2027 as we prepare for our final stages for LEDS deployment before retiring the PNC," he said.

Sources indicate that "Once the transition of all police customers to the LEDS has been completed and policing has confirmed that the PNC is no longer required, the system will be disconnected and decommissioned. The decommissioning process is expected to take place between late 2027 and early 2028," he remarked.

Recent findings suggest that according to the National Audit Office (NAO), the PNC is the most important national policing information system in the UK. It serves front-line officers from all 45 local police forces, as well as by 127 other organisations that need to access its data. In 2019-20, the police searched or updated the PNC 133 million times.

The ongoing delay to the decommissioning of the PNC raises questions about how — and whether — it is being adequately supported.

The PNC is sited at a datacenter in Hendon, North London, and is based on a Fujitsu BS2000/OSD SE700-30 mainframe, but this is technology originally developed by German engineering outfit Siemens inherited by Fujitsu when it acquired the data processing division of that organization.

It was the subject of controversy in 2021 when an error led to the accidental loss of 413,000 records of police evidence from the system, the result of a scripting error in code designed to weed out records targeted for deletion, although the lost data was eventually recovered.

According to recent reports, in 2022, Fujitsu — the Japanese tech giant at the center of the Post Office Horizon scandal — was awarded a £48 million ($56.6 million) renewed contract for support and maintenance of the PNC after no other companies tendered for the work. The contract was set to expire in March 2026.

In its 2021 report, NAO stated the Fujitsu mainframe computer used by the PNC would reach the end of its supported life by 31 December 2021.

Sources indicate that "The Software AG database used by the PNC is only supported on the Fujitsu mainframe until 31 December 2023. The Department has an option to extend the contract for this support for a further 12 months but it has not yet agreed this with the supplier. If [the replacement system] is not ready, the PNC will need to be moved off the mainframe onto a supported operating system from 2025 or run unsupported," the report noted.

According to recent reports, however, at the time, the Home Office told the NAO it had decided to accept the risk of running the PNC without support for the database for 12 months after December 2024. "This would leave the PNC at higher risk of disruption for at least a year, although the Department's view is that the actual risk profile is low because of the mitigation actions put in place," the report said [PDF].

It added that moving the PNC to a newly introduced operating system could take three years and Home Office estimated that it will cost at least £30 million, including the cost of rewriting code for the new OS.

A report from the Public Accounts Committee later in November 2021 said: "The team that maintains the PNC is under-resourced and several staff are nearing retirement… The Department presently intends to run this piece of critical national infrastructure without full manufacturer support for the database after 2024, meaning it will not have that support for at least a year, though the Department plans to provide in-house support instead.".

Unless the Home Office has come up with another plan in the meantime, it is set to extend that out-of-support period to around three years. It has been offered the opportunity to provide additional comment. ®.
