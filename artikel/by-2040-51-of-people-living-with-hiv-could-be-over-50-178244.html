By 2040, 51% of People Living with HIV Could Be Over 50

Advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have helped people living with HIV live longer, contributing to a growing population of older adults with the virus.

According to recent reports, in fact, a recent report published in The Lancet HIV projects that approximately 51% of people living with HIV worldwide will be over age 50 by 2040. The report’s authors emphasized the “pressing need” for HIV prevention and screening services tailored to older adults, who face additional age-related challenges when accessing care.

In a recent development, many adults over age 50 who are living with HIV acquired the virus during young adulthood in the late 1990s and early 2000s. During that period, some experienced prolonged periods of uncontrolled HIV and exposure to older ART medications with more severe side effects, which may have contributed to long-term inflammation and other health complications.

William Schaffner, MD, professor in the Division of Infectious Diseases at Vanderbilt University, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline, “That older persons with HIV are on track to outnumber younger persons with HIV infection is a double-barreled success story.”.

Industry observers note that “First, the effective treatment of persons with HIV infection has resulted in the expectation of normal or near-normal lifespans; thus, more older persons living with HIV infection,” he remarked. “This is a great success; both in creating effective, easy-to-use medicines as well as developing medical care systems that can reach such patients and provide lifetime care.”.

Schaffner said the second success has been educating younger people about HIV prevention, helping reduce new infections.

Older adults also account for a growing proportion of new HIV diagnoses. They are more likely to receive a diagnosis later in the course of infection, which can delay treatment and increase the risk of serious health complications and death.

Mathematical models project that the average age of people living with HIV worldwide will increase from 41 in 2025 to 50 by 2040, largely driven by demographic shifts in low- and middle-income countries.

Sources indicate that while the World Health Organization (WHO) does not specify an upper age limit for HIV testing in its published overview, U.S. guidelines focus on specific age groups.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends routine HIV testing for people ages 13 to 64, and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends testing for people ages 15 to 65.

However, these age limits don’t mean older adults shouldn’t be tested for HIV, as both U.S. organizations also recommend testing older adults who may be at risk.

According to recent reports, older adults can still be sexually active and have a chance of acquiring HIV. Certain physical changes that come with age, such as thinning of vaginal tissue, may also increase the risk of HIV infection.

Sources indicate that despite these risks, older adults are often overlooked in HIV prevention campaigns, research, and clinical trials. The report’s authors say more needs to be done to address these gaps.

“Targeted and tailored HIV prevention and screening services for older adults are a pressing global need,” the report authors stated.

Schaffner agreed with this and added, “Increasing the effectiveness of diagnosis and treatment of HIV infection in older adults will increase their expectation of a healthier, longer life.”.
