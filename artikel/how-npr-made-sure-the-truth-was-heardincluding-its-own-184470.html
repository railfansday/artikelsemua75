How NPR made sure the truth was heard—including its own

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, “If you’re not sharing with the public what your brand is all about, the public will make up a story for you,” claims Kristin Hume, VP of marketing at National Public Radio (NPR). “We saw that happen, and it had terrible consequences.” The story that had congealed around NPR—at least for President Trump—was that it was a mouthpiece for progressives, heavily funded by the government. That narrative led to an executive order resulting in Congress shutting down NPR’s main source of federal funding last fall. Federal funding actually only accounted for 1% to 2% of the budget, but NPR immediately showed that it was not going anywhere. On its first day without government funding, it issued a message across its social channels and those of nearly 250 affiliate stations: “We won’t be silenced.” The blunt, spare dispatch appealed for help, and money poured in, leading to a record number of donations in one day. In March, NPR followed up with an initiative called “For Your Right to Be Curious.” It changed the letters in its logo on billboards and social media to spell “who,” “how,” and “why,” emphasizing its ironclad promise to serve inquisitive listeners. By highlighting the right to be curious, though, NPR reframed its identity while subtly hinting at what’s at stake when a government becomes hostile to a free press. Physically altering the signage outside its D.C. headquarters to display WHY and HOW and putting its manifesto in a full-page ad in The New York Times generated a wave of positive coverage. Donations have continued—including $80 million from billionaire philanthropist Connie Ballmer and $33 million from an anonymous donor. NPR says Harris Poll figures show familiarity with the NPR brand in June 2026 up by nearly 10% from its average over the previous six months.
