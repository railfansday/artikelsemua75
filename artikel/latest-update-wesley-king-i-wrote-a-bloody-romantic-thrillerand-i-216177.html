I grew up as a nerd. Well. I also grew into a nerd. That is to say, I loved all things fantasy and science fiction, literary passions which later grew to incorporate historical fiction, murder mysteries, and heists. The Lies of Lock Lamora by Scott Lynch is a perfect example: We have magic, we have crime, this is fun.

In a recent development, but when romance came knocking? I needed some guidance to get started…and so I joined a reading group. I was the only man in the group, and I was pre-emptively warned that they had a predilection for smut. But, hey, I could read smut. Why not? It wasn’t just romantasy either. We ran the gauntlet. And you know what? There was some good stuff in there.

It occurred to me that I might just like to write something romantic.

It has been revealed that the first question I asked myself was: Is that genre mash-up even a thing? Do thriller or crime readers want to read romance? Is an explicit sex scene going to force them to cast the book into the fire from whence it came? I didn’t know. The greatest murder mystery of all time for my money, The Murder of Roger Akroyd by Agatha Christie, didn’t have time for such frivolity.

I knew for starters that my characters and their connection needed to feel real. That was one thing my reading group taught me: We can’t see our sexy heroes. We need an emotional runway. We need our hearts to send us a confusing memo: Wait. I could love that person.

So I wrote backstories. I wrote a mystery that was violent and complex and provided the twists and turns we demand in thrillers. I placed the story in smalltown Montana with massive skies and big characters: ranchers, cops, and a mysterious exile named the Hill Man. There is plenty of atmospheric horror, symbology and scares, and the ever-hanging question of whether something paranormal hangs over the mounting disappearances. I like a little Mulder in my mysteries.

And I wrote two characters entwined in a long-doomed-romance, forever caught in each other’s orbits, and explored the way they could find their way back to each other in the pursuit of the truth. But I also leaned into it. This part wasn’t Mulder and Scully. I wanted it to feel like romance. True love and longing and lust in the midst of the carnage.

So what were my lessons learned? How did I merge the genres?

Here’s five things I did or learned through the process:.

According to recent reports, 1. I read a shitload of genre romance.

Sources indicate that 2. I didn’t sacrifice the fundamental aspects of crime thrillers and murder-mysteries. I built a love story in and around them.

Industry observers note that 3. I laid off the sap. Crime and thriller readers aren’t here for I love you’s. But we do want authentic characters and dialogue, and we do have hearts buried somewhere beneath our hard-earned cynicism. (Related: Industry News: Train a Sentence Embedding Model with 1B...)

4. The love story contributed to the plot. Yes, they have sex. But there’s a reason. And if it’s the first time you’ve read anything remotely explicit, you can say: All right, fine, not my cup of tea, but I know why we went there.

5. Match your setting and story. Wolf Season isn’t YA. It’s also not romantasy—it’s a rough, rural crime thriller. I wanted the romance to match the setting. It’s less star-crossed lovers and more hard-boiled, cussing, and raw attraction. No one is reciting poetry. There are insults flying, guns pointed, very real danger…and two people who just can’t stay apart. (Related: Simple considerations for simple people building fancy ...)

According to recent reports, so there you have it. I wrote a romantic thriller, and I like it. I wasn’t able to think of a clever name for the genre, so in honor of Agatha Christie, I just went across the pond with the Bloody Romantic Thriller. Feel free to use the accent.

I’ll be looking up more crossovers now, and if you’re writing a thriller or murder mystery, don’t be afraid to add a little good old-fashioned romance. Go full-on smut, if it strikes your fancy. Either way, I hope you enjoy a little romance with your murder mystery. And if not, don’t worry, there is a whole lot of shit going down in Wolf Season to buttress that hard-earned cynicism.
