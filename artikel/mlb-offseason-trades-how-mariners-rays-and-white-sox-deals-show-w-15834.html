MLB offseason trades: How Mariners, Rays and White Sox deals show why they come out of nowhere

When it comes to the MLB offseason, the two primary types of transactions — free-agent signings and trades — transpire quite differently.

Free agency tends to dominate the headlines, and understandably so. The top players are no secret, as their arrival on the open market has often already been discussed for months if not years. Once the offseason arrives, the courting process for the best available players tends to follow a similar script. It quickly becomes clear which teams are the best landing spots depending on spending power and positional fit, meetings are had, negotiations begin, and eventually, a deal is reached. According to recent reports, some free agencies drag on longer than others. Mystery teams occasionally emerge from the shadows to strike major and shock fans and industry insiders alike. Certain contracts raise eyebrows. But for the most part, free agency tends to be a logical game of matchmaker, one that takes only time to resolve. (Related: Industry News: Judge blocks construction of Trump’s arc...)

Trades, meanwhile, are a completely different beast.

Sure, insiders report on certain players being floated as trade candidates, or sometimes team officials acknowledge outright that a player is being discussed in deals. Fans, from the loosely informed casuals to the plugged-in diehards, circulate their fanciful imagined trade proposals on social media. Analysts attempt to construct more sensible deals based on team needs and complex formulas involving surplus value. But because of the nature of the baseball business — with contract sizes and lengths varying wildly in the majors, and an expansive universe of players looming in the minors — it is practically impossible to accurately forecast the majority of trades.

Sources indicate that consider the first two significant trades of this offseason completed earlier this week. After finalizing the six-player swap headlined by All-Star third baseman Maikel Garcia, Mariners president of baseball operations Jerry Dipoto mentioned that Seattle was focused on other infield possibilities and had even come close to making a different deal before Garcia came into focus as a target. On the other side, Royals front-office head J.J. Picollo wasn't sure that someone like Bryce Miller would be attainable in their wide-ranging search for pitching. But after enough conversations at the GM Meetings last week in Las Vegas, the possibility crystallized as legitimate.

The Royals' trade of third baseman Maikel Garcia was a surprise for many in MLB.

Sources indicate that suddenly, after years of Seattle contemplating trading from its enviable homegrown starting pitching depth, it was go time. Off to Kansas City went three major-league pitchers, and Garcia — who appeared to be a long-term fixture in the Royals' infield — was a Mariner. Just like that.

Then came Thursday's deal between the Chicago White Sox and Colorado Rockies. Earlier in the day, general manager Chris Getz sat on the podium in Chicago alongside latest free-agent signing Dustin May and fielded questions about whether the White Sox were pursuing more starting pitching. About seven hours later, Getz made a dramatic subtraction from the pitching staff, sending veteran Luis Castillo and recent rotation mainstay Davis Martin to the Rockies, who have probably been having a tough time convincing free-agent pitchers to come to Coors Field. The White Sox landed a pair of prospects in return — left-handed pitcher Sean Sullivan, who debuted in June, and 17-year-old catcher Douglas Veliz — but this deal was money-motivated to offload Castillo's onerous salary (one year left for $22.75 million), perhaps to free up some payroll flexibility to pursue more pitching in free agency. Whatever the intention on either side, it came out of nowhere. For all the time White Sox or Rockies fans may have spent formulating their ideal offseason prepares, conjuring up this specific trade would be laughable. Yet there it was, a done deal.

Sources indicate that for fans, trades can be unexpected jolts of transactional chaos and curiosity, mystery box moves featuring combinations of players we almost never foresee. They are typically concentrated in the weeks leading up to the midseason deadline or at random intervals throughout the winter, but sometimes our phones buzz on a Sunday afternoon in mid-June and Rafael Devers is headed to the San Francisco Giants.

According to recent reports, for those who are tasked with executing such deals, trade talks are a year-round exercise in patience and delicate collaboration. Some executives prefer to sit back and let the market come to them. Others like Dipoto take a more proactive approach.

"We are always going to be on the front end of aggression with how we operate in the market," Dipoto stated in Las Vegas with the Garcia trade evidently simmering in the background. "So whether the market wants to operate faster or slower than it typically does, we're usually not listening — we're just calling.".

With the expiration of the collective bargaining agreement fast approaching, MLB front offices are presently navigating an unusual offseason. The league's team owners are expected to institute a lockout when the CBA expires at 11:59 p.m. on Dec. 1, creating an artificial deadline that could halt transactions right around the time it would typically start to pick up in a normal winter. Some baseball operations leaders have experience dealing with such circumstances having been at the helm in 2021, the last time club owners instituted a lockout when the CBA expired.

Dipoto got a bunch of business done then, including the free-agent signing of Robbie Ray and an extension for reliever Andrés Muñoz. More notably — and more in character — he pulled off one of just a handful of trades before the lockout, a deal with another trade-happy organization in San Diego to acquire second baseman Adam Frazier.

The Mariners have always been active in November under Dipoto, not just in lockout-shortened offseasons.

"Teams know that … if we don't start the hot stove," Dipoto mentioned, "we're among the starters.".

This week's deal to acquire Garcia from Kansas City was the 209th trade Dipoto has made since taking over baseball operations in Seattle at the end of the 2015 season. Thirty-two of these trades have come in November, the first of which was a six-player swap with the Tampa Bay Rays, who have since emerged as Dipoto's favorite trade partner over the years. Nineteen more trades involving the two clubs have followed since that introductory swap 11 years ago, ranging from minor leaguers being flipped for cash to full-blown blockbusters like the 2024 deadline deal that sent Randy Arozarena to Seattle.

As a small-market club without the financial resources to participate frequently in free agency, the Rays have leaned more heavily on trades than any other club — and not just in deals with Dipoto. For Tampa Bay's president of baseball operations Erik Neander, trade talks are never confined to one part of the calendar, but an omnipresent element of how his front office contemplates improving the club. It's also about the people involved.

"Trades to me trace back to relationships, and it traces back to trust," Neander remarked. "It's fun. It's fun to talk baseball, it's fun to talk [about] players, it's fun to think about how two organizations can help one another achieve whatever their goals are at the moment in time. I think if we're doing it well, it's constant. You're always having those conversations.".

Industry observers note that the weeks leading up to the trade deadline can be a frantic time to have these conversations, but those discussions are also influenced heavily by the standings and ultra-specific positional needs at a certain point in time. In the offseason, the scope of what's possible in a trade opens up exponentially. And deals that ultimately die before the deadline can sometimes be resuscitated later.

Industry observers note that "I remember one of the most consequential trades I've ever been a part of, long ago in my career," recalled St. Louis Cardinals president of baseball operations Chaim Bloom. "I was not anywhere close to being in the seat to pull the trigger at the time. But the concept first came up at a deadline, like really close to the buzzer, and it was like, 'Yeah, we can't focus on that right now.' But it ended up being the skeleton of an offseason deal.".

In a recent development, bloom is one of several alumni of Tampa Bay's renowned front office who have since scattered across the league to run other clubs' baseball operations departments, resulting in frequent trade negotiations involving former colleagues. New Athletics president of baseball operations Taylor Smith spent his formative front-office days with the Rays before spending the last two seasons as an assistant general manager with the Boston Red Sox under Craig Breslow.

"Both in Tampa and in Boston, I think the key to making a lot of deals and hopefully making good deals is you need to have both good information that's providing you a competitive advantage and the willingness to act aggressively on that information," Smith noted.

Chaim Bloom (middle), the Cardinals' president of baseball operations pictured with Masyn Winn (left) and Alec Burleson, has Rays roots.

"I think that's something [Neander] is so good at — having conviction to act on his information, to be willing to not need it to be like a slam dunk in order to get it done.".

Smith invoked the 20-80 scouting scale to explain how a steady stream of solid trades can sometimes be more impactful than one massive swing.

In a recent development, "It's knowing that if you make 30 '55' deals over time, that is better than making like two '70' deals. It's constantly acting on your information, trying to make the team better wherever you can, and feeling confident that you have information that's good enough to do that.".

Randy Flores, hired as general manager by the Houston Astros at the end of October, expressed a similar sentiment as he prepares to lead a baseball operations department for the first time and learn the rhythms of trade negotiations at the highest level.

"With front office sizes being the way they are, and the complexity of options, it's really about putting your team in the right position to get accurate info," Flores mentioned in Las Vegas. "Sometimes that's the relationship I might have with someone. Sometimes it's the relationship someone in our office has. And so when I get back to Houston ... I look forward to finding those lanes to get up to speed.".

In a recent development, once in a blue moon, a trade can get suggested and completed in a blink. Far more commonly, they are the product of frequent back-and-forths as teams carefully seek the right combination of players who can address their respective needs.

"There are trades that I've participated in that have been two years in the making, and there have been trades that have taken two minutes," noted Miami Marlins president of baseball operations Peter Bendix, who also honed his front-office mettle for more than a decade with the Rays before heading across Florida to lead the Fish.

"It depends on the players involved and whatnot, but it also depends on the circumstances, right? Sometimes all you have until the deadline is two minutes.".

Liam Hicks was a trade deadline acquisition for the Rays that helped them reach the World Series.

It is hard to ignore that Bendix has made a bevy of trades involving his old pals with the Rays, including this past deadline when Miami sent catcher Liam Hicks to Tampa Bay in exchange for three prospects. Bendix doesn't shy away from his transaction log, acknowledging that it's no surprise that conversations with his former front officemate tend to be fruitful.

Industry observers note that "I enjoy conversations with Erik," Bendix said. "I have so much respect for him. Where they are as a franchise is where we're trying to get to. But it also means that their goals in their trades right now are a little different than our goals, which means it's a little bit easier to line up. And we do speak a common language, and we value players reasonably similarly, which makes it easier to line up than maybe teams that don't value players quite the same way.".

"You've just got to line it all up," Neander said. "You've got to understand the player universe. You've got to understand your priorities.

Sources indicate that "You've got to understand what other teams are trying to do, and then start knocking on doors. And 99 out of 100 don't open.".

When all else fails, Neander usually knows who to call.

"And if they're not opening, find Seattle.".
