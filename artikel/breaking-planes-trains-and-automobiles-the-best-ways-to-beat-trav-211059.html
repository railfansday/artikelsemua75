In a recent development, five-hour flights. Eleven-hour road trips. Fifteen-hour train rides. All potentially exhausting options for the annual tradition of convening around the dinner table with loved ones on Thanksgiving.

According to recent reports, an estimated 81.5 million Americans are projected to travel, or have already traveled, at least 50 miles from their homes between Nov. 24 and Nov. 30, according to the American Automobile Association. And their mode of transportation is shifting: Fewer people are flying or driving this year compared to 2025, and more are traveling by buses, trains and cruises, AAA data indicates. It has been revealed that high gas prices and soaring plane ticket costs may factor into the shift. As of Wednesday evening, fuel prices are roughly 17% higher than they were on the same date in 2025, according to AAA data. Meanwhile, "months of elevated jet fuel costs due to the conflict in Iran have contributed to Thanksgiving and Christmas airfares reaching 10-year highs," according to fare-tracking platform Hopper.

Recent findings suggest that the alternate modes of transit may open travelers up to latest experiences — and new forms of travel fatigue to keep you from returning home feeling rested and fulfilled. You're probably already familiar with the exhaustion you might get from a day of plane travel, for example. In airplanes, "there can be lower humidity, maybe lower oxygen levels at cabin pressure. Sometimes people report feeling more dehydrated or drained. You're adjusting to that change in altitude," says Alaina Tiani, a Cleveland Clinic behavioral sleep medicine psychologist.

But even waiting in places like train stations or cruise ports can feel draining due to sheer sensory overload, Tiani states. And then, depending on your mode of travel, you may still need to deal with cramped seating and limited ability to get up and walk around or drink some water and eat a snack. In a recent development, once you arrive at your destination, managing responsibilities like remembering where you put your hotel key and deciding what to eat and how to structure your day can also take a mental toll, says travel writer Shannon O'Donnell, who's written about managing travel fatigue. "All of that actually requires a lot more thinking and planning for your brain," she says, adding: "People just don't understand how those add up and why a relaxing vacation suddenly has them feeling wrecked.".

In a recent development, the consequences can sneak up on you, O'Donnell notes. Travel fatigue can "present as maybe a lack of interest in something that you might usually find fascinating," she indicates. Instead of looking out the window on a plane ride or playing car games with your kids on a road trip, you might disengage and appear apathetic to the people around you.
