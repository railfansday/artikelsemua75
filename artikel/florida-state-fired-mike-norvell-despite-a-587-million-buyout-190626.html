Ohio State made that commitment to a coach who had already delivered. Day won the national title in 2024, according to CBS Sports. A disappointing season has turned that promise into a cost Ohio State has to weigh. The Buckeyes lost four games in 2026, according to CBS Sports, a heavy total for a team that had won it all two seasons earlier. The cost of acting has also fallen since the $70.9 million figure was dated Dec. 1, 2025. A firing once the 2026 season ends would leave Ohio State with a $57.5 million bill, a figure dated Nov. 21, 2026. The drop comes from how the contract is built. Day's buyout is set as a fixed sum for each remaining year rather than a share of his remaining salary, so the total shrinks as each season passes. The longer the school waits, the less it would pay to make a change, and the less protection Day gets from his contract.

The size of the figure demonstrates how strongly Texas bet on its coach a short time ago, more than how secure his job is today.

Mike Norvell's contract promised him far more protection than his paycheck would suggest. Florida State agreed to a $58.7 million buyout, the sixth-largest among football coaches, even though his salary ranked only 45th in the sport, according to Sports Illustrated. The school wrote the deal to make firing him very costly, not to pay him like one of the game's top earners. A price that large is meant to make a school think hard before it acts. Florida State fired Norvell on the Sunday after a loss to Clemson, The Post and Courier reports, and the size of the bill did nothing to slow the decision. The $58.7 million figure is dated Dec. 1, 2025, and is not a confirmed payout, so what Florida State will end up paying is still unsettled.

In a recent development, at Indiana, the cost of firing Curt Cignetti moved well ahead of what the school was paying him. Indiana agreed to a buyout of $56.7 million, the eighth-largest among college football coaches, according to Sports Illustrated, even though his salary ranked only 18th in the sport. The school promised far more to keep him than it paid him to coach. Indiana used the buyout to show how badly it wanted to hold on to Cignetti, and it made that commitment before his paycheck matched it.

The gap opened because Cignetti won so swiftly that other schools had good reason to want him. The $56.7 million figure dates to Dec. 1, 2025, before his biggest season was over. Indiana went on to finish 16-0 with a national title win over Miami, according to The Gazette. Each of those wins made him more costly to lose, and Indiana gave him a raise after the buyout figure was fixed.

In a recent development, illinois has made Bret Bielema one of the most expensive coaches in college football to fire, and the cost runs well ahead of where his pay ranks. His $49.5 million buyout is the 10th-largest among football coaches, while his salary ranks 19th in the sport, according to Sports Illustrated. The cost is high because Illinois has committed to him for a long time, not because it pays him like a star. The News-Gazette reports that Illinois has extended Bielema's contract for a second time and that the deal now runs six years. The price of firing him reflects the school's own decision to renew its bet on Bielema more than any judgment of what other schools would pay him.

At the top of college football, firing a head coach has become one of the most expensive decisions a university can make. Schools have shown they will pay sums in the tens of millions of dollars to change direction. The Associated Press reports that Texas A&M owed Jimbo Fisher more than $75 million when it decided to start over. LSU also accepted a large buyout to part ways with Brian Kelly, according to CBS Sports. Both schools chose to pay that price rather than keep a coach they no longer wanted. A buyout's size alone doesn't decide whether a coach keeps his job. It sets the price a school must accept to make a change, and the wealthiest programs in the sport have shown they will accept it.

Industry observers note that the figure attached to a coach is less settled than it looks, because it marks the most a school might owe rather than what the school ends up paying. The Associated Press reports that the amount depends largely on how many years remain on a contract. A school that waits a season before acting owes less than one that acts today. A school can also bargain. Penn State owed James Franklin $49 million when it let him go, CBS Sports reports, and the two sides settled on $9 million. That changes how to read any coach's price tag. Each figure describes a contract on one date. It notes as much about what a school was willing to promise as about how secure the coach really is.

It has been revealed that sports Illustrated set out to identify which college football coaches would cost the most to fire, at programs ranging from Indiana and Illinois to Georgia and Florida State. Each figure is the price a school would pay to change course, not a measure of a coach's salary or skill. Some of these contracts were written to keep a winning coach in place. For those schools, a large number is a statement of how much they want to keep the man they have. Other contracts are being tested by losing seasons, and for those schools the figure is the real cost of a change they may be weighing. At least one school on the list has already made that change anyway.

Industry observers note that georgia has made the largest commitment to its coach of any school in college football. As of Dec. 1, 2025, firing Kirby Smart would have cost the school $105.1 million, far more than any other known buyout in the sport, according to Sports Illustrated. Georgia faces a bill that large because of a promise it made, not because of a penalty clause. Bleacher Report reports that the school fully guaranteed Smart's deal if it fires him without cause through Dec. 31, 2028, and that the guarantee drops to 85% after that. Georgia's full promise therefore covers the next few seasons, and for those seasons the school has chosen to face the sport's highest price if it ever wants a change.

The contract binds Georgia far more than it binds Smart. Smart could walk away for a small part of what Georgia would owe to push him out, paying $5 million through the end of 2026, according to Bleacher Report. Smart, not the school, holds the easy way out. If another job ever tempts him, the decision is his.

It has been revealed that georgia has little reason to want a change, because Smart keeps winning. Under Smart, Georgia claimed back-to-back national titles in the 2021 and 2022 seasons and holds a 46-3 record over that stretch, according to Bleacher Report. That kind of winning makes the guarantee easy for the school to stand behind. Georgia wrote the largest buyout in college football to protect a coach who almost never loses. At a school with a struggling coach, a buyout is a price to weigh. At Georgia, it is mostly a statement of intent.

Georgia has promised more than any other school to keep its coach, and Smart is the one who decides whether the deal holds.

A smaller bill alone may not settle Day's future. A school should fire a coach only when it has a clearly better replacement in mind, and the market for coaches is thin right now. Day has gone 89-16 at Ohio State, according to CBS Sports, and has won the large majority of his games. For a change to pay off, Ohio State would need to find a coach who could top that record.

For Ohio State, the money is only one reason to keep Day, alongside his winning record and a short list of better options.

Sources indicate that alabama would pay one of the steepest prices in college football to fire Kalen DeBoer, but most of that price comes from a promise the school made before its latest move. Firing him would cost Alabama $60.8 million, the third-largest buyout among football coaches, according to Sports Illustrated. FanSided reports that DeBoer's original contract already ran through 2031, and that Alabama restructured it and added a raise rather than writing a wholly new commitment. Alabama had largely accepted its side of the bill before the new terms. The school's recent changes were aimed at the other side of the contract.

The restructuring set what DeBoer would owe if he chose to leave. Under the original deal, he could have walked away owing Alabama nothing after Jan. 31, 2027, according to FanSided. Michigan and Penn State were rumored to be interested in him, which made a free exit a real risk for the school. The new deal has DeBoer owing $10 million to leave through Jan. 31, 2027, and that gives Alabama some protection. Even so, the contract binds the school far more than the coach. In exchange for a raise, Alabama made DeBoer harder to lure away.

Industry observers note that alabama made this commitment before DeBoer's results could show whether he is the coach for the long run. He has gone 20-8 in his two seasons at Alabama, according to FanSided, and that record has not settled the question. The cost of firing him comes from the terms Alabama wrote, not from how often his teams win. Alabama is paying to avoid starting over, with the expense and risk of a newly introduced coaching search at a program that has to keep winning now.

At Alabama, the significant number is less armor for DeBoer than a premium the school accepted to keep the coach it knows.

Industry observers note that the exact cost of firing Sarkisian is not a single fixed number. The $60.3 million figure dates to Dec. 1, 2025. Longhorns Wire reports a different amount for a firing after the 2026 season, putting the bill at $50.15 million. Texas now has to decide whether one season's fast fall justifies a check that large.

Firing him would have cost $60 million as of Dec. 1, 2025, the fifth-largest buyout in college football, according to Sports Illustrated, and his salary also ranks among the top 10 in the sport. Clemson pays Swinney like one of the game's elite coaches and has promised to keep doing so for years. Post and Courier reports that his contract runs until 2031, so most of that $60 million covers seasons Swinney has not yet coached. The buyout records how much faith Clemson placed in Swinney when it signed the deal. It does not guarantee his future.

In a recent development, clemson is now weighing that faith against a season that has gone wrong. A sum that large can make a school hesitate, but it does not take the question off the table. Any decision about Swinney would cost Clemson tens of millions of dollars, and it would be a choice the school makes on purpose.

Clemson made its promise to Swinney before this season, and after a middling year the school now has to decide whether it still wants to keep it.

For any school weighing whether to fire a losing coach, Florida State highlights that even a huge buyout does not stop a school from acting.

As of March 2026, he was one of only three active FBS coaches paid $13 million or more a year, along with Georgia's Kirby Smart and Lane Kiffin, according to The Gazette. Indiana wrote the buyout first, and the salary followed.

In a recent development, nebraska does not pay Matt Rhule like one of college football's very top coaches, but firing him would cost the school more than almost any other program would pay to replace its coach. His salary ranks only 15th in the sport, yet his $49.6 million buyout is the ninth-largest among football coaches, according to Sports Illustrated. Rhule's contract with Nebraska covered eight years and $74 million in total as of 2023, according to The Associated Press. The length of that promise, not the size of his yearly paycheck, is what places him among the most expensive coaches to fire. The price reveals how long Nebraska pledged to stand by its coach more than how highly it values him in any single season.

In a recent development, that protection is strongest at the start of the deal and weakens every year the contract runs, whether or not Nebraska does anything. In November 2023, Rhule's buyout stood at $62 million, according to The Associated Press. As of Dec. 1, 2025, Nebraska would have owed him $49.6 million, a much smaller bill only two years later. Any decision about Rhule becomes cheaper for Nebraska the longer the school waits, so the cost of firing him holds the school back less than its size suggests.

Nebraska wrote that protection on purpose. Trev Alberts, the school's athletic director in 2023, considered long terms and buyout clauses the tools that make it harder for a school to push a coach out, according to The Associated Press. He also saw the risk that those tools create for the school itself. "On the other hand, it certainly does put universities in precarious situations if it's apparent a leadership transition is required," Alberts mentioned.

At Nebraska, the buyout is a bet that the school will never need to break its promise to Rhule, and each year he stays, Nebraska would pay less if it lost that bet.

Illinois wrote this contract mainly to keep its coach from leaving. Starting July 1, 2026, the school will pay Bielema a retention incentive of $700,000 each year he stays, according to The News-Gazette. "We’ll pay a premium for that kind of certainty for our program and our student-athletes," Josh Whitman, the Illinois athletic director, said. The large firing price is the other side of that premium, because it is what Illinois would owe to give up the certainty it bought.

Bielema's terms are part of a policy that covers much of the Illinois athletic department. The News-Gazette reports that several Illinois coaches have non-compete clauses in their contracts. Whitman notes the school wants steady leaders at a time when college sports treats turnover as normal. Buyouts carry weight in college football because schools replace coaches so often, and Illinois has chosen to spend money to avoid doing the same.

At Illinois, $49.5 million is less a fee for firing Bielema than a measure of how hard the school has worked to make sure it never needs to.
