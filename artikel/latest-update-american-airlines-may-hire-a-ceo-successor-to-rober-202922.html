Latest Update: American Airlines May Hire A CEO Successor To Robert Isom Before End Of Year — Its Best Outside Candidate Is At Southwest

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When Robert Isom became CEO of American Airlines in 2022, he never named another executive to replace him as president. Now former Wall Street Journal airline reporter Scott McCartney says conversations he’s had lead him to think “American is trying to bring in an outsider as president and heir apparent to CEO Robert Isom.” And he thinks a president could be named by year-end. That’s much more plausible than long-reported speculation that Isom would get replaced as CEO by the board. On Airlines Confidential, McCartney thinks Isom wants to turn American around and then hand it off. McCartney’s first suggestion is a good one: drive across town and talk to Southwest’s COO Andrew Watterson who’s reportedly been sidelined from the CEO path there. American existing chief commercial officer, Nat Pieper, has already appeared to play much of the President role (albeit without full reporting lines tiering up to him). A New President Lets American ‘Try Before They Buy’ This is the board that watched Delta take American’s South American partner LATAM away from it in 2019. Doug Parker kept his job for another two and a half years. When he handed it to Isom, he stayed on as chairman. A board with that history can get comfortable with succession planning a lot more easily than with removing a CEO for poor performance. And – for all of the lingering complaints about Robert Isom – they’ve finally been turning around the airline’s value proposition. Vice Chair Steve Johnson, who ran commercial for 18 months after Vasu Raja left and oversaw the new Citi deal, retires at year-end. Chief Operating Officer David Seymour never got Isom’s old President job and is retiring with severance. Hiring a president lets the board try before it buys. Someone who has run a much smaller airline can be a credible candidate for this job even if you wouldn’t hand them American right away. But what portfolio would the president have? Isom just told employees that Seymour’s successor, JonCarlo Gulbranson, will report directly to him. He could change his mind. Nat Pieper Has Already Been Doing Much Of The Job McCartney says he’s hearing that American wants someone with Delta experience and nobody inside is ready yet. The first requirement would hardly rule out Pieper. Pieper ran Delta’s commercial business in Europe, the Middle East, Africa and India. He helped bring Alaska into oneworld and went on to run the alliance. He’s only been at American about a year, so still qualifies as an outsider. He’s the one explaining to employees why American has to fight United in Chicago. On the October earnings call, Pieper took analysts’ questions and walked investors through an 18.5% year-over-year increase in premium unit revenue. After the August reorganization, Chief Customer Officer Heather Garboden reports to him alone, rather than jointly to him and the operating head. He’s led the process to purchase new widebody jets that’s expected to come to completion soon. None of that is unusual for a commercial chief, but in front of employees and investors he’s already doing much of a president’s job. You can see why Pieper’s name was already coming up around headquarters in February. He’s the only plausible internal successor today. The next step would be greater operational responsibility, and a greater role in finance. Who Would Be Worth Hiring From The Outside? Southwest COO Andrew Watterson wouldn’t even need to relocated, and he’s served as that airline’s chief commercial officer as well. He had responsibility for revenue for two years while running the operation. He’s had to both sell the schedule and run the flights. And he’s well-liked by employees. Paul Jacobson is McCartney’s other suggestion. If he gets passed over for Mary Barra’s job as CEO of General Motors, he could be available. Jacobson spent 23 years at Delta, from financial analyst to CFO. His team raised nearly $30 billion during the pandemic. American has plenty of debt, but its bigger problem is getting customers to pay a premium to fly it. While Delta did this, Jacobson wasn’t in charge of commercial or operations. And he already makes as much as Isom does. Contra McCartney, I’d put Peter Ingram ahead of Jude Bricker. McCartney mentions both, and both spent time at American. Ingram was Hawaiian’s CFO, commercial head and then CEO. He couldn’t turn Hawaiian around and sold it to Alaska, but he wouldn’t be asked to become CEO on day one. And he ran a premium global airline, albeit a smaller one. Bricker was president of Allegiant Air, then ran Sun Country. He also took first class out of Sun Country’s planes. That may have been the right call for Sun Country but his record is running cost-focused operations, while American’s challenges are primarily around revenue rather than cost. Besides, Bricker just bought into Omni Air and became its CEO this month. He’d have to walk away from his own deal. The Delta executive I’d want is Glen Hauenstein. He spent nearly a decade as Delta’s president building precisely the premium revenue advantage American wants. He never got the CEO chair there. But he retired as president in February and agreed to advise Delta through year-end. But it’s not clear he’d come out of retirement for another president’s job without a date for the handover, and he’s older than Jacobson. I wasn’t a fan of Hauenstein, but he certainly understands the commercial side of the Delta business. However Hauenstein is not going to happen. His Delta non-compete appears to run through the end of 2028. Hauenstein retired as president on February 28, 2026. The agreement runs two years, but he’s continuing to receive payments as an advisor through the end of 2026. And Delta’s compensation proposal specifically says that continuing to provide services as an independent contractor keeps the clock from starting unless the compensation committee decides otherwise. American Airlines is explicitly named in the non-compete, Hauenstein would lose his outstanding incentive awards, and the agreement lets Delta seek an injunction to stop him from taking the job. At Delta’s current share price, Hauenstein has an estimated $20 million in unvested incentives contingent on honoring non-compete (if Delta hits its targets). After the next round of payouts, that falls to $7.5 million. American could offer to make him whole, but they still risk a court enforcing the non-compete via an injunction to stop the hire. McCartney wonders whether the CEO search at GM was even triggered by American’s interest in Jacobson – deciding whether they want to give him the big chair or let him go. And McCartney gives long odds to another Delta alumnus, former operating head Jim Whitehurst, who is now a senior partner at Silver Lake and a United board member. There’s a cost to hiring any of them over Pieper. In 2016 American chose then-COO Isom for president and American’s President Scott Kirby went to United. Parker later told employees the board didn’t think it could keep both men, and they had to choose. Ten years later, the executive American is counting on to beat Kirby in Chicago is Nat Pieper. In deciding whether to hire him a new boss, the board has another moment of choosing. Contra McCartney, I’d put Peter Ingram ahead of Jude Bricker. McCartney mentions both, and both spent time at American. Ingram was Hawaiian’s CFO, commercial head and then CEO. He couldn’t turn Hawaiian around and sold it to Alaska, but he wouldn’t be asked to become CEO on day one. And he ran a premium global airline, albeit a smaller one.

Bricker was president of Allegiant Air, then ran Sun Country. He also took first class out of Sun Country’s planes. That may have been the right call for Sun Country but his record is running cost-focused operations, while American’s challenges are primarily around revenue rather than cost.

According to recent reports, besides, Bricker just bought into Omni Air and became its CEO this month. He’d have to walk away from his own deal.

The Delta executive I’d want is Glen Hauenstein. He spent nearly a decade as Delta’s president building precisely the premium revenue advantage American wants. He never got the CEO chair there. But he retired as president in February and agreed to advise Delta through year-end. But it’s not clear he’d come out of retirement for another president’s job without a date for the handover, and he’s older than Jacobson. I wasn’t a fan of Hauenstein, but he certainly understands the commercial side of the Delta business. However Hauenstein is not going to happen.

In a recent development, his Delta non-compete appears to run through the end of 2028. Hauenstein retired as president on February 28, 2026. The agreement runs two years, but he’s continuing to receive payments as an advisor through the end of 2026. And Delta’s compensation proposal specifically indicates that continuing to provide services as an independent contractor keeps the clock from starting unless the compensation committee decides otherwise.

American Airlines is explicitly named in the non-compete, Hauenstein would lose his outstanding incentive awards, and the agreement lets Delta seek an injunction to stop him from taking the job.

At Delta’s current share price, Hauenstein has an estimated $20 million in unvested incentives contingent on honoring non-compete (if Delta hits its targets).
