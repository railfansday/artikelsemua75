It has been revealed that A Upcoming York native, Joe graduated from an avant-garde theater school upstate, where he was obsessed with sketch comedy. There, he and his lunatic friends founded The Sketchies — which still lives on at Skidmore College today. Living in New York City, a screenwriter friend of his then taught him how to write for film. This awakened a new passion in Joe. Since then his scripts have garnered over 200 festival selections, nominations, and wins all over the globe (including the BlueCat Best Feature award). Several of his screenplays have been made into feature films — some of them not that bad! Overall, he loves to create unique, hilarious, touching, and bizarre stories.

The newly introduced millennium brought with it not only a quelling of the widespread fears of the dreaded Y2K bug, but also a surge in brain-busting thrillers. Unforgettable hits like American Psycho, Donnie Darko, Zodiac, Mulholland Drive, and Memento blew audiences away with their twisty takes on various psychological thriller sub-genres. Luckily, though, the substantial headliners weren’t the only noteworthy works produced; a hearty helping of other, not-so-sensationally publicized films broke out in dynamic fashion.

These fun films are six of the most well-executed psychological thrillers proffered by the 2000s. Much like the aforementioned movies, these screen gems cover various sub-sets of the thriller, like sci-fi, crime, and dark comedy, but all share one commonality: they are beautifully bold bangers, thrillin’ and chillin’ viewers the world over.

This list begins with a sensationally subverted action thriller neo-noir from Japan, The Chaser. Director Na Hong-jin takes the familiar trope of the “disgraced, former cop who needs to use his former detecting acumen to solve a latest, strange case” and interjects it with blisteringly bold new life. Bursting with lightning-paced, realistic action sequences and pulse-elevating tension, The Chaser will have you chasing the thrilling cinematic high contained within long after the credits roll.

It has been revealed that eom Joong-ho (Kim Yoon-seok, who does a great job of making a disreputable character very likable) was a cop who was forced to resign in shame because he got caught accepting bribes (which, in Korea, just like the U.S., is a big no-no). He then made the only logical career transition; become a pimp. All is going (seethingly) swimmingly for Eom, until he begins to notice that some of his employees just aren’t coming into work (despite the amazing benefits package he probably offers them). Eom pieces together that all the missing girls were sent to one client, Je Yeong-min (K-film fan favorite, Ha Jung-woo), and that this dastardly dude just may be a murderous sicko. Still clinging to his self-preservation methods, he sends one of his “top earners,” Kim Mi-jin (Seo Young-hee), to investigate. When she doesn’t return, Eom now understands that it’s up to him to use his old wily cop ways to track this pervert down and save Mi-jin before she joins the likes of the other ill-fated call girls. It’s a shamed-cat-and-malevolent-mouse game, with souped-up stakes; one heck of a twisty roller-coaster of underworld thrills — with a shocking, unexpected ending. Is there anything more cinematically satisfying than when the hunter becomes the prey? Director David Slade sure doesn't seem to think so, evidenced by his faux-saccharine, fully sour film Hard Candy. The themes explored in this one are definitely controversial, and it was quite refreshing that someone addressed the repulsive behavior of certain cretins in the world — all while delivering a “revenge is a dish best served cold” finale.

Industry observers note that the seriously icky story focuses on Hayley Stark (Elliot Page), a precocious 14-year-old who initially comes off as appropriately naive. She encounters a 32-year-old photographer online, Jeff Kohlver (Patrick Wilson), who she playfully chats with for several weeks. It appears that Jeff is grooming her; he compliments her in just the right manner to get her in the ideal mindset to do his bidding. Hayley seems to be playing it safe by agreeing to meet Jeff in a public space, irl, but then appears to lose her good judgment as she elects to go back to Jeff’s home with him. Jeff has her right where he wants her, then: oops. This was all an elaborate scheme that Hayley devised. She drugs Jeff, ties him up, and grills him. The next twist within a twist is that Hayley doesn’t just want to expose Jeff as the slimy pedophile that he is: she has serious questions about a girl who mysteriously went missing. A savagely delightful take on the damsel in distress trope, Slade's film — with its wily and firmly resolute protagonist — is a fun way to watch a villain get what they really deserve. Modern films that pay homage to the style of Alfred Hitchcock have the benefit of a pretty darn solid jumping off point; even if the filmmakers fail to reach the level of the master of cerebral cinema, at least you get a decent copy. Italian director Giuseppe Tornatore fortunately did a molto bello job with his mystery-thriller The Unknown Woman (originally titled La sconosciuta), which exudes Hitchcockian vibes all over the place (a spiral staircase or two ostentatiously tips the viewer off). With a commanding female lead, creative camerawork, and a stirring score, this neo-melodrama is one that definitely should not remain unknown.

According to recent reports, the plot is centered around Irena (Kseniya Rappoport, absolutely shredding this role), an immigrant from Ukraine who has fought and clawed her way through the brutal trials of human trafficking and landed in Trieste, a spectacular coastal city in Italy. Here she finagles her way into a nanny position for an affluent family (after enacting a somewhat diabolical initiative to get their previous childcare worker “booted”). The parents, Donato Adacher (Pierfrancesco Favino) and Valeria (Claudia Gerini), initially seem oblivious, but as the truth surrounding the lineage of their daughter Tea (Clara Dossena) leaks out, the mystery fully unravels and obfuscated secrets come to light. The pain that Irena has experienced is emotionally visceral and visually impactful in this stunning work of thought-provoking thrills. Bravo, tutti.

While Jurassic Park was possibly the first movie to discuss “chaos theory” by name, it’s brought to stirring life in directors Eric Bress and J. Mackye Gruber’s exhilarating sci-fi-tinged thriller The Butterfly Effect. The concept is basically that one minuscule change can have a ripple effect in not only one person’s life, but everyone else in their orbit — and beyond. In the film, the lead character doesn’t have to just speculate about what would happen if he made this choice or that; he gets to see the results play out in living color, and the overall effect is mesmerizing (and often devastating).

Evan Treborn (Ashton Kutcher, in a rare serious role — which he’s actually quite adept at) is a college student with massive gaps in his memory due to ultra-traumatic events that occurred in his childhood. He stumbles on a journal that he scribed when he was a kid, and, through some weird wormhole or another, he is able to travel back to the specific times in his existence where disaster struck, and alter them. However, with all good comes some (or a lot of) bad. When he’s zapped back into the present, the alterations that he made to his timeline have drastic, and sometimes dire, consequences — for not only himself, but for his family and friends, too. His girlfriend, Kayleigh (Amy Smart, film darling of the early 2000s), is typically at his side…until one of the time warp adaptations leaves her, well, exploded. So, Evan is faced with a real moral conundrum: live with the stark repercussions of his time manipulation, or find a way to circumvent the whole scenario so that the people he cherishes (namely, Kayleigh) can come out unscathed — even if that means never meeting them in the first place. The whole film is a mind-blowing game of “what if?,” where the answer is never what one hopes.

Recent findings suggest that robin Williams obviously had a dramatic/comedic range that was off the charts. Here, in writer-director Mark Romanek’s understated slow-burn sensation, One Hour Photo, Williams was able to demonstrate his ability to tackle a character that begins as almost the polar opposite of the manic ones he portrayed throughout most of his career — and the result is a shockingly profound one.

It has been revealed that seymour Parrish (Williams) is a humble, loner of a man. He ekes out a meager existence working in the rapid photo lab of a store (remember those?), and, while viewing strangers' pictures, he often develops illusory attachments to them. The prime object of his initially benign affection is the Yorkin clan, with mom, Nina (Connie Nielsen), dad, Will (Michael Vartan), and little tyke Jakob (Dylan Smith). While Seymour creates fantasies in his mind about how he is actually, emotionally connected to these individuals, the reality is that he barely knows them — only having interacted with the Yorkins when their film is dropped off. Then, a monumental event occurs for Seymour; he witnesses, in the pics, that Will is having an affair. Uh-oh. This sends Seymour completely off the rails, as he spirals into a vengeful fit (the idealized family he once knew and loved has been shattered). Seymour then takes matters into his own disassociated hands, and things get a touch messy. With creepy angles and voyeuristic shots in spades, this film makes the viewer's skin crawl clean off. Romanek showcases how, back in 2002 (prior to Flock cams) and even well before that, there was always the possibility that you were being watched, intently.

It has been revealed that now for the film that set the adoption movement back hundreds of years, director Jaume Collet-Serra’s wild ride of a psycho-thriller, Orphan. This terrifying film is almost a home invasion movie; one where the villain has been joyfully invited into the victims’ very domicile. Packed with eerie visuals and electrifying (in a horrific way) set pieces, this is one film that definitely shouldn’t be orphaned on your streamer of choice.

According to recent reports, kate Coleman (Vera Farmiga) and John Coleman (Peter Sarsgaard) are a relatively content couple with two children, Daniel (Jimmy Bennett) and Max (Aryana Engineer). However, they lost another baby due to miscarriage, and this has left a hole in both parents’ hearts, especially Kate’s. So, they do what many kind couples around the world have done: they go to the adoption center to snatch up a pre-made kid. The lucky girl chosen is Esther (Isabelle Fuhrman), a little human ostensibly from Russia. It seems that Esther is quite precocious, scarily even, and after several odd occurrences, it’s clear that she is not in possession of a great deal of empathy — but she definitely does have a manipulative streak. As tragedies begin to befall the Coleman household, Esther’s true nature is revealed in a twist that will knock your knickers clean off. Orphan is a terrifically terrifying film, with heartfelt performances and keen direction. Just don’t expect any heartwarming Annie-like musical numbers.
