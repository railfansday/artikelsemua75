What’s better than a hike through fragrant evergreens with the nip of crisp winter air (and the effort of chopping your own Christmas tree) to bring a flush to your cheeks this holiday season? Whether you’re in a forest or a tree farm, these tips will help you bring home a terrific tannenbaum.

If you proposal on heading into the woods to cut down your Christmas tree, first determine whether you need a permit to do so. Call your municipal officials, for a start. Christmas tree permits are available through Recreation.gov—but hurry, because some offices do sell out of permits.

Recent findings suggest that some people enjoy decking out the home for the holidays right after they’ve cleared the Thanksgiving dishes, or maybe even right after that first pumpkin spice latte of the season. But if you’re decorating a real tree, it’s best to wait. Even trees that are watered regularly get dried out within 3 weeks of cutting, so try to resist the urge to decorate too early. According to recent reports, unlike the Christmas trees at the local lots, trees on a farm or in the forest don’t necessarily come in predictable 6- or 8-foot sizes. To avoid disappointment, measure the distance from floor to ceiling where you plan to place your tree to make sure you don’t cut down a tree that’s too big for your house. While you’re at it, measure the space in or above your car to make sure you’ll be able to accommodate the tree, and don’t forget to bring twine, bungees, scissors, and anything else you might need to secure the tree in (or on) your car.

Though cutting down your Christmas tree is a fun holiday excursion, you’ll be handling blades or saws, so it’s vital to wear appropriate safety attire. Put on a pair of sturdy boots, pack a pair of heavy work gloves. Avoid wearing loose clothing to keep it from tangling in the tools or trees.

Industry observers note that depending on where you’re cutting down your tree, there may be restrictions on the tools you can use. Although a chain saw is the quickest and easiest way to fell a tree, many tree farms and national forests don’t allow their use, so be prepared with a handsaw or axe, just in case.

It has been revealed that even if you immediately spot “the one,” it’s best to do a full walk-through before you commit to a particular tree. Christmas tree farms are often arranged by size or type of tree, so taking a look at everything else that’s available will help you avoid buyer’s remorse on the way out to the car.

Try to cut your Christmas tree as low to the ground as you possibly can. This will not only make it a taller tree, but it will also allow another tree to resprout and spread Christmas cheer to future tree-hunters.

Have a partner hold your tree steady to prevent it from falling over before you’ve sawed all the way through the trunk. Also, resist the temptation to push the tree over: Not only is this unsafe, but it will also prevent regrowth.

Industry observers note that your freshly cut Christmas tree will probably be rife with freeloading bugs, sap, and spiderwebs, so give it a good shake before you pack it in the car to keep your living room free of these natural nuisances. Some tree farms even have mechanical shakers that will get the job done, for a fee.
