“A quest is a search and an adventure,” reads the opening salvo of the September 1976 Popular Mechanics cover story, “7 Great Quests of Man.” Admittedly, this is a somewhat muted, almost “Maid of Honor Reads the Webster’s Dictionary Definition of Love for Her Toast”-esque opener to place beneath such a grandiose headline.

In defense of that half-century-old article, finding a really great opening line for a story can be hard. That’s as true now as it was 50 years ago. At least the author didn’t take the easy way out and just start it by quoting somebody else instead. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

But what of the “Great Quests” themselves? Have any of them been conquered in the last half-century? Have any of the tools and technologies developed since 1976 brought us a smidge closer to solving these age-old mysteries?

To mark the 50th anniversary of the “7 Great Quests of Man,” we took a look back at the quests that captivated readers in 1976, and how close we’ve come to bringing them to an end since then.

Industry observers note that the first Great Quest on the list is one of the oldest, the search for the lost continent of Atlantis. The earliest extant record of the story of Atlantis was penned by Plato, who put the words in the mouth of his interlocutor Critias in a pair of dialogues written around 360 B.C.E. (Related: Leading House Democrat targets Trump’s inner circle for...)

In a recent development, by 1976, Atlantis was having a moment in the sun (one whose rays could presumably reach its watery depths). Paranormal phenomena researcher Charles Berlitz’s book The Mystery of Atlantis was back on bookshelves in a upcoming paperback edition, and on television, everyone from Leonard Nimoy to Jabberjaw was seemingly in search of the sunken continent.

In “7 Great Quests of Man,” after dismissing the claims of a psychic who noted Atlantis would rise again, we actually offered up a different theory: that Atlantis had already been found back in the 1960s, and it was actually a volcanic island called Santorini.

In a recent development, at the time, we dated the eruption to sometime between 1480 and 1450 B.C.E. Modern radiocarbon evidence suggests it happened more than a century earlier, likely around 1600 B.C.E. Either way, the blast caused a large part of the island to sink, leaving only three smaller islands above water (including the largest, now called Thera or Santorini).

“During the 1960s excavations of Thera revealed that it had once been the home of the Minoans,” we wrote, “These were a highly civilized, seafaring people whose center was on the island of Crete about 70 miles from Santorini. The Minoans on Thera had been wiped out by the eruption. The well-preserved ruins now being uncovered are one of the major archaeological finds of this century.”.

According to recent reports, here in the 21st century, Santorini is actually still making waves, literally. We reported last year that magma from the island’s crust caused a remarkable 25,000 tremors in just a 30-day period. And while advancements in tech like sonar have yet to turn up any evidence of Atlantis, intrepid adventurers have uncovered several other sunken cities, from the relics of Canopus and the Sanctuary of Poseidon at Samikon to the medieval city of Shipden and the “Atlantis of the Sands,” Ubar.

Industry observers note that next up on the list was Noah’s Ark. That readers in 1976 were dying to know where Noah’s Ark was is actually no surprise; it would be the same as folks in 2026 wanting to find the Trojan Horse after seeing The Odyssey. That’s because in 1976, a documentary called In Search of Noah’s Ark proved a box-office sensation, becoming one of the top ten highest-grossing films of the year.

Part of the film’s appeal was the sentiment that the remnants of the Biblical vessel could well be hidden away on Turkey’s Mount Ararat, and only the political strife of our modern times was keeping us from finding the ancient treasure. After all, during this time, the United States had actually implemented an arms embargo on Turkey after its actions in Cyprus in 1974, so things were undoubtedly fraught geopolitically in the region.

“7 Great Quests of Man” suggests as much. “Right now the Turkish government is discouraging foreign expeditions to Mount Ararat,” it concludes, “it’s in a politically sensitive area.” Back then, we remarked that Ark hunters would no doubt return to the site “as soon as restrictions are lifted,” which is the kind of thing you casually say when you don’t realize there’s about to be a violent military coup in four years.

Now, in the 21st century, some progress has actually been made in the search for Noah’s Ark. In October 2026, we reported that soil samples were taken from Mount Ararat’s Durupinar formation, a peculiar geological feature in the area that’s long been considered a potential resting place for the Biblical vessel. The preliminary tests highlighted a high concentration of organic material, such as wood. Further lab work is being undertaken to try and determine “if man-made activity or decaying wood sit in the soil.”.

“The hottest quest going in 1976 is the quest for the Loch Ness monster,” we wrote in “7 Great Quests of Man,” and that was no joke. 1976 really was Nessie’s year, with coverage of the alleged cryptid even appearing in the pages of the MIT Technology Review and the Newly introduced York Times.

It has been revealed that why was the Loch Ness Monster having such a moment during the Bicentennial? Simple: In 1975, new photographs purportedly showing the creature had surfaced. While we’re all familiar with the famous 1930s photo of Nessie’s alleged neck rising above the water, the two new pictures came courtesy of an underwater camera, and seemed to show the creature close up and beneath the waves. “One highlights a close-up of what may be the head of an unknown animal,” we wrote in 1976, “the other what may be its head, neck and body.”.

Back then, we cited numerous notable biologists who felt the images were “sufficiently suggestive of a large aquatic animal” and concluded that “the long search may be nearing a conclusion.”.

Back in 2013, Scientific American blamed the photos’ appeal on pareidolia, our brain’s habit of finding faces and figures where there are none. The photo that was thought to show Nessie’s head “is most likely a gnarly tree-stump on the loch floor.”.

That being said, the last few years have still yielded some interesting Loch Ness Monster talk, from a 2025 study concluding that Nessie wouldn’t look like you think (if it exists at all) to the recent recovery of a 1970s Loch Ness Monster trap with its own underwater camera (which captured no Nessie pictures, unfortunately).

So, in a sense, the Loch Ness Monster mystery is kind of like the Starland Vocal Band’s “Afternoon Delight.” It’s still around on the periphery today, and we’re all a little confused as to why it was such a massive thing back in ’76.

For decades, man pondered two questions:.

2) Could Bigfoot beat the Six Million Dollar Man in a fight?

And in 1976, one of those questions was finally answered. In a two-part episode of The Six Million Dollar Man called “The Secret of Bigfoot,” Steve Austin (the character played by Lee Majors, not the professional wrestler) faced off against Bigfoot (played by André the Giant, the actual professional wrestler). Believe it or not, this was just one small component of the Bigfoot craze that overtook America in the 1970s. There were Bigfoot movies, Bigfoot TV indicates, a Bigfoot board game and, unbeknownst to the average American, the FBI was even actively investigating the legitimacy of hairs that reportedly came from Sasquatch himself.

For real, Bigfoot has an FBI file, and we read it.

It has been revealed that as our article pointed out back then, while talk of Yetis and Sasquatches originated near the start of the 20th century, 1970s Bigfoot mania was ignited by the famed Patterson-Gimlin film. You know the one. You’ve undoubtedly seen it. Back in 1967, amateur monster hunters Roger Patterson and Bob Gimlin went out into the woods with a camera and captured footage of a seven-foot-tall creature covered in hair strolling by. While some were skeptical of the footage, thinking it was just a guy in a suit, back in 1976 our writer spoke to a primate expert at the Smithsonian Institution who said, “I could not see the zipper and I still can’t.”.

Now in 2026, though, you can see the zipper if you want—in a manner of speaking, anyway.

We don’t want to bum out the Bigfoot believers here. Look, maybe there is still a Sasquatch out there somewhere—and if you ask that one guy, there certainly was one, but it’s dead now). But as for the Patterson-Gimlin film? That received a thorough debunking in 2026 thanks to the revelatory documentary Capturing Bigfoot. The film, which debuted at SXSW in March 2026, confirms that the famous footage was a hoax, revealing never-before-seen “rehearsal footage” shot by the men in 1966, featuring a different design for the Sasquatch.

According to recent reports, “Oak Island, a small island on Mahone Bay off the coast of Nova Scotia, is the site of what has to be the most frustrating quest of modern times,” we wrote back in 1976. “There may be a treasure on the island. People have known where the treasure is buried for nearly 200 years. But it is so cleverly protected that all attempts to recover it have been foiled.”.

Recent findings suggest that from the supposed “Money Pit” to the cipher stone to the Oak Island Curse, this north-of-the-border treasure hunt has built up its own glossary of lore and lingo over the last two centuries as eager treasure hunters braved booby traps in search of a historic payday. Popular Mechanics first covered the Oak Island treasure hunt 100 years ago, in the March 1926 story “Hunting Hidden Treasures,” and its allure attracted the attention of major names like John Wayne and Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Here in 2026, we can say that the Oak Island mystery stands out from the other “Great Quests” outlined in our pages 50 years ago for one specific reason: the “7 Great Quests of Man” list itself had a direct influence on the current-day search for answers about Oak Island.

How do we know that? Well, the new faces of the Oak Island treasure hunt noted so themselves.

“Growing up, I loved that magazine. Absolutely loved that magazine,” Marty Lagina told us. “There is one connection to Popular Mechanics too, aside from our interest in the magazine,” brother Rick Lagina chimed in. “There was an article in Popular Mechanics about Oak Island.” The two men are the stars of the long-running television series The Curse of Oak Island, currently airing its 14th season. While they’ve yet to uncover some massive trove at the bottom of the much-discussed Money Pit, the show’s efforts have helped uncover some of the island’s previously undocumented history, and the series’ popularity has kept the mysterious island in the cultural zeitgeist.

“Did visitors from other planets come to earth in ancient times?” we asked in 1976. Then we answered: “Recently, yes has become a very popular answer to that question.” And this was no exaggeration. People really were all in on this theory in the 1970s, and not just the fringe conspiracy crowd.

To give you an idea of just how pervasive alien fever was, we need to touch on a film called Chariots of the Gods. Based on the pseudoscientific work of Erich von Däniken, this 1970 documentary laid out the theory that the world’s religions and early human development were all influenced by ancient extraterrestrial visitors.

How did audiences in the Disco Decade receive this claim? Well, not only was the film a commercial success, it actually managed to get an Academy Award nomination for Best Documentary Feature that year, putting it up alongside the historic concert film Woodstock and the MLK documentary King: A Filmed Record... Montgomery to Memphis. That would be like if this year You Can See Everything had to compete against an episode of Ancient Aliens at the Oscars.

Sources indicate that supporters of the theory pointed to ancient constructions they felt couldn’t have been built with “primitive tools,” and massive monuments constructed that could only be seen properly from the skies. Skeptics claimed these “view-from-above” tributes were intended for gods in the heavens, but true believers remarked it was a message to aliens.

In our world today, the ancient alien theory still gets bandied about from time to time. Recently, we’ve covered phenomena like the “Black Knight” satellite and the Silurian hypothesis, both of which have attracted their fair share of fringe “it was aliens” theories. But arguably the greatest cultural cachet the conspiracy theory has today is the face we’re all picturing when we read the sentence “it was aliens.”.

Industry observers note that you know who we’re talking about. You’ve seen the memes.

Author Giorgio Tsoukalos got the meme treatment all the way back in 2010 for his credulous role on the immensely popular television show Ancient Aliens. That series, which still airs today, took the torch of Chariots of the Gods and ran with it, keeping the “ancient aliens” theory in the popular discourse through 22 television seasons, a video game, and even an adjacent series in which audiences watch very stoned people watch the show. As a Great Quest, we’ve come no closer to finding proof of ancient aliens, but as a pop culture concept? The brand has literally never been stronger.

“Ask most scientists what they think of the ancient-astronaut theories, and they will just snort at you,” we wrote in 1976. “But that doesn’t mean that they don’t take the subject of intelligent life in space seriously. They do. They are now trying to contact extraterrestrial civilizations. That is the last, and perhaps the most exciting, quest of all.”.

It’s likely the oldest of the “7 Great Quests of Man,” as well. For as long as mankind has been aware that there was more beyond our world, we’ve wondered if there’s anybody out there. Unlike the other quests, your Bigfoots and Noah’s Arks—which call for some credulity, some willingness to believe the thing is possible—to many people, the idea that there must be some other intelligent life out in our vast universe feels so plausible that it’s hard not to resist. Yet, despite how improbable it seems that mankind is really alone in the universe, no credible evidence has ever materialized that there’s intelligent life out there.

It has been revealed that A few years before our article was published, in 1972, Pioneer 10 took off from Cape Kennedy carrying a gold-covered plate with images of a man and a woman accompanied by diagrams that scientists hoped could be understood by any intelligent life it reached. It wasn’t the first attempt to contact alien life (Dr. Frank Drake launched Project Ozma back in 1960), but the legendary Carl Sagan thought this “message in a bottle” approach might be the key to communicating with intelligent life among the stars.

In the 21st century, the search for intelligent life continues unabated, be it attempts to identify aliens moving about in our universe today, or searching for remnants of those who may have died off far in the distant past. At Pop Mech, we’ve continued to keep our eyes to the skies, while trying to contend with the perplexing Fermi Paradox—the question of why, in a universe billions of years old, we appear to be the only intelligent life to ever exist in it.

Yet, with all our new technology developed in the last 50 years, we still find ourselves gazing up at the stars and wondering who might be out there, just as we did when “7 Great Quests of Man” was written.

In a recent development, back then, we posited that our message would be heard by whatever life is out there. “It may happen a thousand years from now, or tomorrow.” We’ll check back in on that in another 50 years.
