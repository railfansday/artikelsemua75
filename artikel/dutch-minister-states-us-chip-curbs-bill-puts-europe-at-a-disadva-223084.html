Dutch minister states US chip curbs bill puts Europe at a disadvantage

US bill to tighten controls on chipmaking equipment sales to China goes “way too far”, Dutch trade minister Sjoerd Sjoerdsma told parliament on Thursday, according to Reuters.

The minister stated the proposal could put Europe at an unacceptable disadvantage. His warning targets the Multilateral Alignment of Technology Controls on Hardware Act, or MATCH Act, which the US Congress is negotiating to attach to its annual defence policy bill.

The Dutch government has been lobbying against the bipartisan proposal for months. Under the bill, US allies with major semiconductor industries, including the Netherlands and Japan, would have 150 days to bring their export controls on China into line with Washington’s. If they did not, the US would impose restrictions on their equipment makers itself. (Related: Tech Report: One Piece Chapter 1200: Release date & maj...)

That would land hardest on ASML, the Dutch company whose lithography machines are essential for making advanced chips, and Europe’s most valuable listed firm. (Related: Scientists find “field” of washing machines in ocean du...)

The government opposes the bill’s extraterritorial reach, Sjoerdsma noted, and the damage would not stop at the Dutch border, because other European countries’ chip industries would also be hit.

“We will not simply be told what to do. If the act is adopted, our message to the US administration will be to not enforce it,” Sjoerdsma stated.

Industry observers note that he also pointed to the stakes for the region’s AI ambitions, telling parliament that Europe cannot afford to fall behind in semiconductors. Sjoerdsma mentioned he was in close contact with his European colleagues about a common response to the bill.

In a recent development, the remarks are a sharp turn in a relationship that has mostly bent to US pressure. The Netherlands criticised US restrictions in 2023 but still introduced its own controls on ASML’s advanced machines that year, and has tightened them several times since.

ASML’s former chief executive Peter Wennink argued in 2024 that the ever-expanding export curbs were driven more by US economics than by security. China was once ASML’s second-largest market, and the restrictions have already cut its sales there.

Whether the MATCH Act becomes law now depends on the defence bill negotiations in Congress, where lawmakers are deciding which provisions make it into the final text. No date has been set for a vote, which leaves the Dutch government time to press its case in Washington.
