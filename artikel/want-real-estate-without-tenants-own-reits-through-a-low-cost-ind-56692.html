Want Real Estate Without Tenants? Own REITs Through a Low-Cost Index Fund

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A REIT, or real estate investment trust, owns income property and must pay out most of its taxable income as dividends. To add real estate without becoming a landlord, buy a low-cost REIT index fund and hold it in an IRA or 401(k), where you owe no tax on those dividends each year.

If you own a total stock market index fund, you already own some REITs, but only a sliver. Real estate makes up 2.1% of Vanguard’s total stock market ETF.

Industry observers note that A REIT index fund raises your real estate share in one purchase. Its holdings are mostly equity REITs, which collect rent on property from apartments and warehouses to cell towers and data centers. Schwab U.S. REIT ETF holds about 120 of them and charges 0.07% of your investment a year. It leaves out mortgage REITs, which lend to property owners instead of owning buildings. Vanguard Real Estate ETF charges 0.13% a year. Both trade on a stock exchange, so you can sell shares on any day the market is open. The other two ways to own REITs take more work or cost more than an index fund. Picking individual REITs means judging each company’s cash flow and debt yourself. Non-traded REITs don’t trade on an exchange and are hard to sell, whether you buy them from a broker or a real estate crowdfunding app. The SEC claims sales commissions and upfront fees on the ones brokers and advisers sell usually total about 9% to 10% of your investment.

According to recent reports, put 5% to 10% of your portfolio in the fund. A REIT has to pay out at least 90% of its taxable income as dividends, and you pay the income tax on them. Nareit, the REIT industry’s trade association, found 79% of the REIT dividends it tracked for 2025 were ordinary income. That part is taxed at your regular rate, not the lower rate other companies’ dividends can get. (Related: Industry News: My Journey to a serverless transformers ...)

Most of that income qualifies for a 20% deduction, a break the 2025 tax law made permanent, but in a taxable account you still owe tax on the dividends every year.

So buy the fund in an IRA, where its dividends aren’t taxed as they’re paid, or in your 401(k) if its menu includes a REIT index fund.
