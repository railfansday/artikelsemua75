This is the question that an eight-year-old named Josephine (Mason Reeves) asks her dad, Damien (Channing Tatum). She’s a sporty kid, the kind of youngster who accompanies her jockish pops on long runs through Golden Gate Park every Sunday morning. It’s during one of these weekly father-daughter bonding sessions that Josephine sprints ahead of her dad and strays off their usual path. Stopping to catch her breath, she sees a female jogger go into a public restroom at the bottom of a hill. Then Josephine watches as a stranger pulls the woman out, knocks her to the ground, and sexually assaults her.

The cops arrest the perpetrator, the survivor goes to the hospital, and eventually, Damien is left with his daughter and that question, hanging in the air. Josephine can’t comprehend what she’s witnessed. Her father doesn’t know how to answer. Instead of comforting or explaining, Damien just resumes their usual training exercises; he’s got to strengthen her up for soccer, he tells his daughter. His intentions are noble. He wants to protect her by pretending nothing’s happened. But Josephine’s father is simply the first adult who fails to help her process the traumatic experience this young girl has gone through. The Grand Jury Prize winner at this year’s Sundance Film Festival, Josephine doesn’t just follow its namesake as she’s forced to navigate a sudden loss of innocence; it sticks so closely to her perspective that you’re allowed to see every confusing gesture, every mixed-message reply, every argument among her parents, and every acting-out response through her eyes. That itself isn’t unusual — most coming-of-age movies let you walk miles in their hero’s Converse All Stars. Yet writer-director Beth de Araújo’s sophomore film handles this shattering take on a kid wrapping her head around trauma with such grace, such delicacy, such force and a lack of false notes, that the work feels seismic even in its silences. It’s not interested in debating how crimes are handled by the cops or the courts, or how society talks around instead of about sexual violence in a constructive way. The movie wants you to reckon with the impact of someone figuring out a foreign landscape filled with unforeseen terrors. Josephine, c’est moi. So much of this rests on the tiny shoulders of Reeves, a first-time actor who seems to intuitively understand how to simply be in front of the camera. When Araújo isn’t replicating Josephine’s point of view, letting things unfold from a lower angle looking up or glimpsed from a distance, she’s training her lens directly on Reeves’ face in close-up. You can see the character attempting to grasp and contextualize situations for which she has no reference point in real time. And when Josephine begins to parrot statements and repeat patterns of behavior — she sees Damien spit on the rapist, then later spits on her dad in a fit of rage — we can see her trying on toxic versions of adulthood for size. It’s an incredible performance by any parameter, much less one delivered by a preteen.

Josephine makes space for her parents as well, with Tatum weaponizing his varsity-athlete physicality and kinder, gentler masculinity to play Damien as a well-meaning yet grasping dad, a paterfamilias in over his head when it comes to talking about tough subjects. His solutions: Take a self-defense class and either sweat out the pain or bury it. Gemma Chan plays Josephine’s mom, a dancer named Claire, as someone both more sympathetic to their child’s bewilderment and equally adrift. (A sequence in which she’s asked by her daughter if she’s ever been assaulted is all the more painful by the lack of an actual answer; Chan’s look alone tells you more than you feel you should know.) Tempers get lost, voices get raised, and Josephine herself lashes out in ways that suggest she doesn’t know how to deal with the emotional turbulence coursing through her. Recent findings suggest that you don’t need to know that Josephine comes from an extremely personal place to feel that Araújo (whose debut, a Neo-Nazi Karen parable titled Soft and Quiet, now feels like an artist clearing her throat before speaking harshly and loudly) is unpacking this youngster’s story with an extraordinary amount of acuity and sensitivity, even as she refuses to pull punches: The assault is shown forensically, pitilessly, and in full. So is Josephine’s cross-examination on the stand by the defense’s lawyer (Dana Millican) when the child is forced to testify as a witness, which represents the ways our legal system can put everyone involved on trial. Surreal touches, such as the ghostly presence of the rapist (Philip Ettinger) hovering around everyday situations, add to the feeling that you’re viewing all of this through a highly subjective lens. Josephine sees this person everywhere, as a phantom both menacing and eerily childlike. So why shouldn’t we?

“Do you think the man is bad on the inside?” Josephine asks one of her caretakers. “Or good on the inside, but does bad things?” Nobody really knows the answer to that, she’s told. Neither Araújo nor the movie itself pretends to have a satisfying reply. But it’s desperate to ask the question and push you to wonder how a child, in some ways wise beyond her years yet still very much a kid, can reckon with a morality that exists in deepening shades of gray. Josephine is a movie that begins in a moment of fear — Josephine is scared to run out of her garage before the automatic door closes — and ends with a portrait of courage under fire. That the courage comes from someone who can barely comprehend her own bravery only makes the moment that much more impressive. It’s hard to think of a work of art that feels this simultaneously scarring and healing. Or that is tougher to watch yet something that must be seen as soon as possible and by any means necessary.
