OpenAI published its AI progress meter to encourage a slowdown. It won’t work

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that though it’s been lost amid the Jensen Huang drama at Nvidia, OpenAI made a startling revelation on Sunday. In an essay, the firm’s chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, made an overture to rival AI labs to agree on ways to slow down the development of fresh models. But with billions at stake and companies racing for enterprise customers, there’s little reason to believe anyone will hit the brakes. Pachocki’s essay, titled “Perplexity and Intelligence,” includes some progress numbers that may have been meant to jolt the industry into a slowdown. He discloses for the first time an internal metric that OpenAI uses to track how much its models improve during pretraining. Pachocki invites other frontier AI labs to publish comparable numbers, creating a common basis for monitoring—and potentially coordinating a slowdown in—the development of artificial intelligence until adequate safety and monitoring tools can be developed. OpenAI’s CEO, Sam Altman, praised the essay, going on X to call it “the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.” Microsoft AI’s CEO, Mustafa Suleyman, voiced his company’s support for the effort and reported a progress score for one of its own models. Pachocki’s proposal, however, is unlikely to matter if the other labs don’t publish their own progress numbers and agree to speed limits. And so far, other labs, including Anthropic and Google DeepMind, have not signaled a willingness to sign on. An alarming acceleration Pachocki has good reason to propose a pause. OpenAI’s numbers show AI advancing at an alarmingly fast rate. OpenAI’s own progress score improved more in 2026 than in the previous three years combined, Pachocki indicates, and an unreleased internal model highlights another large stride in intelligence. The numbers OpenAI published are “perplexity” scores, which measure how confidently a language model predicts the next word in unfamiliar text. Researchers test models on withheld data, with lower scores indicating more confident predictions and a log-perplexity score of zero meaning the model assigns 100% probability to every correct token. (Yes, the AI search company Perplexity was, sadly, named after this metric.) Since 2022, OpenAI’s base-model log-perplexity has fallen from roughly 0.99 (capable of “fluent conversation”) to 0.76 (capable of solving “a Millennium Prize math problem”)—a striking improvement in how well models predict tokens, which can represent words, numbers, code, etc. The chart’s biggest story may concern what comes after current frontier models. OpenAI’s most recent model, GPT-6.2 Astra, reaches a score of 0.76, while an unreleased internal model has already dropped to a score of 0.73, OpenAI says—a conspicuous break from the earlier pace of progress. Pachocki’s essay comes on the heels of a claim by the Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge that Claude helped him prove the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis, a famous unsolved problem in mathematics. Earlier this year, OpenAI’s own models solved a series of exceptionally difficult math problems. But also, the company’s AI agents broke out of their testing environment this summer and infiltrated servers at Hugging Face, an AI collaboration platform, looking for ways to cheat on a math task. And AI models have advanced rapidly in computer coding and cyber capabilities this year, as well as in their ability to develop new AI models. A decent proposal Pachocki asks frontier developers to set shared norms for evaluating and red-teaming models before release and to commit to common limits, based on perplexity and other measures, that no one crosses until monitoring reaches a concrete milestone. Today, AI labs use a kind of monitoring tool to track a reasoning model’s chain of thought, or the logical steps the model takes to reach a conclusion or action. Pachocki suggests developing a new kind of monitoring tool to keep pace with improvements in perplexity. And that’s one of the problems. The AI labs use different testing metrics and training data mixes, so imposing a shared limit on lowering perplexity scores would first require a common way of measuring perplexity: a shared test. Also, because perplexity alone doesn’t capture gains from reasoning and reinforcement learning, a lab could keep its base model within the agreed limits and still ship a far more capable product. Coordination has already run into the law—antitrust law. After Anthropic’s CEO, Dario Amodei, called for labs to slow down earlier this year, OpenAI’s policy chief, Chris Lehane, said OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google had been talking about safety for weeks. Senator Josh Hawley, a Missouri Republican, said there was “no world” in which he would grant them an antitrust exemption. On September 18, paying consumers of ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, and Gemini sued the four companies behind them, alleging an illegal agreement to slow AI development. A shared perplexity floor could invite that same kind of reaction. Perverse incentives But the biggest barrier—and the elephant in the room that makes all this seem performative—is the massive economic incentive to speed ahead with AI model development. All the major AI labs are under pressure to continue driving up revenues to offset the massive capital expenditures needed to develop and operate frontier models. By this time next year, all the major AI labs could be public companies, their stock prices subject to the revenues they report every quarter. The best way for the labs to keep revenues growing is to hold on to their existing customers and attract new ones. Since the pool of customers is finite, the major labs must compete for those customers. The best way to do that is by releasing ever more capable models as rapidly as possible. So increasing model capability is rewarded, and safety research is seen as a cost of doing business. The only way slowing down makes sense is if all AI labs participate. Perhaps a government entity could help the AI labs agree on their measurements and slowdown terms. Amodei explicitly pointed to the Federal Aviation Administration as a model for AI regulation and government oversight that includes third-party evaluations of powerful models. This is unlikely, at least during the next two years, because the Trump administration’s alliance with the tech industry is built on a commitment to leave the AI labs to regulate themselves. The administration has even tried several times to preempt states from regulating AI. One of the main arguments for leaving the AI industry unregulated is the narrative that slowing down AI R&D is bad for the U.S. because it would open the door for China to take the lead. China, then, would be the country dictating technological standards to the world and perhaps using its AI dominance in military applications as leverage against other countries. Still, Trump said Friday that the U.S. would work with China on “joint guardrails” for AI, Semafor reported. Give Pachocki credit for doing something new. Through his essay, OpenAI showed competitors where it stands in a way no other lab has done. Credit Microsoft for showing that labs can compare base models on a shared test. If the other major labs follow suit, it would be a major step toward the industry’s working together to pace AI development and ensure adequate safety standards.
