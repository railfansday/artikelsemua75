Industry observers note that there is a striking gap in debates about artificial intelligence. Policy bodies and frontier AI laboratories are borrowing the vocabulary of legal constitutions to guide model development and oversight. However, these statements at the moment don’t include the institutional safeguards that would make such value commitments work.

In September, the UK Parliament’s Joint Committee on Human Rights published its report on AI regulation. It uses ‘human rights’ more than 100 times in as many pages, but doesn’t discuss which rights should be protected or how. AI firm Anthropic in San Francisco, California, has written a ‘constitution’ for its model Claude. In it, numerous values are listed as the tool’s priorities, including the preservation of user autonomy. Yet some of these values, such as autonomy and well-being, compete with each other, as Anthropic acknowledges. As a scholar of constitutional law, I see parallels between the current debate on AI governance and how national constitutions have been crafted, broken, borrowed and fixed.

In a recent development, countries’ constitutions have distinct underlying priorities affecting their design. These fall roughly into two historical approaches (C. Skach Int. J. Const. Law 7, 175–181; 2009). One takes liberty as its priority: describing what people are free to do and what the state should not do to interfere. The other focuses on human dignity: noting not what individuals may do, but what cannot be done to them, regardless of the circumstances or whether they have consented to it.

Germany’s post-war constitutionalism demonstrates the latter. Its founding document, or basic law, was designed in 1949 to put dignity first and foremost — as both the highest guiding value and a constitutional right, the violation of which is actionable in court. It established an independent federal constitutional court, which citizens can approach directly, that became operational two years later. The choice of this value and the legal institutions to protect it were a response to the atrocities of the Nazi regime, in which people became instruments of state objectives.

Frontier AI companies and policymakers should seriously consider using these features of German constitutionalism. When dignity as a constitutional right is violated — by an AI-generated deepfake or by a chatbot that treats a distressed user as just another interaction — the people affected could petition the court directly, and the perpetrator could be stopped and held accountable.
