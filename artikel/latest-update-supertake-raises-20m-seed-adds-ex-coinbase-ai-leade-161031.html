Supertake co-founder Michael Mignano revealed on November 23, 2026 that the AI investing startup had raised a $20 million seed round led by Adjacent, with Lincoln Murr joining as chief executive and John Peterson as chief technology officer.

Murr and Peterson also joined as co-founders. Murr previously led AI product at Coinbase and Peterson led engineering; their work included x402, Coinbase Advisor, Coinbase for Agents and agentic wallets. Union Square Ventures, which incubated the product, participated alongside Coinbase Ventures and angels including Nat Friedman, Nikita Bier, Scott Belsky and Guillermo Rauch. Mignano said Supertake had been introduced a little over a month earlier and that beta customers were investing real money and building followings around their market takes. The announcement supplied no user or asset totals. (Related: 3D Asset Generation: AI for Game Development #3...)

In a recent development, on its vision page, Supertake says a user can enter an investment thesis in everyday language rather than choose securities directly. The product, which calls that thesis a “take,” uses frontier AI agents to construct and manage a strategy that can be shared with and followed by other users. The manufacturer eventually wants to rank ideas by their results and the backing they attract, with successful contributors gaining reputations, audiences and a share of the value created.

Industry observers note that the terms of service provide more mechanical detail. AI can produce lists of equities, cryptocurrencies or other assets related to a submitted idea, and the service may refresh or update those outputs over time. Published portfolios and commentary can appear on public pages, and the service can let users copy, adapt, fund and republish another user’s idea.

Sources indicate that supertake’s public leaderboard reveals how those ideas are packaged, with themes including consulting surviving an AI shakeout and businesses auditing AI agents. It displays percentage returns alongside the takes, but the terms state that published performance is not verified or audited and may reflect simulated Practice Mode results rather than actual trading. Practice Mode uses simulated funds without a brokerage connection; its results do not reflect real execution, fees, taxes, liquidity constraints or other live-trading effects.

Industry observers note that people can connect Robinhood, Coinbase or another supported provider. Depending on the permissions granted, Supertake can obtain account and position information and transmit approved instructions through the provider’s API. It does not hold or custody customer assets and cannot withdraw or transfer them. The connected provider decides whether to accept an instruction; execution and custody remain with that provider, and securities clearing remains with the broker.

In a recent development, real-money investing starts in “Ask me first” mode, which requires approval before each trade instruction is transmitted. Optional Autopilot can determine when to generate buy or sell instructions within an approved strategy and settings, without separate approval of every trade. Settings can include a maximum dollar amount per trade, a maximum weight per holding and a drift alert; users can review or change them and disable Autopilot.

That control stops at transmission. Supertake cannot alter an instruction after it has been sent, and the provider may delay, reject, partially fill or execute it at a price different from the one displayed.

Supertake is invitation-only, with a waitlist and an option to skip the line by connecting a funded brokerage account. The company says it is not presently a broker-dealer or investment adviser and characterizes its role as a technological interface between people and their connected providers. It currently receives no subscription fee, commission or other direct or indirect compensation for its AI-powered investment-idea and portfolio-intelligence features.

According to recent reports, the AI output itself carries broader qualifications. Supertake’s terms caution that probabilistic output may be inaccurate, incomplete, outdated, biased or unreliable, omit relevant assets or include unrelated ones, and produce different results for similar inputs. Supertake does not review the output before showing it to customers and conducts no suitability review. Users remain responsible for assessing strategies, risks and settings, and for deciding whether to authorize individual or automated trades.

Supertake’s meritocracy pitch therefore hinges on more than generating portfolios. Its proposed marketplace needs credible comparisons between takes, while the current service leaves published performance unaudited, AI output unreviewed by the tech giant and judgments about suitability with people.
