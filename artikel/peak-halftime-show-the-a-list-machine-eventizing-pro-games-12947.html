Peak Halftime Show: The A-List Machine Eventizing Pro Games

Nearly 73,000 fans inside Rio De Janeiro’s Maracanã Stadium rocked out to Ricky Martin and Brazilian DJ Pedro Sampaio on Sept. 27, when the duo performed their song “pikito pikito” live for the first time. But they weren’t headlining a concert — they were playing a halftime show.

Industry observers note that the NFL has long held global ambitions, but this is the year it leaned into music to serve as a sort of cultural diplomacy. The Rio game, between the Baltimore Ravens and Dallas Cowboys, may be the best example, combining a global superstar in Martin with a local talent. “There was a lot of preparation and many hours of rehearsals leading up to the performance,” Martin tells The Hollywood Reporter, “but it was one of those experiences where everything came together beautifully.”. He adds, “These are memories I’ll carry with me for a long time.”.

In fact, the NFL has seven halftime demonstrates planned this season, kicking off with the Jonas Brothers in Melbourne on Sept. 10, running through The Warning in Mexico City on Nov. 22. It’s also part of a concerted effort to elevate games into cultural events. Just look at Nov. 25, when U2 will perform during a halftime show for Netflix’s Thanksgiving Eve game at SoFi Stadium between the L.A. Rams and Green Bay Packers. “There’s really this gathering momentum about how we can further eventize some of the parts on our schedule and our calendar,” Hans Schroeder, executive vp and chief media officer of the NFL, told reporters in October. The league is leaning into halftime shows as the sports world overall has been more heavily embracing music programming in games. FIFA for instance, had its first World Cup Final halftime show this summer, with Coldplay’s Chris Martin curating a lineup that included BTS, Madonna and Justin Bieber. The NCAA, meanwhile, tapped EDM act The Chainsmokers to play a “bridge show” between the two Final Four games. The NHL has dug in, too, tapping Benson Boone to headline the first intermission during this year’s Winter Classic on New Year’s Eve.

Industry observers note that “The NFL and all these leagues understand that to grow the game internationally and even domestically for the younger generation, they need to find organic ways to get those eyeballs,” indicates Tyler Stimmel, a booking agent in UTA’s music brand partnerships division. He handled booking on many of the NFL’s international events this year, including Jonas Brothers, Burna Boy in Paris, The Warning and Drew Baldridge in Munich.

It has been revealed that “It all stems from the younger generation,” Stimmel notes, pointing toward a recent USC Annenberg study that suggested nearly 80 percent of Gen Z respondents in a survey suggested they’d go to a sporting event because of concerts and other brand activations rather than because of the game itself. It’s beneficial to the talent, too, with international acts getting a shot to play before a wide swath of stateside NFL fans who otherwise likely wouldn’t have been exposed to their music. The agent adds, “We live in a highly saturated pop culture ecosystem the NFL wants more ways to cut through. “Music is the most natural way because we already know where to put the show, and you can pick a booking to tap into a version of culture that people within the region can easily relate to.”.

In the U.S., that means working with such partners as Netflix to create more event-sized moments, like having U2 perform. “Netflix has leaned in because they see the success of Super Bowl and the Super Bowl halftime show, and it draws more eyes and it brings more attention and it brings more people, and not necessarily all avid football fans,” says Jon Barker, senior VP of global event production and operations for the NFL. “Now we’re tapping into casual fans, now we’re tapping into new fans, and it’s just one more element that opens another door for somebody to walk into the ecosystem of football and participate.”.

It has been revealed that unlike the Super Bowl, which is booked by Jay-Z’s Roc Nation (next year’s headliner is Jay-Z himself), the league books its other halftime shows itself, though Barker notes that there is close coordination with its media rights partners. “It gets a little complicated, to be honest with you, in trying to find the right balance,” Barker says of the booking process. “Who is touring and is available? Who can put on a show that can fill a stadium? Who will translate well and do well on television? Who is the right act for the audience that’s inside this area, and who is the right act that will attract new fans that will be interested in what we’re doing?”.

Stimmel, for his part, describes the process as sophisticated, saying the NFL has heavily relied on its regional offices throughout the world, identifying artists who are a major draw in each market but who can pull viewers from several verticals. “Burna Boy in Paris in particular is one that checks off a lot of those boxes,” he says. “He was one of the first Nigerian artists to sell out Stade de France, so we know he is popular there, but he can draw from Africa, North America.”.

While the increased bookings may bring in more younger viewers, they may not always be a popular offering to older fans, who might prefer to see just a traditional halftime report with pundits rather than a concert. Stimmel acknowledges that critique but adds that “they want this to appeal to everyone, but the leagues are recognizing that in order to sustain the sport, it has to appeal to younger fans.”.

NFL exec Barker indicates the league isn’t done expanding its halftime ambitions, noting that while “there’s probably a limit and the right balance, I don’t think we’re there yet,” even if it maintains the Super Bowl as the ultimate showcase.

From the league’s point of view, the performances are ultimately about growing the game — and reasserting the league as the most culturally relevant piece of American pop culture. “The way that we think about music is the same way we think about art, the same way we think about fashion,” notes Barker, adding that the more ways “we can open to bring somebody in and show them the sport, the better off we all are.”.
