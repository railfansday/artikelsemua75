6 post-earthquake checks every Californian can finish before the next quake

Six checks for Californians after this week's quakes, from emergency kits and phone alerts to insurance, and why a quick version is not enough.

When the shaking stops, most Californians feel relief first. Then they start to wonder whether they would be ready for a worse quake. The quake centered in Mendocino County, Calif., this week was a magnitude 5.6, according to Bay City News, and it brought that question back for households across Northern California. For the many households that came through it unhurt, the days after a scare are a rare stretch when preparing feels urgent and the next quake has not yet come. The risk behind the scare comes with living in California, where the danger of earthquakes is greater than in any other state except Alaska, according to CNBC Select. This week's quake is part of the ordinary, lasting risk of life in the state, not a rare event. (Related: Leading House Democrat targets Trump’s inner circle for...)

Most advice after a quake sounds like a quick errand: switch on a phone setting, buy batteries, glance at an insurance policy. A household can tick those boxes in five minutes without becoming any safer than it was before. The task this week is to test the basics against a real scare, not to collect new tips. Sources indicate that nacionale set out to lay out the preparedness steps residents should take after the recent California quakes, from insurance coverage to the bookshelves in a bedroom and the settings on a phone. Each one is easy to start and just as easy to leave half-done. Every step costs less and causes less stress to settle now, while the house is still standing, than in the hours after a bigger quake.

Industry observers note that most households tick off the emergency kit without opening it, because buying it once feels like finishing it. A kit needs refilling, and a fresh scare is the closest thing to a refill date most households will get. Supplies such as water and lights should be replenished while the quake is still on people’s minds, according to Nacionale. A real check means opening the box and replacing what is used up or expired.

According to recent reports, the refill covers the most basic needs: water to drink, and flashlights and batteries to see by. The CDC’s checklist covers natural disasters in general, so fitting it to a quake is up to each household.

Decide how many people the kit must cover and for how long, then stock it to that, rather than stopping at a closet with a flashlight in it.

According to recent reports, after a quake, the useful walk through the house looks for what could have landed on a person, not for what broke. The hazard is ordinary furniture that already stands in most living rooms and bedrooms. Nacionale advises residents in areas near active fault zones, such as the San Andreas Fault, to secure their bookshelves and wall art. Nearly every home has these pieces, and that makes the step easy to overlook. A person sitting on a couch or sleeping in a bed beside an unanchored bookcase is at greater risk than the books on its shelves.

Industry observers note that A shelf that stayed upright this week has passed no test, because the shaking may not be over. The CDC warns that aftershocks, not only the main quake, can shift household objects, knock them down or smash them. Seeing nothing fall feels like a pass, but it shows luck, not readiness. The days of aftershocks are when securing these items does the most good, and the job should not wait for a quieter week.

Brackets and anchors are only half of the fix, and placement is the other half. Bulky and heavy items belong on the bottom shelves, according to the CDC, so the top row of every bookcase is the first thing to rearrange. Start in the rooms where people sleep and sit. Heavy art hung over a bed or couch hangs directly over a person and should come down first.

The check is finished when nothing heavy remains high up or overhead where people rest, however tidy the room looks.

It has been revealed that A phone alert matters most when everyone in the house is asleep. Nacionale advises making sure alerts are enabled ahead of early-morning quakes, such as one at 3:30 a.m. This week’s quake struck at 8:10 a.m. Wednesday, after most households were already up, according to Bay City News. The test that counts is whether an alert would wake the household.

In a recent development, switching alerts on is not a single step, because warnings are sent through several separate channels. Bay City News reports that iOS, Android, FEMA's wireless alerts and the MyShake app each carry warnings, and that different phone operating systems send alerts at different thresholds. Two neighbors with different phones who both turned alerts on may not get the same warning from the same quake. Whether a warning gets through depends on the phone's setup, not only on the switch. "A lot of it has to do with what you have on your phone, how you have it set up,” mentioned Robert de Groot, a scientist at the U.S. Geological Survey's Earthquake Science Center in Pasadena, Calif. MyShake is free, so cost is no reason to rely on one channel.

Recent findings suggest that an alert by itself does not mean the danger is close. Well over 1 million people in Northern California received ShakeAlert notifications this week, including residents of Oakland, Berkeley and Concord, according to Bay City News. The first alert is issued from about one second of data, yet de Groot indicates people very near the source will likely notice the shaking before any warning comes. At 3:30 a.m., an alert by the bed is a cue for people to protect themselves where they lie, not a promise of time to spare.

The check worth doing tonight is whether everyone in the house would wake to a phone alert, and whether they would know what to do in the seconds after.

In a recent development, the most urgent danger in a house can begin after the shaking stops, in pipes behind walls and under floors where nobody can see it. Nacionale advises that residents locate the main valves beforehand so they can act quickly if a bigger aftershock arrives. The need does not depend on a second quake. Leaking gas and water pipes are among the dangers that can remain once an earthquake is over, according to the CDC, so the shut-off is a response to damage that may already exist.

Most households stop once they have found the valves. Acting swiftly at a leaking line also takes knowing how to close each one. The calm days after a moderate scare, with nothing leaking, are the right time for every adult in the house to learn the steps for each main valve.

If a line breaks, the minutes that matter are better spent turning a valve than working out how to.

A family communication initiative covers the hours after a strong quake when relatives can’t simply call one another. Loved ones need an agreed place to gather in case phone service fails, so each person knows where to go without calling anyone. Picking the spot takes a short conversation, and it’s easy to treat that as the whole job.

A single meeting place assumes every family member can physically get there, and after a strong quake that may not be possible. A family therefore has to plan for two separate problems: losing phone service and losing access to the meeting place. The CDC recommends choosing an out-of-area relative whom family members can reach if they can't get to their reunion location, and it puts that relative at least 100 miles away. Someone that far from the quake is outside the local disruption. That person can take word from each family member and pass it along, and nobody has to search a damaged neighborhood for anyone else. A household with only a meetup spot has solved the first problem and left the second one open.

In a recent development, the plan has to work for people who can't use their phones. A plan saved only on a phone is lost when the phone dies or goes missing, and writing the meeting spot and the relative's number on paper keeps both within reach. Every member of the household, including children, should know both answers ahead of time. Nobody can explain the plan to a family member they can't reach.

With a place to meet and a person to call, a family has a proposal that still works when the first answer fails.

A policy review looks like the easiest check on this list, because it involves reading a document rather than installing anything. Nacionale urges households to review their quake coverage now, ahead of any large disaster. That timing matters because the protection a household assumes it has may not exist. Standard homeowners and renters insurance both exclude earthquake damage, according to CNBC Select. A real review means confirming whether the household holds a separate earthquake policy.

In a recent development, fixing that gap is a budget decision, and high home values in California make it a heavy one. Coverage is priced on the value of a home, and the median California home costs just over $900,000, CNBC Select reports, which means a typical owner has a great deal of house to insure. By its rough estimate, a policy for a median-priced home could cost $4,500 to $9,000 a year. A household has to weigh that expense against the risk of paying for quake repairs on its own. Even with a policy, an owner pays a large amount before any claim pays out. Quake deductibles are typically set at 10% to 25% of dwelling coverage, a share of the home's insured value rather than a flat fee. A homeowner can't skip the review, but buying a policy remains a judgment call, and the goal is to make that choice deliberately rather than by default.

It has been revealed that households that already carry quake coverage need to check what their policy leaves out. Fires, landslides, flooding and tsunamis caused by an earthquake are typically excluded from earthquake policies, according to CNBC Select, so an owner can be covered for the shaking and still be uninsured for the damage that comes after it.

A review is finished only when the household knows plainly what is covered and what is not, and that is the difference between holding a policy and being ready.
