Tech Report: Shrek 2 Remastered Fails To Capture The Magic Of Its Source Material

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When I first heard the announcement for Shrek 2 Remastered, I couldn’t wait to get my hands on it. As I’m sure many of you can relate to, movie tie-in games were a major part of my childhood. While the rest of my family rented their favorite films from Blockbuster, I always made a beeline for the games section, where I eventually discovered one of the best licensed games from the early 2000s, Shrek 2.

In a recent development, to this day, I’m still a major sucker for licensed games, so revisiting a modern version of a classic wasn’t even a question. Now that I’ve played it, however, I’m left wondering if a PS5 port of the original would’ve been a better option. While this is a remaster, not a full remake, playing it back-to-back with The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) has been a jarring and eye-opening experience that highlights the right and wrong ways to bring back a beloved game from the past. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

While there are plenty of games that benefit from the traditional remaster treatment of updating visuals while leaving gameplay mostly unchanged, Shrek 2 likely would’ve been better off with the reverse.

Industry observers note that despite their age, Shrek 2’s original graphics had their own charm, whereas improvements to character responsiveness could’ve transformed the game into a great experience for both new and returning players. Instead, Shrek 2 Remastered retains the same frustrating controls while the visuals have become something unsettling at best and downright nightmarish at worst. (Related: Industry News: Judge blocks construction of Trump’s arc...)

Little Red, also known as Lil’ Red, was almost entirely left out of the marketing for Shrek 2 Remastered, to the point that many believed she’d been removed entirely. Now it’s a bit clearer why that decision might’ve been made. Most of the characters have a disturbing look to them now, but Lil’ Red’s makeover is certainly the most terrifying.

Characters' softer features and movie-accurate appearances have been replaced by lifeless eyes, intense contrast, and enhanced details, leaving them almost unrecognizable compared to their appearances in the 2004 release and the movie it’s based on. I'm also left wondering why so much effort was put into redesigning characters when the cutscenes haven't been formatted to fill the screen and are often cut off before they're finished.

Though the visuals were certainly disappointing, I might’ve been able to overlook them if they'd been accompanied by upcoming gameplay, but that remains unchanged. Most stages feature four characters that a solo player or a group of players can switch between to complete the required tasks.

Recent findings suggest that nearly every character is needed over the course of a single level due to their unique abilities, preventing one player from steamrolling others through the game. However, it also means solo players will be forced to use characters with less favorable controls.

Aside from Lil’ Red, who is actually somewhat enjoyable to play, thanks to her ranged apple throws and more fluid movements, most characters have limited attack ranges and feel stiff and unresponsive. Additionally, you’ll likely find yourself swapping characters more often than necessary because trailing characters (usually Shrek) frequently get stuck on obstacles and can’t free themselves.

Perhaps the one thing still saving this remaster from being a complete disappointment for me is that I’m playing local co-op with my partner, which has been fun simply because the game is so unforgiving. On more than one occasion, we’ve had to repeat a level multiple times to the point of delirious laughter. It’s not difficult, per se, but the lack of lives and poor controls make it brutal nonetheless.

In a recent development, unfortunately, all the aforementioned issues make it difficult to imagine upcoming players getting much enjoyment out of this remaster. Some older fans may be able to overlook the subpar gameplay in favor of nostalgia and the chance to play the game again, but I am finding it difficult. I’m grateful to have the opportunity to experience it on modern consoles with my partner, but that’s where my enjoyment ends.

It has been revealed that if you’re a fresh player curious about the hype surrounding the original, this remaster isn’t the way to discover it, and I’d recommend skipping it. On the other hand, if you’re a returning player eager to revisit the game and have no way to play the 2004 version, this will scratch the itch. If you do opt to play, however, I’d insist on playing multiplayer. Shrek 2 has always been better as a shared experience, but that’s especially true now in Shrek 2 Remastered.

Players do need to sign in to their Epic account to access multiplayer, which has been a major point of frustration for those playing on Steam in particular, so keep that in mind.

Overall, I’m walking away from Shrek 2 Remastered heartbroken. Had it received the full remake treatment with refined mechanics and a visual overhaul that enhanced rather than stripped away its original charm, this could have been an incredible experience for everyone to enjoy together. As it stands right now, however, I’m just left wishing I had a copy of the original game.
