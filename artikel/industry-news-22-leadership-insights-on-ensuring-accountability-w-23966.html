According to recent reports, accountability has always been crucial, and more so now that artificial intelligence is in the mix. The term can come with negative connotations: If something goes wrong, it means whoever is accountable is to blame. Fearing accountability, some team members may hesitate to take roles that require much of it. Even if they step up, fear can keep them from doing their best work or taking needed risks. We asked members of the Fast Company Impact Council how they ensure accountability without creating fear. Below, 22 of them share their best advice on keeping accountability as a necessary and positive role instead of something people dread. 1. TREAT MISSES AS INFORMATION Be as clear about expectations up front as you are about results afterward. Fear comes from ambiguity: People don’t know what success looks like until they’ve missed it. I set clear owners and outcomes at the start, then treat misses as information. What did we learn, and what do we change? I also name my own misses out loud, before anyone else’s. When the leader models it, accountability stops feeling like judgment and becomes a shared standard the whole team owns. — Megan Noel, Covista 2. MODEL SELF-REFLECTION Accountability is less likely to create fear when leaders hold themselves accountable to the same standards and deadlines they expect of others, are honest about where they fell short, and share the lessons they learned. The more leaders model that kind of self-reflection, the more comfortable others will feel doing the same—and helping their teammates build that skill. — Grace Keith Rodriguez, Caliber Corporate Advisers 3. FOCUS ON LESSONS LEARNED Accountability should give people the confidence to take big swings. When it only indicates up for the misses and mistakes, you create a culture of fear, which doesn’t inspire creativity or big thinking. Instead, be clear about expectations, set ambitious goals together, and when something falls short, focus on the lesson learned and move on. — Max Friedman, Givebutter 4. LEAD WITH CURIOSITY RATHER THAN BLAME Accountability without fear begins with clarity—clear expectations and realistic goals—balanced with my personal commitment to being accessible and providing timely feedback. This shared accountability builds trust as the same standards apply at both levels. When challenges arise, I am able to lead with curiosity rather than blame, asking “What happened, and what support do you need?” which creates space for my team to surface issues early and treat setbacks as learning opportunities. Consistency reinforces this sense of safety, so instead of fearing accountability, my team feels supported and invested in our shared success. — Maurice Twomey, Roto 5. ENSURE REAL ALIGNMENT FROM THE START Many tend to think of accountability as something you create at the end of a process. Someone didn’t do what they were supposed to do. Will you hold them accountable or not? In my experience, however, accountability issues are often alignment issues in disguise. What are we trying to do and why? By when? What are we both assuming? What is really feasible? Good leaders ensure there is real alignment around these questions at the start of a process. They approach accountability not as a punishment question but as a clarity question—and in doing so, they debunk the idea that accountability and fear must come hand-in-hand. — Maril MacDonald, Gagen MacDonald 6. OWN THE OUTDATED DASHBOARD Accountability isn’t perfection. A team member flagged outdated targets on a dashboard I was presenting. I owned it and thanked them. Small moments like that set the tone for the big ones. When we miss a real target, we own our work and our choices and solve it as a team. Do that consistently, and you build that muscle for the harder moments. — Michele Walsh, UNICEF USA 7. SHIFT THE MINDSET When leaders shift their mindset from “don’t break this” to “let’s try this,” accountability becomes about outcomes and learning. As leaders, we need to role model what strong accountability looks like and acknowledge fear with grace and empathy. This means we should understand that some good ideas won’t stick, and we should celebrate innovative thinking even when it doesn’t result in a win. When people see their leader take a smart risk, own what follows, and adapt promptly, they do the same. — Jacqui Canney, ServiceNow 8. CUT THE ENDLESS APPROVAL CHAINS If you want real accountability, let team members own the outcome and cut the endless approval chains. Nobody likes to fail, but you can build a culture of permissible failure and actually walk the talk. When mistakes happen, address them head-on, take the lesson, and move on fast. — Khozema Shipchandler, Twilio 9. ASK WHAT HAPPENED, NOT WHO DID IT Make the destination crystal clear and the path flexible. Fear shows up when people don’t know what “good” looks like until they miss it. I’d rather over-communicate the outcome, agree on ownership out loud, and let people choose how they get there. When something goes sideways, my first question is “What happened?” not “Who did this?” Accountability is a promise teammates make to each other, not a punishment waiting to happen. — Colby Proffitt, Seekr 10. MAKE ACCOUNTABILITY A WEEKLY RHYTHM Accountability works when colleagues do the flagging, not the leader. Every week, especially in our executive meetings, we review our trackers top to bottom and call out anything blocked or at risk, in the open, in front of the team. It’s rare that someone gets blindsided in a one-on-one weeks later. Fear creeps in when accountability feels personal or arbitrary. A visible, weekly rhythm makes it structural instead, and that is what keeps people honest without making them defensive. Consistent communication is key. Beyond email and Slack, good ole phone calls and texts are quite effective. — Cody Barbo, Trust & Will 11. TEACH CLEAN ESCALATION For our team, it all starts with providing clear communication so people understand what’s expected and how success will be measured. We approach conversations from a point of kindness and empathy, which includes listening and practicing radical candor so everyone is provided honest, respectful feedback and knows where they stand. Teaching clean escalation and other techniques also plays a role in maximizing accountability while preserving the best possible team dynamics. When people know they can raise issues early and get help addressing them, taking responsibility becomes much easier. — Leerom Segal, Klick Health 12. DON’T LEAVE PEOPLE GUESSING Clarity, transparency, and continuous communication. That is the foundation—and frankly, it is the ethos on which we built our company. When people know exactly what is expected, where they stand, and that honest feedback flows early and often, there is nothing to fear. Fear creeps in when people are left guessing. The goal is shared accountability. Not imposed from the top down but owned collectively by a team that feels genuinely empowered. When that culture exists, people hold each other to a higher standard because they want to—not because they have to. — Meredith Rosenberg, NU Advisory Partners 13. THE KIND OF FEAR MATTERS Fear isn’t always bad. Fearing your boss is different from fearing you didn’t do your best work. Top-down fear can produce compliance, but rarely excellence. I want my team accountable to themselves first, because people only do their best work on things they care about. That means clear quarterly and annual goals, professional development funds for growth areas, honest conversations about where they want to go, and a real grasp of why our work matters and why their contribution is key. People who hold themselves to a standard outperform people who only answer to a boss. — Muneer Panjwani, Engage for Good | The Halo Awards 14. ATTACH A HUMAN OWNER TO AI-RELATED DECISIONS I would argue that it is the lack of accountability that creates fear. Accountability should remove ambiguity. Each artificial intelligence-related decision should have a human owner. There are clear rules of engagement that establish what is and is not in the bounds of permissibility. And when mistakes are made, they are inspected and not concealed. Fear breeds in darkness: Create light. — Scott Brighton, Bonterra 15. HAVE EMPLOYEES SET THE BAR FOR THEMSELVES Accountability is a matter of doing what you commit to. Our team members have some flexibility to set expectations regarding the timing and scope of their deliveries. Publicly demonstrating finished products in team or client meetings improves accountability. Fear is effectively removed when trying to clear bars we set for ourselves. — Brad Weber, InspiringApps 16. SHOW VULNERABILITY Model that it’s ok to own up to a mistake. We’re only human and no one is perfect. Ultimately that leads to greater accountability because there is an honesty at the heart of it. It removes the bullshit and gives folks the support they need to own their accomplishments, warts and all. — Helen Andrews, Johannes Leonardo 17. SET CLEAR EXPECTATIONS Every employee plays a role in the company’s overall success, but depending on their level or access to senior leadership, they may not see how their contributions impact the greater whole. What is crucial to the CEO? What are the growth ambitions of the company? How does each individual’s responsibilities ladder up to the organization’s goals? It is essential to set clear expectations for each person, provide honest feedback, and communicate transparently about how daily activities and individual performance are linked to company success. — Celia Jones, FINN Partners 18. WRITE IT DOWN Accountability holds when the measure is agreed before the work starts. Clarity is kindness, as they say. Set it together, write it down, and review it on a fixed schedule so a missed target shows up early and gets treated as information. People get defensive when the standard appears after the result, or when it shifts depending on who’s asking. Other than that, I try to give real guidelines and real feedback. If you start off that way, it instills the proper expectation of communication. — James Chester, WVN 19. DISCUSS HOW TO CLOSE THE PERFORMANCE-EXPECTATION GAP I try to set a high bar of excellence by sharing great work, and by praising and rewarding excellent work. That way, when someone isn’t delivering, we can point to the gap between their performance and the expectation and talk about how to close the gap. It’s designed to create a supportive, safe, and fair environment. No finger pointing and passive-aggressiveness allowed! — Phillip Haid, Public Inc. 20. TAKE AN ASPIRATIONAL APPROACH I’ve learned that empathy and accountability aren’t opposites. A few years ago, I leaned too far into being accommodating, and expectations slipped. I used to frame performance gaps as “We’re not where we need to be,” which I think created fear. Through coaching, I learned to take a more aspirational approach: “This is what excellence looks like. How can we get there?” It shifts the focus from fear to problem-solving. When people care deeply about the mission and understand what’s expected of them, accountability becomes a shared responsibility rather than something driven by fear. — Felicia Curcuru, Binti 21. REPLACE PUNITIVE REACTIONS WITH CURIOSITY AND COLLABORATION Having experienced the damage of fear-based leadership early in my career, I know criticism and punishment only breed toxicity and burnout. Accountability without fear starts with connecting our work to purpose. At a mission-based organization, holding ourselves accountable is directly tied to achieving our mission. When performance issues arise, leaders should replace punitive reactions with curiosity and collaboration. Approaching staff with two-way dialogue to uncover blockers, rather than one-way scrutiny, turns accountability into a partnership that helps everyone succeed. — Jaymes Black, The Trevor Project 22. SEPARATE THE PERSON FROM THE OUTCOME Accountability works best when expectations are clear before something goes wrong. I try to separate the person from the outcome, so the conversation becomes about what happened, what we learned, and what needs to change. Fear makes people hide problems. Clarity makes them easier to solve. — George Kailas, Prospero.ai.
