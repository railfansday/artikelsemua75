How Escambia County Uses Miovision’s AI to Investigate Traffic Signal Complaints

Sources indicate that escambia County, Florida, is using Miovision’s AI Studio to help investigate complaints about traffic signals. In results released November 23, Miovision stated the county could pull an initial analysis together in about five minutes, down from 30 to 45 minutes under its previous workflow. The improvement was in investigation time, not the number of complaints or the amount of time drivers spent waiting at red lights.

Sources indicate that the release follows the introduction of Mateo, Miovision’s traffic engineering assistant, in April. The newer results show what one traffic team is doing with it in daily operations. They also illustrate why access to the right data is more important here than an AI model’s ability to give a confident-sounding answer. Sources indicate that according to Miovision, county staff previously had to pull signal controller records and compare timing intends and traffic volumes by hand. They now use AI Studio to ask for a view that brings those records together. The AI Studio product page describes how a natural-language request can be turned into an interactive workspace that draws on sources including signal performance data and telemetry. That is useful when an engineer needs to compare the same junction over multiple hours or find out whether a pattern appears across several locations. (Related: How to generate text: using different decoding methods ...)

It has been revealed that one example is a split failure: vehicles are still waiting when their green phase ends. Miovision’s traffic performance software monitors this alongside measures such as arrivals on green. Escambia already used this kind of data. A separate case study of a corridor project in the county describes how existing controllers and field detectors supplied timing and detection records for earlier before-and-after analyses. In the upcoming workflow, the assistant helps assemble and compare the records; the traffic engineer still decides what the patterns mean.

According to recent reports, that review matters because a split failure can have different explanations. A recurring queue during an expected peak, an unusual surge and a detector that is not reporting reliably can all warrant different responses. An initial chart gives staff a place to look, but it is not a basis on its own for changing a signal blueprint. A five-minute investigation also does not mean the underlying problem was fixed in five minutes.

Sources indicate that agencies evaluating a similar workflow can separate three measures: time to gather the records, time to reach a decision and time to resolve the complaint. A tool that improves the first step could be worthwhile even if a field visit or an equipment replacement still determines how long residents wait for the concern to be fixed.

According to recent reports, the county also used AI Studio after a hurricane to prioritize checks of 343 network cameras across five partner agencies, Miovision stated. Staff built a tool that compared pre-storm pictures with post-storm views and paired the comparison with the cameras’ last communications. That helped separate views that had moved from cameras that were no longer reporting and gave maintenance teams lists to verify and act on. The 343 figure refers to the cameras audited, not the number destroyed or successfully repaired by AI.

Miovision reported roughly a week of manual inspection time avoided and nearly full camera functionality restored within 30 days. Those are vendor-reported results from one local deployment, not a controlled comparison. They do point to a practical use for AI in a public agency: make the relevant evidence easier to retrieve and review, while keeping decisions about signal timing and field repairs with the people responsible for the network.
