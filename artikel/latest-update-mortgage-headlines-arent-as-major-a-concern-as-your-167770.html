Latest Update: Mortgage headlines aren’t as major a concern as your cost per closing

If you run a real estate team, several weeks ago you probably saw and heard the chatter about potential rate hikes as high as 9% in 2027.

I understand why it spread. Freddie Mac had the 30-year fixed at 7.28% on Oct. 1, up from 6.01% in February and the Fed raised rates in September for the first time since 2023. If everything moves the wrong way at once, 9% could sound possible.

Industry observers note that the Real Estate Agent Facebook groups were bubbling about this in the comments. It is likely causing some anxiety for many agents. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

It has been revealed that it helps to look at what it would actually take. Cotality’s chief economist, Selma Hepp, raised 9% on CNBC as a worst case, not a forecast. HousingWire’s Logan Mohtashami laid out the math: the 10-year would have to push past 6%, and mortgage spreads would have to widen well beyond the roughly 2% they sit at today. That requires a hot economy, oil prices that stay high and a Fed that keeps hiking past what markets expect, all lasting well into next year. September’s jobs report showed 29,000 latest jobs, which already works against the hot-economy part.

Sources indicate that so I wouldn’t blueprint around 9%. I also wouldn’t plan around rates falling back to 6% anytime soon. The Fed’s own projections show no cuts in 2027, and the Mortgage Bankers Association expects rates to stay close to where they are.

In a recent development, A crash needs forced sellers, and we don’t have many. Homeowners with a mortgage carry an average loan-to-value of about 44%, and only around 2% are underwater, according to Cotality. That isn’t 2008.

According to recent reports, what we do have is fewer deals. In early October, pending sales were about 10% less than the year before, and purchase applications were down 14%. Those applications turn into closings 30 to 90 days later, so a lot of teams have been looking at a softer first quarter. At today’s rate, the payment that covered a $400,000 loan in February covers about $357,000 now. Same buyer, same budget, about 11% less loan.

Prices mostly hold, and the deal count shrinks. I think of 2027 as a consolidation year rather than a crash year.

In August, I wrote here that cost-per-lead is the wrong number to run a team on, and that cost-per-closed deal is the one that tells you the truth. A slow year is when that stops being a philosophy and turns into a real potential problem.

Most of what a team spends to generate business is fixed. The ad campaign, vendor contracts, the ISA hours and the coaching all get billed whether deals close or not. When conversion falls, none of those invoices get smaller. (Related: Latest Update: How to generate text: using different de...)

In a recent development, say a team spends $6,000 a month on lead generation and closes 12 deals a quarter from it. That’s $1,500 per closing. Then buyer conversion slips, and the same spend produces nine closings. Now each closing costs $2,000. Nothing on the invoice changed, and every deal got a third more expensive.

That’s the exposure I’d worry about: a cost structure built for a 6.5% market, running in a 7.5% one.

The gap between a team that knows its number and one that doesn’t gets much wider when volume drops.

You can’t control the 10-year Treasury. These are the things you can control though:.

Find your cost per closing by source. Add up everything you spend on each lead source, including ad spend, platform and referral fees, ISA time and software, and divide it by the closed transactions that source produced over the last 12 months. Use closings, not appointments or signed agreements. Most team leads I talk to have never broken this out by source, and the answer usually surprises them.

According to recent reports, stress-test it at a lower deal count. Run the same numbers assuming 20% fewer closings. Any source that ends up costing more per closing than your net is one to fix or cut now, while you still have room to choose.

According to recent reports, shift your mix toward sellers and your sphere. Buyers are the first to stall when rates jump. Sellers still have to move for newly introduced jobs, divorces, estates, and growing families, whatever the rate happens to be, and your past clients are sitting on real equity. If most of your pipeline is buyer leads, 2027 will hurt more than it should.

In a recent development, lengthen your nurture instead of cutting it. The buyer who is priced out at 7.5% may be ready at 6.75%. If your follow-up ends at 90 days, someone else gets that deal if rates turn, and you end up paying for the same lead twice.

In a recent development, give your agents training so they can handle these clients. Don’t promise rates will fall. Understand the concern first, then help the buyer evaluate actual numbers without treating a forecast as a fact.

2023 had the fewest existing-home sales since 1995. The teams I watched hold up through it weren’t the ones that guessed right about rates. They were the ones that knew what each closing cost them and kept moving money toward what was working.

There will be another very scary hot topic after this one, and another after that. The best hedge against all of them is the same: know your cost per closing, and build a pipeline that doesn’t depend on buyers feeling good about rates.

Robyn Thompson DeSantos is the founder and CEO of Ascension Group, a business solutions and management company for real estate team leaders and brokers.
