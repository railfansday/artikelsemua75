Retired FBI Supervisory Special Agent Jason Pack breaks down what sits inside America’s law enforcement computers, how hackers get in and how to protect your data.

A retired FBI supervisory special agent warned Saturday that a cyber intrusion into the bureau's National Crime Information Center (NCIC) could endanger police officers during traffic stops if hackers altered records inside the database. Jason Pack, a former FBI supervisory special agent who worked in cybercrime and public information, told Fox News Digital that the bureau's incident response team must rapidly determine whether data was manipulated inside the system, which feeds criminal records to patrol cars nationwide. "What they worry about is if any of this data has changed, then it might affect officers' safety on the side of the road," Pack said, noting there is no indication yet that any records were altered or that the system is experiencing an outage.

FBI PROBES SECOND CYBERATTACK IN MONTHS AS HACKERS BREACH KEY CRIME DATABASE, SOURCES SAY.

Industry observers note that hackers have targeted the FBI for the second time in recent months. (Kevin Lamarque/Reuters, File).

Industry observers note that three sources familiar with the matter told Fox News that hackers breached the NCIC, marking the second cyberattack against the bureau in months. The FBI is investigating how the intruders gained entry and whether any information was compromised.

Sources indicate that in an interview with Fox News' Kayleigh McEnany on Saturday morning, President Donald Trump stated he had "just heard" about the intrusion, adding, "I will say that it’s not good. I don’t like that.".

Pack described the NCIC as "America's computer for law enforcement," explaining that roughly 56 state terminals feed data out to hundreds of thousands of patrol car computers, so officers can check warrants, criminal histories, missing children, gang members and suspected terrorists.

While the database does not hold classified intelligence or sensitive FBI investigative case files, Pack mentioned it stores personally identifiable information, including names, dates of birth and Social Security numbers.

Investigators will examine IP logs and metadata to determine whether attackers exploited a compromised password among the system's more than 100,000 users, breached a state terminal or infiltrated through a third-party vendor, Pack said, adding that cyberattacks have accelerated to "machine speed" as criminals increasingly use artificial intelligence to probe for vulnerabilities.

"You have this cat and mouse game where cybersecurity experts are trying to patch the vulnerabilities that they find. They're looking for their own vulnerabilities at the same time, they have to play defense against the criminals who are out there trying to steal information or do harm to our systems. I think we're just seeing now this speed of these intrusions ramp up a lot," Pack said.

The intrusion follows a September cyberattack in which the extortion group ShinyHunters breached an FBI job portal website and claimed to steal personal records belonging to bureau employees.

The FBI did not immediately respond to a request for comment on the NCIC breach.
