Hubble’s edge-on view of spiral galaxy NGC 1827 reveals dark dust lanes, a bright central bulge and blue patches where stars may be forming.

Today’s Image of the Day from Hubble shows a ribbon of rust-colored dust cutting through a bright band of starlight. (Related: Training Design for Text-to-Image Models: Lessons from ...)

The ribbon breaks into dark patches toward the edges, and it takes a moment to recognize what you’re looking at: a spiral galaxy seen from the side. (Related: Latest Update: One Year Since the “DeepSeek Moment”...)

It has been revealed that the galaxy is NGC 1827. From this angle, the arms that would sweep around its center in a face-on view overlap along our line of sight.

What stands out instead is the structure of the disk itself, from its glowing central bulge to the uneven ribbon of dust that runs through it.

NGC 1827 lies about 37 million light-years away in the constellation Columba, also known as the Dove.

Hubble captured this view with its Advanced Camera for Surveys, and NASA released the image on October 16.

A spiral galaxy’s disk is wide and relatively thin. Its arms curve through that disk, carrying stars, gas and dust.

When we see the disk nearly edge-on, much of that material appears in a narrow strip.

The central bulge, which extends above and below the disk, gives the galaxy its thicker middle.

That geometry also makes the dust especially easy to see. Along parts of the disk, we look through enough dust to dim the combined light of the stars behind it.

The resulting dark lane runs in front of the bright bulge, where the difference between the obscured and unobscured regions is easiest to make out.

Follow the dust lane away from the center and you’ll see bright blue patches scattered across the disk. NASA identifies them as places where upcoming stars are likely forming.

In a recent development, hot, massive young stars give off intense blue light. They burn through their fuel promptly by stellar standards, so a concentration of them can point to relatively recent star formation.

In NGC 1827, those blue patches sit among dark clouds, where gas and dust provide the raw material for future generations of stars.

The side view makes it difficult to tell which arm a particular patch belongs to.

A knot of blue light that appears close to the center in the image could sit farther out in the disk, somewhere along the same line of sight.

Hubble reveals where each feature appears in the image, but its true distance from the galaxy’s center is harder to pin down.

Just left of the galaxy’s bright middle, a star stands out with four sharp spikes.

In a recent development, it looks close enough to belong to NGC 1827, but it lies between Earth and the galaxy. Its apparent position is a chance alignment.

Those four spikes offer a clue to another kind of structure, this time inside the telescope. They appear when light from a bright point source bends around the supports that hold Hubble’s secondary mirror in place.

The pattern is especially obvious on this star because so much of its light comes from one tiny point in the image.

In a recent development, other objects sit much farther away. Look into the dark sky beyond the disk and you’ll find small, faint smudges, some of them distant galaxies.

A single frame brings together a foreground star, a nearby spiral and galaxies deeper in the background.

The portrait came from a Hubble program targeting 260 satellite galaxies around 22 hosts similar to the Milky Way.

These smaller galaxies give astronomers a way to test how well models of the universe describe what happens on the scale of individual galaxies and their neighbors.

Computer models can reproduce many of the broad patterns astronomers see across the universe.

The details get harder to match on smaller scales, where the growth of galaxies depends in part on how gas gathers and how stars affect the material around them.

Looking at many satellite systems gives researchers a broader sample for those comparisons.

Hubble’s sharp view is useful because a galaxy’s overall brightness tells only part of its story.

In this image, the narrow dust lane, bright central bulge and scattered blue patches each give a different view of how its material is arranged.

From another vantage point, the spiral arms might be the first thing we’d notice.

From Earth, the most striking feature is the dark streak down the middle, visible because the galaxy’s own stars light it from behind.
