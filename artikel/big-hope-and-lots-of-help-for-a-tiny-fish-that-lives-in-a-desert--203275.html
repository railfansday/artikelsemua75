Big hope and lots of help for a tiny fish that lives in a desert cave

The endangered Devils Hole pupfish rebounded to 137 fish in October 2026, following captive-bred releases and feeding after earthquake damage.

Biologists counted 137 Devils Hole pupfish on Oct. 10, 2026, in the only place the species lives in the wild: a water-filled cavern in the Nevada desert.

The fall 2025 survey had found 69 fish, and the spring 2026 survey found 77, according to the National Park Service (NPS).

According to recent reports, devils Hole is a detached unit of Death Valley National Park. It opens onto an aquifer, and Ash Meadows is the discharge point for a groundwater system that reaches more than 100 miles (160 kilometers) to the northeast.

The cavern is more than 500 feet (152 meters) deep, and its bottom has never been mapped, according to the park’s Devils Hole page. The water stays at 92°F (33°C).

The fish forage and spawn on a shallow rock shelf near the surface, where they feed on algae and diatoms. That shelf, the population’s main feeding and breeding ground, covers about 215 square feet (20 square meters).

It has been revealed that the fish are about 1 to 1.5 inches (2.5 to 3.8 centimeters) long. Males are iridescent blue, while females are olive-green.

In a recent development, the species has been isolated for 10,000 to 20,000 years, longer than any other pupfish in the Death Valley system. Recent findings suggest that scuba divers and surface counters from the NPS and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) survey the fish at the same time. Divers count in the cavern’s depths, while others watch the limestone shelf.

Industry observers note that biologists have used these methods nearly every spring and fall since 1972. Through the mid-1990s, counts averaged about 200 fish in spring and 425 in fall. (Related: Industry News: huggingface_hub v1.0: Five Years of Buil...)

Recent findings suggest that in the early 2000s, spring counts averaged about 90. The number dropped to 35 in spring 2013.

Industry observers note that pupfish numbers naturally rise and fall. The two agencies say continued monitoring, research and habitat management remain essential.

Industry observers note that two earthquakes, one in December and the other in February, set off waves in the cavern’s normally still water. The waves moved algae, organic material and fish eggs off the shelf.

Recent findings suggest that A dive in March 2025 found only 20 or so fish. The official spring 2025 count recorded 38, down from 191 one year earlier.

“The present size at near-record lows deserves our serious attention, and the actions in the strategic proposal, including supplemental feeding and population augmentation, provide the best opportunity for recovery,” Michael Schwemm, a senior fish biologist with the USFWS, remarked in April 2025.

“I’m proud of the work of this interagency team to monitor and protect the Devils Hole pupfish,” Mike Reynolds, superintendent of Death Valley National Park, noted at the time.

Sources indicate that an earthquake struck Panama, nearly 3,000 miles (4,800 kilometers) away, on Oct. 9, 2026, the day before the survey. Biologists first saw signs of stress in the fish, but a later visit found them in healthier condition.

Industry observers note that backup fish live at the Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility, built in 2013 with a Southern Nevada Public Land Management Act grant. Its refuge tank holds about 100,000 gallons (380,000 liters) and includes a replica of the Devils Hole habitat.

Sources indicate that staff collect eggs, aquatic invertebrates and algae from Devils Hole each month with NPS partners. They raise the young fish and add them to the refuge tank as young adults.

Industry observers note that in spring 2025, biologists added captive-raised fish to the wild population for the first time. In all, 72 such fish were released into Devils Hole between March 2025 and February 2026.

Teams also fed the wild fish while the algae recovered, using data from the facility and from NPS monitoring.

“We were fortunate we had a strategic initiative that allowed us to act cautiously but swiftly,” mentioned Kevin Wilson, an aquatic ecologist and Devils Hole program manager at Death Valley National Park, in May 2026. That plan guided the step of “moving the refuge tank fish to Devils Hole to assist with recovery,” he said.

Schwemm said the 2022 plan “was instrumental in ensuring the wild population’s survival.”.

The rebound followed those releases of captive-raised fish and the months of supplemental feeding in Devils Hole.

Still, the pupfish lives only 10 to 14 months on average. Biologists therefore believe most of the fish in the October count hatched and grew up in Devils Hole.

“It’s exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year,” Reynolds said this fall.

“While challenges remain, we’re encouraged by this progress and committed to its long-term survival,” he added.

The species was listed as endangered in 1967. A 1976 court ruling in Cappaert v. United States recognized a water right for Devils Hole, and an injunction limited groundwater pumping to keep the shelf underwater.
