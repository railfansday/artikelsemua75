Two Norwegian energy companies announced that they'd been breached on two consecutive days this week.

Recent findings suggest that on the evening of Nov. 17, Equinor — one of the world's most economically and geopolitically significant oil and gas companies — announced that it had suffered a cyberattack. Hardly a day later, another billion-dollar Norwegian energy manufacturer called Skagerak Energi admitted to the same. The two companies' breach disclosures read remarkably similarly, though there is no evidence yet that the two attacks are connected. Equinor did not respond to a request for comment on this story. Skagerak Energi declined to comment on specifics of the company's incident.

According to recent reports, equinor first publicly reported its cyberattack on the evening of Nov. 17, Central European Time (CET). Its initial investigation suggested that no data had been stolen, and that operations and production would remain uninterrupted. The issue seemed to be limited to internal IT systems. For instance, one employee told Reuters that the manufacturer's email system was down.

Industry observers note that the next morning, the tech giant reported having gained "a clearer understanding of how the attack was carried out and which systems have been affected." Without sharing much, it alluded to the attack not being viral in nature, so there was "no risk" of it spreading. Besides that, it noted there was no evidence employees, partners, or suppliers were impacted.

According to recent reports, despite all of the good signs in its first two updates, Equinor reported on the morning of Nov. 19 that it was still dealing with the attack, with no timetable for when the situation might resolve itself.

"This is yet another high-stakes cyberattack in what seems like weeks of relentless firefighting throughout Europe," claims Anders Storbråten, cybersecurity expert at IDEX Biometrics. "Equinor says there is no indication that data has been stolen, but I wouldn't breathe a sigh of relief just yet. They wouldn't be the first organization to offer early reassurances, only to have to backtrack once details become clearer.".

It has been revealed that even if the breach does turn out to have been limited, he adds, "Energy companies depend on business systems to organize staff, maintenance and supplies. If those stay unavailable, keeping operations running safely gets harder. Production is unaffected, but nobody should need to see the gas stop flowing before taking this seriously.".

Also in its most recent patch, Equinor noted that its attack "does not appear to be specifically targeting Equinor." This was especially notable seeing as, between its Wednesday and Thursday updates, nearby Skagerak Energi disclosed that it too had fallen victim to a cyberattack.

In many ways, the Eastern Norwegian renewable energy and power utility company's press releases mirrored Equinor's. First, on Nov. 18, it reported an attack affecting its IT systems, which didn't impact operations or production. It added that there was no indication that any data had been lost. The next day it reported having obtained a better view of the situation, but provided no additional details.

Though the two companies' breaches remain separate incidents based on public reporting, other Norwegian energy sector companies have been put on high alert. A representative of oil and gas giant Aker BP told the press on Wednesday that it has mobilized its emergency preparedness and strengthened its monitoring, and the hydropower company Statkraft remarked it's also closely monitoring the situation.

According to recent reports, "Every other energy tech giant should be asking: Are we exposed to the same thing?" Storbråten advises. "The danger is treating this as Equinor’s problem until it becomes your own.".

Three weeks before these incidents became public, Norway's National Security Authority (NSM) published an unprecedented warning. For the first time in its history, it declassified its national cyber-risk assessment, warning that Norway faced a "high" cybersecurity risk to businesses. NSM's director stated that it was time to finally share its assessment with the public, "because it is absolutely necessary to strengthen both resilience and the ability to handle cyber operations in Norwegian companies.".

According to recent reports, storbråten says that since the warning, "NSM and DSB [The Norwegian Directorate for Civil Protection] have urged households to prepare for power, water, and communications outages, and, earlier this month, the government called for every municipality to stress-test its security. But people still remain paralyzed and unsure what to do. The guidance is just that: guidance. It's not clear instruction, and thus businesses and civilians alike are in a sort of limbo.".

Amid all this, less than one week before the incidents became public, Norway's Police Security Service (PST) published its own warning regarding the threat of cyber sabotage in Norway. Besides the defense industry and its suppliers, the press release highlighted that "Critical infrastructure can also be affected, for example, objects within the energy sector.".

In a recent development, the country's energy sector has been trying to match its government's action. Earlier this autumn, representatives of Equinor and Aker BP, among other organizations, took part in "Grey Dawn Petroleum" — an oil and gas-themed tabletop exercise jointly arranged by the Norwegian Ocean Industry Authority (Havtil) and KraftCERT, a sector-specific combination Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT), Information Sharing and Analysis Center (ISAC), and Incident Response Team (IRT).

Per Atle Vagle, Equinor's head of cyber defense, characterized the exercise as "good training," which provided him with "new perspectives that we can bring back to our own business.".
