Industry observers note that CGI announced on November 24, 2026, that CGI AI Head Analysis, developed with Helsinki University Hospital and Planmeca, had received class IIb certification under the European Union Medical Device Regulation. The software analyzes head computed tomography (CT) scans for non-traumatic, spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages and marks suspected acute blood for physician review.

According to recent reports, CGI remarked the certification confirms that the system meets national and EU standards for safety, quality and performance, and enables hospitals to deploy it at scale. Sonja Pietilä, who led solution development at CGI, said: “It can now be integrated directly into hospital imaging workflows and decision-making.” Its intended use is diagnostic support in settings such as busy emergency rooms, where physicians want an additional check against overlooking a potentially life-threatening hemorrhage. Several algorithms work in parallel to identify common non-traumatic hemorrhage types, with CGI reporting that those types account for 99% of acute cerebral hemorrhages and that the system identifies a specific bleed with up to 98% accuracy. In the described workflow, a radiologist first interprets the images independently, then receives the AI outputs as support and compares the diagnosis with the system’s results. The software therefore supplies a second assessment rather than taking over the diagnosis.

Development and clinical testing used images from different hospital imaging devices. Several hospitals independently tested and validated the solution, including a large Swiss university hospital. The project’s responsible AI framework also addresses privacy and security risks in the data, operating environment and data movement from diagnostic imaging to analysis, with experts involved from design through output interpretation. Industry observers note that A Scientific Reports study published on August 15, 2025 describes four base convolutional neural networks feeding their predictions and the original image into a metamodel, followed by rule-based post-processing; training used 300 head CT scans. Researchers retrospectively evaluated the system on 7,797 consecutive emergency head CT scans from ten emergency rooms, including 118 scans with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage.

According to recent reports, with full post-processing, the study reported case-level sensitivity of 89.8% and specificity of 89.5%—respectively, the proportions of hemorrhage cases detected and negative scans correctly classified. Median analysis time was 6.7 seconds per scan. The system detected all 78 hemorrhage cases imaged definitely or presumably within 12 hours of symptom onset and identified five hemorrhages absent from initial on-call reports.

It has been revealed that the ensemble incorporated an earlier subarachnoid hemorrhage algorithm evaluated in a 2023 Neurology study. Across 1,379 external validation cases, that study reported 99.3% sensitivity and 63.2% specificity.

The earlier authors remarked the false positives would produce an alert in roughly every third normal scan, requiring clinicians to inspect positive findings and positioning the algorithm as assistance rather than a replacement for clinicians. The later ensemble and post-processing were developed to reduce that false-positive problem.

It has been revealed that the 2025 researchers cautioned that their validation remained internal to Finnish hospitals and used on-call radiology reports as the ground truth. Sensitivity declined with longer symptom-to-imaging intervals, making the system best suited to acute cases. Clinical-workflow usefulness was not assessed because the system was not an approved medical device at that time.

Sources indicate that certification addresses that earlier regulatory barrier and gives the partners a basis for wider hospital deployment. The practical question for scaled use is how the acute-case detection and false-positive review described in the studies will affect day-to-day radiology workflows, an effect the 2025 study did not measure.
