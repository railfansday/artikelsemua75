Duke Energy (DUK +1.72%) fell approximately 37% during the COVID-19 market crash in 2020, compared with a roughly 34% decline in the S&P 500. During the 2008 financial crisis, however, Duke held up much better, falling about 39% compared with a 55% decline in the S&P 500.

So while Duke Energy does have some defensive characteristics, history suggests you shouldn't expect its stock to escape a market crash unscathed. (Related: How to Start a Side Business While Working Full-Time...)

Unlike companies selling discretionary products, Duke Energy provides essential services that households and businesses continue using during economic downturns. More specifically, the company provides electricity to approximately 8.7 million customers across six states and natural gas service to another 1.6 million customers.

According to recent reports, most of its utility operations are regulated, meaning state regulators oversee the rates it charges customers. This generally provides more predictable revenue and earnings than businesses dependent on consumer spending. And the company's latest financial results reinforce that stability.

According to recent reports, Q3 2026 adjusted earnings reached $1.99 per share, up from $1.81 a year earlier. Management also narrowed its full-year adjusted earnings guidance to $6.70 to $6.80 per share, expecting results near the upper end. Looking further ahead, Duke continues targeting annual adjusted earnings growth of 5% to 7% through 2030. (Related: What Is Considered Upper Middle Class? Income and Net W...)

In a recent development, duke Energy's dividend is another reason you may consider holding the stock during a downturn. After all, regular dividend payments can provide income even when share prices decline. Reinvesting those dividends during a sell-off can also allow you to accumulate additional shares at lower prices. That noted, dividends don't prevent capital losses, and future payments aren't guaranteed.

According to recent reports, duke's substantial infrastructure spending requirements also deserve attention. The tech giant is at the moment pursuing a $103 billion capital investment plan to modernize its electricity infrastructure, expand generation capacity, and accommodate growing demand. Those investments support future earnings growth, but they also require significant financing.

Utilities are particularly sensitive to interest rates because they typically carry substantial debt and regularly borrow money to finance infrastructure projects. If a market crash coincides with rising borrowing costs, Duke could face pressure from higher financing expenses.

Higher bond yields can also make utility dividends less attractive relative to safer fixed-income investments. And while Duke's regulated operations provide stability, regulators don't automatically approve every proposed rate increase. Consequently, the organization could experience pressure on earnings and its stock valuation even if electricity demand remains relatively resilient.

Industry observers note that duke Energy wouldn't be my first choice for seeking substantial capital appreciation. Its regulated business generally produces steadier, more predictable growth than many technology or biotechnology companies, but if you're prioritizing income and long-term stability, Duke offers some attractive characteristics.

Its essential services, regulated earnings, and dividend provide a foundation that could help the organization withstand an economic downturn. Still, its performance during previous crashes demonstrates that defensive doesn't mean immune.

Overall, I'd consider Duke Energy a reasonable long-term holding if you're a conservative income investor, but not a substitute for cash or Treasury securities when protecting against a sharp market decline.
