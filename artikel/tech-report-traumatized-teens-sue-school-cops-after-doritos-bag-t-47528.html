Tech Report: Traumatized teens sue school, cops after Doritos bag triggers AI gun alert

🌐 Source: Ars Technica • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 9 min read (1731 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • It has been revealed that in October 2025, four Black high school students were sharing Cool Ranch Doritos after football practice, winding down and waiting for their rides home.
  • Recent findings suggest that within two minutes, Baltimore County Public Schools officials who reviewed security camera footage linked to the alert determined there was no actual threat.
  • The false alert could have ended there—before a traumatizing campus clash with police—but it didn’t.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that in October 2025, four Black high school students were sharing Cool Ranch Doritos after football practice, winding down and waiting for their rides home. It was then, in the lull of a moment any teen with after-school activities knows well, that the school’s AI gun detection system suddenly flagged one teen holding a bag of chips as a potential gunman.

Recent findings suggest that within two minutes, Baltimore County Public Schools officials who reviewed security camera footage linked to the alert determined there was no actual threat. They recognized the Doritos bag and canceled the alert, which notified not only other school officials but also the Baltimore County Police Department that no response was needed.

The false alert could have ended there—before a traumatizing campus clash with police—but it didn’t.

According to a lawsuit filed by the current and former students, Taki Allen, Yui-Osa Ogbeide, Devon Thompson Jr., and Keith Winfield III, due to “both technological and human errors,” school officials called police to the scene anyway, and the cops responded with force.

According to recent reports, at least six patrol cars arrived with lights flashing, and some cops emerged with guns drawn. The teens froze and cooperated, confused and scared for their lives, their lawsuit remarked. (Related: The Age of Machine Learning As Code Has Arrived...)

“No one told the students what they were suspected of,” the lawsuit said. “Officers separated the students, ordered them to their knees on the concrete, handcuffed them, and searched their persons and property.”.

It has been revealed that only after the cops failed to find a gun did students learn that it was the school’s AI gun detection system, Omnilert, that triggered the police response. Allegedly, one of the responding officers muttered, “stupid AI cameras” and “fucking Omnilerts,” while another told the students that “AI is not the best.” Finally releasing the students, one officer seemed to acknowledge that they had put the teens in danger. (Related: Porting fairseq wmt19 translation system to transformer...)

“Hey, thanks for not running away, that would have been a real problem,” the officer allegedly said. Walking away, another joked that the boys should remember to “be careful with your chip bags.”.

The cops did not just target the suspected gunman but apparently told the other teens that they were guilty “by association” when they asked why they were searched.

Suing to stop any other teens from facing allegedly life-or-death situations due to officials’ blind trust of AI, the teens have asked for an injunction barring BCPS from using Omnilert until they can effectively establish protocols to prevent police responses to false alerts.

The lawsuit alleged that students’ constitutional rights were violated, as students were unlawfully searched and detained.

Damon Hewitt, president and executive director of the Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law—a nonpartisan nonprofit that’s representing the teens in the suit—said that “overreliance on artificial intelligence technology mixed with inept communication and coordination among key officials is a toxic mix.”.

Industry observers note that the complaint noted that BCPS was among the earliest adopters of Omnilert, and officials have touted it as “the largest deployment of AI visual gun detection” as a means to supposedly protect students. Instead, they alleged that Omnilert is marketing a defective product as triggering “near-zero false positives” without effectively requiring the adequate human review that claim depends on.

Industry observers note that “We need a reckoning on how AI is being used, especially concerning public safety and kids,” Hewitt remarked. “We are fighting to deliver justice in this case, but it also holds lessons for educators and police officials across the country who should think twice before putting our children’s fate in the hands of AI.”.

All the teens feel less safe on campus and less trusting of police following the incident. They also suffered from physical pain—including ”deep red marks” on their wrists from handcuffs—as well as “acute and lasting emotional distress, fear, anxiety,” and “humiliation.” Further, the teens report that rumors follow them around, falsely claiming that there was a gun and damaging their reputations.

Recent findings suggest that car pick-up will never be the same. The student falsely flagged as the gunman, Taki Allen, “no longer feels safe waiting for rides or spending time outside the school after hours” and “frequently pays for rides home from private car services to avoid waiting for rides from friends or family.” Keith Winfield’s house was very close to the school, but his mother, Frieda Winfield, stated his anxiety was so bad that he would call her “multiple times after football practice ended to confirm that she was on her way to pick him up.”.

Frieda Winfield called for change following her son Keith’s chilling interaction.

“The police told my son this could have been a real problem,” Frieda Winfield remarked. “No child in Baltimore County should face this kind of danger while waiting outside his own school for a ride home from his mother.”.

It has been revealed that the teens’ lawsuit targets Omnilert and responding Baltimore County Police Department officers. It also challenges Baltimore County Public Schools and individual school officials who allegedly failed to establish proper protocols to prevent a police response to a canceled alert.

Sources indicate that in a later investigation, the Office of the Inspector General (OIG) for Education concluded that Omnilert’s system had no “bias.” But OIG acknowledged that the “delegation of authority” process that Kenwood High School set up didn’t stop its principal, Kate Smith, from noticing the alert, failing to see it was canceled, and texting a school resource officer to deploy police 15 minutes later—all while acknowledging in that text that the object in the alert was “maybe not” a gun. Smith apparently was never trained on the Omnilert system, OIG’s report indicated.

It has been revealed that the OIG agreed with teens that things should change. Immediately after the incident, an internal Omnilert training link was shared with BCPS staff, and OIG recommended that schools conduct biannual trainings moving forward.

In a recent development, they also recommended that the BCPS Department of School Safety “review its protocol” and reduce the number of individuals who receive campus gun alerts. That could help ensure “internal notification controls are followed” and police “are fully informed of a verified threat,” the OIG noted.

Without changes, the OIG acknowledged that “the loss of alert notification controls could place not only BCPS staff and students at risk but could also place responding law enforcement in a potentially tragic situation.”.

Industry observers note that another change offered a technical solution that may have prevented the tragic event. The OIG recommended that school officials switch to using the Omnilert app. Since Smith opened the alert in a text message window on her phone, they suggested that she could easily have overlooked the cancellation text message if her first instinct were to enlarge the image or review the camera footage.

Industry observers note that these recommendations aren’t enough for current and former students suing, one of whom remains an active student who claims the principal never apologized and instead made light of the incidents using cry-laughing emojis in texts with other school officials. Teens alleged that Smith should have known that more than “99 percent of OGD Alerts in Baltimore County Public Schools were false positives that required no police response,” as the OIG’s report remarked, and she should have prioritized students’ safety and verified the threat before calling in police.

“BCPS’s failures amounted to deliberate indifference to the constitutional rights of its students,” their lawsuit alleged.

In a recent development, the teens asked a court for an injunction barring the use of Omnilert until BCPS staff and police are adequately trained. In their complaint, they alleged that none of the officers on the scene had reviewed the camera footage before responding, and none of the officers informed of the alert cancellation intervened.

BCPS declined to comment on the lawsuit, pointing Ars to a statement issued to parents following the incident. That statement did not directly apologize to students but did say the school would provide “ongoing counseling and emotional support” to them while promising to take steps to prevent future incidents.

Blake Mitchell, Omnilert’s vice president of marketing, told Ars that Omnilert is reviewing the complaint.

Sources indicate that “What these four students went through on October 20, 2025 should not have happened, and we are truly sorry for the fear they and their families experienced,” Mitchell said. “We understand why they want answers.”.

Mitchell said that Omnilert is “built to send anything that could be a firearm to trained people for review, because missing a real gun is the worst possible outcome.” He also reiterated that the OIG investigation found no bias in Omnilert’s system and seemed to agree that it was the school’s authority delegation that failed the students, not necessarily the technology.

Sources indicate that “In this case, the school’s designated safety staff reviewed the alert and canceled it within about two minutes, and everyone who received it was notified,” Mitchell said. “That is exactly why human review matters, and why a canceled alert must stay canceled.”.

Len Kamdang of the Lawyers’ Committee representing the teens told The Newly introduced York Times that teens want BCPS and Omnilert to acknowledge that “what happened to them was unacceptable.”.

“This case is ultimately about how human beings allowed AI to substitute for their judgment,” Kamdang noted.

In a recent development, as a mother, Frieda Winfield told a local news outlet that she was shocked by the incident, despite deliberately teaching her son to be cautious around police. The boys’ caution “saved their lives in my opinion,” she said.

“I was angry. I was fearful,” Frieda Winfield said. “The biggest part was fear, because they’re young men. Young Black men, and they were at school, and they didn’t understand why what was happening, happened to them.”.

Sources indicate that kamdang told NYT that “for anyone with children, that is a scary, scary situation. For a Black student, especially a young Black man in America, that is a life-or-death situation.”.

As AI tools are increasingly positioned as public safety solutions, the Lawyers Committee is pushing for police reform when cops or officials appear to be blindly trusting AI, their press release said. They noted that “this injustice also raises a broader concern regarding the use and reliability of AI-powered technology in public safety where accuracy is essential in life-and-death situations.”.
