Industry observers note that there's really no way to come to a definitive answer on whether Roth conversion is a net positive or not, because while it should be a straightforward algebra question, in reality, there is no way to know the true value of some of the key terms in the equation (your and your spouse's dates of death, your/your spouse's medical expenses from now until then, your heirs' tax rates after your death, etc.). So there is endless discussion of Roth conversions here, because it can never actually be solved-- people are debating assumptions.A common simplification for Bogleheads is to Roth convert up to a desired income level (set by tax rates/IRMAA or TPAW target) early in the year and then spend from Roth for your bills throughout the remaining part of the year.I would also say that, usually, Roth conversion is a "small potatoes" decision. This isn't the thing that will make or break your retirement aims. It won't be the cause of you running out of money before you die (that's some combination of SORR, sustained high inflation, unexpected high expenses, and unusually long lifespan... Roth conversion does nothing to affect any of these). No, for most people, "getting it wrong" amounts to a few percent higher tax rates-- not what you want, maybe, but not the thing that makes you end up on welfare. As a quick rule of thumb, traditional balances under $1M for a married couple or $500k for a single person won't likely affect your tax rates too much due to RMDs. It sounds like you're in that group, or could be in that group with a little bit of Roth conversion per year, so I wouldn't worry overly much about it. If it makes you feel good, go right ahead. But if not, then not.Statistics: Posted by gamma-photon — Mon Nov 23, 2026 8:21 pm.
