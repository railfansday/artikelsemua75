It has been revealed that backstage at the Spring 2027 Chanel show in Paris, after a parade of elegantly skirt-suited women strolled beneath an extravagant set of birds suspended midflight, Matthieu Blazy told a story of picking his teenage nephew up from school. Observing the students, he was struck by the irreverent way they dressed. “They wear a lot of vintage. They are not dictated by a decade—they are a lot more free,” he told Bazaar. “They wear something from the ’30s, ’50s, ’70s. I wanted to expand on that.”.

Recent findings suggest that one look in particular felt more akin to how someone would dress to attend the Chanel show rather than to walk it—an old-school Chanel jacket worn with new Chanel shoes, a pair of black trousers, eclectic jewelry and an old belt. It wasn’t an anomalous look; the vintagey vibes loomed large. Knee-length skirt suits were styled with heaps of necklaces. Men’s shirting was mixed with relaxed tailoring and leather separates; tiny white tank tops peeked from beneath tweed jackets festooned with oversized, floral brooches. According to recent reports, loving and collecting vintage (or better yet, holding onto pieces until they became vintage) was once the domain of fashion insiders. But today, young people are growing up native to the secondhand market. (According to ThredUp’s 2026 Resale Report, 62 percent of Gen Z shopped secondhand last year.) As they age, dressing from a closet that contains clothing from multiple style eras has become inevitable. And designers are taking notice. Just as the Dolce & Gabbana show was about to start in Milan a week before the Chanel show, the loudspeaker turned on to regale the audience on “the wardrobes of Sicily’s grand houses. Clothes once worn by great-grandmothers, grandmothers, mothers, aunts and daughters. A slip dress is worn over a jacket, antique lace meets contemporary pieces, lingerie and eveningwear coexist, and the precious and the everyday merge in an unexpected balance.” The styling made it feel as if a young woman pilfered grandma’s closet, mixing an antique, pussy bow blouse with embellished and ripped jeans or pairing a D&G baby tee with a leopard-trimmed, tweedy jacket.

At Gucci, Demna dissected the archetypes of the bourgeoisie, including “the rebellious aristocrat.” A series of looks paired variations of rugby shirts, pleated tartan skirts, puff-shaped bomber jackets with oversized belts adorned with horsebits and heaps of jewelry piled onto necklines. It’s what a “blue-blooded child of an aristocrat might wear to the private school that she hates,” Demna mused after the show. Within that layered ensemble, he defined a character: Her kilt is part of her school uniform; her rugby polo and leather jacket were mined from a thrift store or her dad’s closet. She wore all these pieces together with no regard for where or what era they came from.

The idea of dressing “vintage” on a runway is inherently contradictory. The runways are a place of fresh; vintage is, by definition, old. But the concept is more about adding depth and texture to an ensemble than it is about the age of the garment itself. It’s not about, as in seasons past, garments that are distressed to look worn and weathered. It’s about achieving a kind of idiosyncrasy in a landscape that had become samey and polished and “quiet luxury.” It’s about a desire for irreverence.

Recent findings suggest that the Row is a spectacular case study here, particularly as it was one of the brands that ushered in the minimalist look of the early 2020s. So how to evolve without alienating your customer? For Spring 2027, the Olsen twins draped and knotted antique linens, lace, and doilies over their typical rotation of sleek, black trousers and perfectly mussed trench coats. Models wore layers of dainty beads or single chunky necklaces that nodded subtly towards eclecticism or bohemian.

Sources indicate that fashion must contend and engage with vintage; increasingly, it is just a part of how people shop. As the secondhand market gains traction, it presents new challenges; houses are in constant competition with their own archives. Just look at the resurgences of secondhand Pucci and Etro from the early 2000s. Many have embraced reissues: Chloé’s Maxime wedge or the McQueen skull scarf, for example, shown tucked into contemporary collections of recent years. Of course, creative directors at legacy houses have always had to find ways to be in conversation with their archives and predecessors. The greatest designers, however, manage to use this formula to their advantage.

It has been revealed that take Blazy at Chanel, for instance. He must contend with Gabrielle “Coco” Chanel’s founding legacy of the early and mid-1900s in addition to Karl Lagerfeld’s from the ’80s through the 2010s. The idea of a knee-length skirt suit in nubby fabric could seem retro, but Blazy leaned into it instead of pushing against it. He didn’t rewrite the idea entirely, but shifted our perception with subtle tweaks in materials, textures, and styling. He even sent Gabrielle’s original 2.55 bag down the runway, paired with a gauzy white cocktail dress and then again later with a black skirt suit.

Ralph Lauren mined his own greatest hits for a spring collection that was a glorious mishmash of old and fresh, clashing Americana Westernwear and prep with tropes of the English aristocracy, and even utilitarian workwear. He strung models’ waistcoats with pocket watches, paired brocade evening jackets with distressed denim, and matched ruffled-collar blouses with metallic skirts.

In a recent development, it’s that dialogue between items that conjures the feel of someone who wears vintage, someone who curates their closet with stylistic depth, nuance, and contrast. What young people achieve with aplomb is decidedly difficult to manufacture. But that’s what makes it so desirable. It turns runway models from mannequins into characters and makes you want to learn their stories.
