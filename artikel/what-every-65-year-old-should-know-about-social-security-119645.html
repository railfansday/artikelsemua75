Industry observers note that age 65 can feel like a gray area for Social Security because it's a few years after you can start receiving benefits, but it's still before you reach your full retirement age. For a while, 65 was the traditional retirement age and seen as the "finish line," but that isn't necessarily the case today. (Related: Industry News: Hyperparameter Search with Transformers ...)

But age 65 still matters because it affects Social Security benefits and has health insurance implications, so if you're nearing 65 or planning to claim at that age, here are a few things to know.

Sources indicate that you can begin claiming Social Security at age 62, but claiming any time before your full retirement age -- which is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later -- will reduce your monthly benefit. Your full retirement age is when you can receive your full monthly benefit, called your primary insurance amount. This is the starting point Social Security uses to determine your benefit based on when you claim. (Related: Tech Report: Transformer-based Encoder-Decoder Models...)

Benefits are reduced by 5/9 of 1% monthly for each month you claim early, up to 36 months. So, if your full retirement age is 67 and you claim at 65, your benefit will be reduced by around 13.33%. If your full benefit was $2,000, you'd receive $1,733.33 if you claimed at 65.

In a recent development, if you claim benefits before your full retirement age, you could be subject to the Social Security retirement earnings test if you earn above a certain limit. For 2026, the limit is $24,480 if you won't reach your full retirement age in 2026 and $65,160 if you will. In 2027, these limits increase to $25,560 and $67,920, respectively.

In a recent development, if you earn above the limit in the year you won't reach full retirement age, your monthly benefit will be reduced by $1 for each $2 you're over. If you reach full retirement age that year and earn above the limit, your benefit is reduced by $1 per $3 you're over.

Thankfully, benefits aren't permanently lost -- just withheld. Once you reach your full retirement age, Social Security will gradually return the withheld benefits by increasing your monthly payout.

Industry observers note that social Security and Medicare are two different programs, but they're connected in how premiums are paid. You become eligible for Medicare at age 65, and if you're receiving Social Security at the time, your Medicare Part B premiums are automatically deducted from your monthly Social Security benefit.

According to recent reports, this year, the Medicare Part B premium is $202.90, but it will increase to $209.40 in 2027. If you claim both Social Security and Medicare and your monthly benefit is $2,000 next year, you'd receive $1,790.60 after the premium is deducted.

This automatic deduction applies only to Part B premiums. If you're enrolled in another Medicare strategy, such as Part C (Medicare Advantage) or Part D (prescriptions), you'll pay those premiums directly.
