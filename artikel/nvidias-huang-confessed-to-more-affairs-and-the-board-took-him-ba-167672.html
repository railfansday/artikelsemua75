Jensen Huang may be back at Nvidia, but that doesn’t mean the book is closed on his infidelities or whether his affair with a suspected intelligence asset for China was a national security risk. Huang, his company, and its customers are still under investigation by—takes a deep breath—the SEC, the FBI, the Commerce and Justice departments, three House committees, one Senate committee, an internal corporate probe conducted by Nvidia’s outside counsel, and the California attorney general’s office. (That doesn’t include the civil litigation.).

Industry observers note that it has been a head-spinning 24 hours, and we have learned some jaw-dropping details about how Huang clawed his way back into the CEO’s office. Among them:. (Related: Industry News: Simple considerations for simple people ...)

More affairs. On Sunday night, “Huang disclosed to the board additional affairs beyond the two that had been widely reported,” the WSJ reported.

Trump’s stock gains. President Trump called into the company’s board meeting to urge the directors to take him back. Trump wasn’t concerned by the affairs, he told White House advisers. Rather, he wanted the markets to rebound, the WSJ said. Trump personally made $15 million in gains as the stock rebounded, according to the FT.

Sources indicate that CFO Kress sidelined. Trump had also seen interim CEO Colette Kress on CNBC a few days earlier and was not impressed. “You interviewed the young woman, I thought she was very good, but she’s not a chip person. She doesn’t know. She knows less about chips than I do,” Trump later stated during an interview with CNBC. (Kress has been the manufacturer’s CFO for 13 years.).

Sunday’s board meeting was kept a secret from Kress, the FT reported.

A humiliating reversal. Although the board had begun a search to replace Huang, they found few candidates willing to step into Huang’s shoes, the WSJ mentioned. (Related: Hyperparameter Search with Transformers and Ray Tune...)

On Wednesday, after categorically ruling out Huang’s return, the company held an all-hands meeting at which management played the Tom Petty song “I Won’t Back Down.”.

According to recent reports, five days later, Huang walked back into Nvidia’s HQ to a standing ovation from employees. “This is my first day of work,” he told them. “I don’t have a computer, so the I.T. department is going to give me a new P.C.”.

It has been revealed that vance lobbied. During his 11-day exile, Huang had a 45-minute phone call with J.D. Vance. The vice president “was seemingly persuaded by the entreaties of Huang and his allies, who are probably a crucial source of funding if Vance decides to run for president,” the FT reported.

Industry observers note that 'Deep state'? Huang’s Silicon Valley allies also played upon Trump’s historic mistrust of the U.S. intelligence community, Axios reported, by portraying Huang as the persecuted victim of the “deep state.”.

Crude humor. Finally, when the board reversed its decision, Trump posted a photo taken of himself raising a fist after he narrowly avoided assassination in Pennsylvania—except that his face was replaced by Huang’s and the fist is holding a red bra.

Not everyone was impressed. “The legal and reputational risk of hiring a CEO who may very well be convicted in the United States or other nations of violating espionage and export control laws is now borne by the board of Nvidia, but the national security risk is passed along to the United States military and its citizens. The risk of extortion by a Chinese asset who’s been banned by Singapore is risk enough to not rehire Huang. The worst part of this is what we may never know,” Sen. Kevin Cramer (R-N.D.) said on Facebook.

In 2002, Gwynne Shotwell had a lunch that would change her life—and the trajectory of the business world as we know it. Then, at 38, she had been working for a decade at a rocket startup, frustrated at the pace of innovation on the ground. “It wasn’t a vibrant industry, and we hadn’t gotten back to the moon [in 30 years],” she said in 2022. “The systems were designed to eliminate risk, so they took forever and cost too much.” A former colleague had just departed for another rocket startup, and she met him to celebrate at a favorite Austrian restaurant in the coastal L.A. suburb of El Segundo. Afterward, her friend suggested Shotwell stop by his upcoming company to meet the owner.

The owner, of course, was Elon Musk. Fresh off making a fortune at PayPal, Musk laid out his vision to the curious Shotwell: make rockets a lot more cheaply than NASA; sell releases to commercial satellite companies at super-bargain prices; slash prices even more by making the rockets reusable. Shotwell blurted out that he needed a “business developer,” effectively a sales leader who could win the pre-product contracts needed to fund the vision. Musk called her with an offer, and within days, Shotwell came aboard SpaceX. “I knew that working for Elon would be my last job in aerospace,” she has often said.

In a recent development, for those of you who were living under a rock: U.S. stocks rose sharply yesterday after Nvidia decided to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO. The tech-heavy Nasdaq 100 was up 6.86%—a massive move for an index its size.

But this morning traders seem to be nursing a hangover from yesterday’s party. S&P 500 futures were down before the open in New York. Nvidia stock, which had gone up 32% yesterday, lost 4.27% in overnight trading. Nasdaq 100 futures were down 1.23%—a relatively major step down for one day’s trade.

Do not fret, some on Wall Street are saying today. “IG’s analysis of 75 years of U.S. stock market data reveals that the S&P 500 has risen in 86% of Decembers following a negative November,” according to IG Chief Market Analyst Chris Beauchamp.

Deutsche Bank also released its official outlook for 2027: “Our equity strategists see the S&P 500 reaching 9,000 by end-2027 on extremely supportive earnings,” Deutsche’s Jim Reid stated.

The stunning return of Jensen Huang to the CEO’s office at Nvidia added nearly $1 trillion to the market cap of the tech giant yesterday—a reminder that this soap opera was as much about the value of equity as it was national security.

“That financial markets moved on the employment prospects of one individual suggests either equity valuation models or current pricing might need to be examined,” UBS’s Paul Donovan noted this morning.

A prescient note from Bryce Anderson at Morningstar, published before Nvidia’s U-turn, describes why the AI trade is so fragile: The circular financing of the industry is very real, he said: “Money goes in as equity and comes back as spending on cloud and chips, so a stumble at one lab would hit its investors’ earnings, its cloud backlog and its suppliers’ orders at the same time. The arrangement works for as long as every party keeps spending, but if any link in the chain breaks, investors could be left disappointed.”.

The scale of Nvidia’s support for the AI industry is huge: It spent $700 billion in “vendor financing”—giving companies money so they can purchase Nvidia products—over the last three years, per the FT.
