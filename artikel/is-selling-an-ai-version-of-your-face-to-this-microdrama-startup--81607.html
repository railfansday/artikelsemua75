Is selling an AI version of your face to this microdrama startup the future of Hollywood?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

For the most part, the content offered by the microdrama startup Shortical is indistinguishable from the rest of its industry. The organization produces soapy, melodramatic storylines — almost always risqué romances — that, in bite-sized two-minute episodes, compel viewers to stay glued to their phones so as not to miss the latest twist in a tawdry, cliff-hanger-happy saga.

According to recent reports, the primary way that Shortical — which was founded last January — makes money is through subscriptions. Typically, the first 7-10 episodes of a micro-drama are free. After that, if the user wants to finish the series, they have to pay a small monthly fee which unlocks access to all of the site’s content. Over the past year, the company has churned out a gush of popular live-action romances like “Crossing the Line with My Neighbor,” “Accidentally Married My Boss,” and “Spoiled by My Billionaire Sugar Daddy.”. (Related: Grandma who accidentally invited a stranger to Thanksgi...)

In a recent development, lately, however, the organization’s homepage is covered in AI-generated content. The reason isn’t exactly hard to figure out: the production overhead for AI content is substantially less than live-action. The profit potential, meanwhile, is substantially higher, if you can draw an audience.

According to recent reports, its latest microdrama premiered in Los Angeles last week. The series, called “Inevitable,” stars Aki Avni, an Israeli film and television star who has been visible in American TV shows for years (he had a stint on Fox’s 9/11-era thriller “24” years ago) but is now trying something largely unheard of in modern Hollywood: licensing his face to Shortical for the sake of its AI content.

The project, which is wholly AI, used Avni’s face to create a digital avatar of him. In “Inevitable,” he plays himself – in a very meta plot in which he — the actor Aki Avni — licenses his face to a film studio, after which thriller-like antics ensue.

This is Shortical’s first foray into such a licensing deal, but it likely won’t be their last.

In a phone interview, Avni told TechCrunch that he found the project both nerveracking and exciting. “Look, it’s a new era for artists,” the actor said. “AI is a new kind of art. It’s a new format. It’s always great to be the pioneer in something.”.

It has been revealed that guy Shimoni, the CEO of Shortical, emphasized the creative potential of the new tool. “One of the things that AI does is it enables smaller teams to be able to produce these movie-scale productions,” Shimoni said. “When we were doing mobile-first live action, we were limited by budget, but now we can serve them with epic fantasies. We have kingdoms and dragons. We have superheroes. We have werewolves.”.

But AI-generated romances involving werewolves — who, exactly, is watching this kind of thing?

In a recent development, apparently, a lot of people. While China boasts the biggest microdrama audience globally, Shimoni says that most of his platform’s viewers are actually in the U.S. The industry’s viewer demographic skews heavily female. Many viewers may watch them to wind down at night, getting minor dopamine hits as they drift off to sleep. Many of the AI-generated programs that already exist on the company’s website appear to have millions of views.

It has been revealed that now, Shortical is betting on the creation of a new generation of creators — those who, like Avni, are willing to surrender their likenesses for compensation. “LICENSE YOUR FACE” Shortical’s website advertises, explaining that interested parties can submit their social media handles to the manufacturer, which will then decide whether to offer them “a contract setting out the terms” of using their persona in the company’s AI-generated content.

A company rep told TechCrunch that this “offering is still in its early days and hasn’t yet resulted in any signed contracts.” They added: “The series with Aki is the first time Shortical has used a real person’s likeness, and it was done through a direct agreement with him rather than through the platform. Shortical hopes the series will draw more attention to face licensing and plans to promote the offering more actively following this launch.”.

Industry observers note that much of this trend has already taken hold in China where the microdrama industry originated, where AI-generated content is big, and where face-licensing is already a trend. Although the trend may not be a particularly profitable one for creators. A recent report in Rest of World found that Microdrama firms were paying people “between US$15 to US$700 to license their facial likenesses.”.

How much money is Shortical offering for its face-licensing deals? The company isn’t saying. As for if Shortical will offer royalty-based compensation for creators — like the residual system in television, “It’s a case by case [basis],” Shimoni answers vaguely.

A Shortical representative further explained to TechCrunch that the manufacturer didn’t want to “share a specific figure” for Avni’s particular contract. However, the person said, he came “on as a partner in the project, and his compensation was in line with industry standards for a comparable non-AI acting role.”.

It has been revealed that for other actors, however, the deals may be quite different. Plus, many creatives are understandably unsettled by signs of automation creeping into their industry. Until recently, microdramas seemed like a safe, entry-level gateway into the entertainment industry, where young actors could gain experience on sets and hone their craft. Now, companies are downsizing and producing content that doesn’t even require real actors.

Jennifer Cooper, a longtime fan of the industry and founder of the consultancy firm Vertical Drama Love, notes that there’s a lot of distrust of AI — both from audiences and within the microdrama industry itself.

It has been revealed that “Everyone’s been impacted by AI,” she said. “A lot of us have either lost work to AI or can see that it’s devastated this industry — [taking microdramas] from something that was a rare thing in the world, that was in growth, and providing work, particularly for young people…[and now] you know, it’s been decimated.”.

Questions of consent with AI likenesses have also dogged the industry (some creators claim they have had their likenesses stolen), which is something that Shortical is obviously trying to address with its new face-licensing endeavor.

Industry observers note that yet these licensing deals could also help to gig-ify the industry, creating a scenario in which creators pay a distributor for the chance to platform their content (or their face), then receive payouts based on the audience engagement they’re allowed to generate. Shortical recently partnered with a firm called Payouts.com, which describes itself as a “payout infrastructure platform powering creator and gig economy disbursements at scale.” The plan is to create a system by which creatives can seamlessly interact with Shortical’s platform and get paid.

Cooper believes the push towards AI in the industry is driven by broader financial trends. Most of the microdrama business is led by startups, and VCs love to hear a growth game plan involving automation. “For anyone to get investment at the moment in microdramas, they’ve got to mention the word AI,” she says. “It’s like AI equals gold in everyone’s eyes.”.

Despite all the investment in this space, Cooper seems skeptical that there is actually a huge audience for this kind of content. “Some of it does get views. A lot of it doesn’t,” she noted, of the AI content. “I think it’s being pushed on an audience more than there is an audience demand or wish for it.”.
