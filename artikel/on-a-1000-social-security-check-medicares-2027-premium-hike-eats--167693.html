On a $1,000 Social Security check, Medicare’s 2027 premium hike eats 18% of the COLA raise—and hits small checks the hardest

🌐 Source: Fortune • 📅 24 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (786 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In 2027, Social Security recipients will get their biggest cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) in four years. January’s benefits will be 3.6% higher, lifting the average retired worker’s monthly check to about $2,170, according to the Social Security Administration’s 2027 COLA announcement. The larger adjustment reflects persistent inflation in 2026.

Most COLA announcements fail to emphasize that Medicare takes its slice first. Starting in January, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services confirmed on Nov. 17 that the standard Part B premium rises by $6.50 to $209.40 a month. For most retirees, that premium is deducted straight from their Social Security benefit.

In a recent development, although $6.50 doesn’t sound like much, it absorbs about 9% of the average retiree’s raise. And because the premium is a flat amount, the smaller the check, the bigger the share it takes. (Related: Breaking: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Faster ...)

Recent findings suggest that for a retiree collecting the 2026 average of $2,095 a month, the 3.6% COLA adds about $75. After the higher premium, roughly $69 remains, so Medicare claims about 9% of the increase.

Lower down the scale, the bite grows. A $1,000 monthly benefit rises by $36, but only $29.50 survives the premium hike, an 18% haircut on the raise. At $1,500 a month, the COLA is worth $54 and the net gain is $47.50, meaning 12% of the raise goes to Medicare. (Related: Introducing Optimum: The Optimization Toolkit for Trans...)

Bigger checks barely feel it. A $2,500 benefit gains $90 and keeps $83.50, losing 7% of the increase. At $3,000 a month, the raise is $108, the net gain is $101.50, and the premium takes just 6%.

That math applies to retirees who pay the standard premium out of their benefits. Higher-income enrollees pay income-based surcharges on top of it, while states cover the Part B premium for about one in five Medicare enrollees with low incomes and savings.

Industry observers note that by recent standards, this year’s hike is mild. The 3.2% premium increase is smaller than the COLA for the first time since 2023; in 2026, the premium jumped 9.7% against a 2.8% COLA.

Sources indicate that even though COLAs are treated like bonuses or raises, they’re not. As David Herbert, director of economics & economic freedom and a senior research fellow at the American Institute for Economic Research (AIER), told The Hill, “It’s meant to make sure that Social Security recipients are receiving the same amount of spending power as they did in the past. It’s not actually going to make them wealthier or have an easier time buying things that they weren’t already going to buy.”.

Sources indicate that seniors appear to agree. In an AARP survey conducted before the announcement, 80% of Social Security recipients mentioned a projected 3.5% COLA in 2027 would not keep up with rising prices. When the final number landed, some retirees dismissed it as too little to offset soaring costs.

It has been revealed that it seems as though seniors are right. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported 3.7% annual inflation for September 2026, its most recent annual figure. The 3.6% COLA will not keep up, especially since the higher payments don’t start until January.

By then, seniors will have absorbed higher prices since March, when inflation hit 3.3% and outpaced the 2026 COLA of 2.8%. It peaked at 4.2% in May. In other words, a 3.6% COLA is a small improvement, but it comes too late.

Recent findings suggest that against the backdrop of higher benefits and costs looms Social Security’s insolvency. The 2026 Trustees Report found that starting in the fourth quarter of 2032, the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) Trust Fund will no longer be able to pay 100% of scheduled benefits. Instead, recipients would receive only 78% of them.

Sources indicate that part of the funding shortfall comes down to demographics. According to the trustees’ projections, about 2.6 workers paid in for every beneficiary in 2025, compared with 41.9 in 1945. The trustees expect the ratio to keep falling, to about 1.9 workers per beneficiary by 2075.

With fewer workers paying into Social Security, “Congress is going to have to step in and do something,” said Herbert. “What is going to be left over for people who are not quite at retirement but are hoping at some point to see any form of a return on investment on this money that we’ve been sending the federal government for our entire working lives?”.

That job falls to the latest Congress, which convenes in January and has roughly six years to act before automatic cuts kick in. In the meantime, Herbert advises those a decade or two from retirement to start saving and investing. More importantly, workers shouldn’t plan on receiving full Social Security benefits in retirement.
