Texas Democrats’ sweeping victory could spell trouble for Elon Musk’s business empire

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, hundreds of millions of dollars later, Elon Musk has some regrets. “I feel pretty foolish about having spent so much money and not seeing much for it,” the world’s richest man noted at The Newly introduced York Times DealBook conference this week, reflecting on the heaps of dollars he poured into Republican campaigns nationwide during this year’s midterm elections. It’s easy to see why he feels that way, particularly in Texas, where Musk has moved his business operations and cultivated close ties with Republican leadership. Despite his extraordinary spending—$446 million, roughly half of which went to races in Texas—Democrats seized control of both chambers of Congress and swept the major statewide elections. That eye-popping figure includes $5 million for Republican incumbent Greg Abbott’s gubernatorial campaign and $10 million for Attorney General Ken Paxton’s U.S. Senate bid, both of which ended in defeat. But Musk may soon have much bigger financial headaches than any futile campaign contributions. The incoming Democratic administration in Austin could complicate the billionaire’s aims to expand his sprawling business empire. Indeed, Texas’s governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, has already signaled that she intends to be less accommodating of the kinds of tax breaks and land deals that helped lure Musk to the Lone Star State in the first place. Abbott has by contrast been an enthusiastic champion of Musk’s Texas vision, appearing alongside him at organization events and celebrating his investments in the state. Abbott and Musk reportedly keep in close contact, and the governor’s office even fought to prevent the release of months of communications with the tech mogul’s companies, arguing that they contain “information that is intimate and embarrassing and not of legitimate concern to the public.” With Hinojosa in office, Musk can no longer count on that same level of intimacy, though he did offer a more sanguine assessment of his future in a blue Texas at the DealBook conference, predicting that Hinojosa would govern as a moderate. “I think things will be fine in Texas with Gina as governor,” he said. Billions in tax breaks Musk’s companies have to date been given the royal treatment from the state. SpaceX, for instance, scored roughly $1.66 billion in potential property tax breaks for its planned Terafab semiconductor facility in Grimes County, between College Station and Houston, according to the Houston Chronicle. The incentives, approved by two local school districts under Texas’s Jobs, Energy, Technology and Innovation program, could cut the company’s school property taxes in half for 10 years per phase of construction. SpaceX also secured a $30 million grant from the Texas Enterprise Fund. Hinojosa has made no secret of her disdain for such arrangements. “I do think we should wipe clean corporate welfare in this state,” she told the Texas Standard in October, singling out Musk’s billion-dollar-plus tax break. She’s also pushing to reveal to the public more about Musk’s relationship with Abbott. In October, she sued Abbott for access to unredacted emails involving Musk and his companies. But Hinojosa won’t have an easy time undoing those incentives. She would have considerable discretion over the Texas Enterprise Fund, although recovering money already awarded would depend on the grant’s terms. Reversing the much larger property tax breaks would prove far more difficult. Those agreements are made among the company, local school districts, and the governor, who cannot simply terminate them over a change in policy. State law does allow the governor to revoke the incentives if a company fails to meet certain employment or wage requirements, but even then, the company must be given an opportunity to remedy or challenge the violation. To that end, Hinojosa has said she plans on reviewing Musk’s existing incentives to ensure his companies are meeting employment obligations. Those requirements can be remarkably lenient: Per the Chronicle, SpaceX needs to create just 80 jobs at its Terafab facility to meet the minimum employment threshold for the school-district tax incentives. SpaceX’s South Texas expansion Hinojosa’s victory could also throw a wrench into SpaceX’s expansion plans in South Texas, where the company is pursuing massive new infrastructure projects—and seeking additional land for its operations. For starters, there’s the $24 billion data center and natural gas-fired power plant planned near its Starbase headquarters near the border with Mexico. SpaceX confirmed the project earlier this month and claims construction activity has already created more than 3,000 jobs, though the facility is not yet operational. (That project has already encountered resistance from local officials, with Cameron County commissioners voting this week 3–2 against advancing SpaceX’s request for a 10-year, 100% property-tax abatement for the project.) Hinojosa has pledged to impose a moratorium on data center approvals until Texas lawmakers enact stronger protections for water supplies, electricity customers, and surrounding communities. And she’s also indicated that projects already underway wouldn’t necessarily be exempt: “If it needs a permit that the state has to approve, then that’s on hold,” she told the Chronicle last week. Again, though, SpaceX has gotten a head start. The Texas Commission on Environmental Quality this week approved an air permit for a natural gas plant associated with the project. But that permit covers only the power-generating component, not the entire $24 billion development. So Hinojosa could still hold up the project if SpaceX needs additional state approvals once she takes office. Meanwhile, SpaceX has also been pursuing a deal to acquire state-owned parkland near its Boca Chica launch facility, at the mouth of the Rio Grande. Texas Monthly reported last month that state officials were considering selling 1,300 acres to the company, raising obvious questions around coastal access and habitat preservation. Hinojosa, who grew up in nearby Brownsville, asked the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department shortly after her election to hold off on any action during the transition. SpaceX has since clarified that it wants approximately 1,200 acres of public parkland surrounding its launch facility—not Boca Chica Beach itself—and says it would preserve half the property as coastal habitat. And SpaceX could face another headache at Boca Chica. Benjamin Flores, the Democrat who is the incoming Texas land commissioner, plans to challenge a 2013 law allowing temporary beach closures for rocket releases, Texas Monthly reports. And while the state Supreme Court threw out a previous challenge in June, that rejection came on procedural grounds, leaving the law’s constitutionality unresolved. A possible Tesla–SpaceX merger Tesla and SpaceX have been discussing a merger, which would bring Musk’s EV, rocket, satellite and AI businesses under one corporate roof. The state’s new Democratic leadership could throw a wrench into those plans. Texas’s incoming attorney general, Nathan Johnson, has already signaled that he intends to take a very close look at any such tie-up. “Any merger of companies of that consequence warrants tremendous scrutiny,” he told Bloomberg last week, citing concerns about corporate concentration. Johnson’s office could investigate potential antitrust violations and, if deemed warranted, challenge the merger in court. Unlike Hinojosa’s proposed data center restrictions, which could require new legislation or regulations, that authority already exists under state law.|.
