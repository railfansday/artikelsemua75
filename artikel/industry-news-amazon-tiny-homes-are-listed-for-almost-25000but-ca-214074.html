As home prices and mortgage rates have climbed in recent years, homebuyers and developers alike have turned to factory-built tiny homes as a potential solution for affordable housing.

While low prices and fast shipping make prefab homes from Amazon look like a bargain, buyers should be aware of several factors with this purchase—starting with the fact that selling them is actually prohibited on the platform. (Related: The Partnership: Amazon SageMaker and Hugging Face...)

Amazon prohibits the sale of habitable tiny homes and prefabricated dwellings, with its Seller Central policy explicitly banning complete prefab homes, tiny house kits marketed as living spaces, modular and container homes, and other factory-built housing units. So, why then does a search for "prefab tiny homes" bring up thousands of results?

Many of the tiny homes on Amazon are simple backyard sheds, compliant with the retailer's rules. Others walk a fine line.

Several listings, which fall between $20,000 and $25,000, use words like “prefabricated steel building” and “expandable modular container.”.

The highlights for one now unlisted item described it as “a portable, multi-purpose outdoor structure for storage, workspace, display, or commercial utility use.” It goes on to say that it has “versatile non-residential use—suitable for office setup, equipment storage, pop-up workspace, site unit, studio, or backyard utility structure.”.

Nowhere in the description did it call itself a home or use words like "residential" or "habitation.".

However, a flip through the photos showed images of a warm and inviting interior—including a seemingly spacious bedroom. Potential layouts showed both one- and two-bedroom floor plans.

Realtor.com® reached out to Amazon for a comment on these listings but received no response.

According to recent reports, those looking to take the leap on a tiny home should still give local permitting requirements a close look. There is no single national zoning law for tiny homes in the United States, so buyers should check their local ordinances before purchasing.

In a recent development, for example, in Los Angeles, tiny homes on permanent foundations, rather than wheels, are generally allowed, but each habitable room must be at least 150 square feet under the California Residential Code. Units must also meet all building code requirements for habitable space, such as proper ventilation and fire safety.

As long as owners receive the proper install and permits, owners can generally take out insurance on tiny homes, whether they serve as a primary residence or accessory dwelling unit like a detached studio or office.

Recent findings suggest that “Insurance companies generally don't have concerns with prefab homes, manufactured homes, or sheds. Most also don't have issues with an accessory dwelling unit on the site of a house,” Tom Firestine, founder of Longmeadow Insurance, tells Realtor.com. “When the local municipality allows them, we don’t have an glitch extending coverage.”.

According to recent reports, regional weather differences raise distinct structural challenges for tiny homes. In Phoenix, extreme heat requires all units to have proper insulation and HVAC. While coastal regions contend with high winds, Firestine, who works out of Chicago, notes their main concern is heavy snow loads.

According to recent reports, “The biggest problem is whether the structure's install is permitted and how the use is presented to the insurance company,” notes Firestine. “Intentionally misrepresenting something on an insurance application is the easiest way to get a claim denied. Insurers generally won't insure an illegal structure.”.
