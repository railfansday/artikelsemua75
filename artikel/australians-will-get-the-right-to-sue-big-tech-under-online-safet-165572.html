Australians will get the right to sue big tech under online safety deal

House of Representatives, Parliament House, Canberra.

It has been revealed that australians are set to win the right to sue tech companies over online harm, under a deal that clears the way for the country’s digital duty of care law. Communications Minister Anika Wells declared the agreement with the Greens on Tuesday and noted the bill would pass parliament this week.

In a recent development, the duty of care flips how Australia polices the internet: rather than regulators chasing harmful content after it spreads, platforms themselves must find and reduce foreseeable risks before people get hurt. The bill passed the lower house in October, but needed Greens votes in the Senate after the opposition Coalition came out against it. Those votes came at a price, as individuals and groups of Australians will be able to sue platforms that breach their duty of care. A separate external complaints body will give people a faster route alongside the courts. (Related: Breaking: Block Sparse Matrices for Smaller and Faster ...)

Industry observers note that the penalties grew in the bargain too. Companies that breach the duty now face fines of A$109mn (about €62mn) or up to 5% of global revenue. Wells said that figure sits between Canada’s 3% and the EU’s 6%, and noted courts in Europe tend to award a share of the maximum based on the case.

Recent findings suggest that the amended bill also names addictive design features, such as endless scroll and popularity meters, as a risk platforms must assess. It expands the eSafety regulator’s power to take down nudify apps and strengthens privacy protections.

Industry observers note that the definition of content that promotes hatred of women gets sharper too. Wells stated it is aimed at material pushing violence and harassment at children, not at opinions about feminism.

“If you build it, you should be responsible for its risks,” Wells told reporters in Canberra.

It has been revealed that the global-revenue penalty may not land well in Washington, which has objected to countries sizing fines on US tech companies by their worldwide earnings. Wells met the US ambassador, David Brat, on Tuesday morning and described the conversation as friendly, with differences still being worked through.

Once the law passes, platforms get a 12-month transition before the duty takes effect. It stacks on top of Australia’s under-16 social media ban, the world’s first, which has been in force since December.
