Industry News: In a West Texas rock shelter, archaeologists found a hunting kit that was left behind 6,500 years

Archaeologists in Texas uncovered a roughly 6,500-year-old hunting kit with darts, an atlatl and a rare preserved pronghorn hide.

Deep inside San Esteban Rockshelter, south of Marfa, Texas, archaeologists recovered parts of a hunter’s weapon kit that someone left behind thousands of years ago.

The Center for Significant Bend Studies at Sul Ross State University presented the find in its spring 2025 newsletter. The center puts the kit’s age at about 6,500 years and calls it “potentially the oldest example of a full weapon system in North America.”.

The kit as a whole has not yet been described in a peer-reviewed study. Only one of its pieces, a spear-thrower known as an atlatl, has been the subject of a journal paper.

The kit includes six stone-tipped dart foreshafts – the front section of a dart – and four self-tipped hardwood dart foreshafts.

There are also four dart nock-ends, the rear tips that fit against the atlatl. A broken hunting boomerang and a partial atlatl complete the weapons.

Sources indicate that the excavators also found a folded pronghorn hide. It had been tanned, and its hair was still intact.

Spaced holes ran around the hide’s perimeter so it could be tied to a frame, as Plains groups often did.

“We just sat there and stared at it in wonder,” said Bryon Schroeder, then director of the center. Someone had folded the hide and set it on top of a rock, he explained. (Related: Director of Machine Learning Insights...)

“And nobody touched it for 6,000 years,” Schroeder mentioned.

An atlatl is essentially a wooden stick with a handle on one end and a hook or socket on the other. It acts as an extension of the human arm, increasing leverage against the dart, the National Park Service explains. (Related: Woman evicted after apartment’s AI assistant mistakenly...)

The San Esteban atlatl is described in a 2023 atlatl paper by Schroeder and Devin Pettigrew, an assistant professor at the center.

A radiocarbon date indicates that the atlatl is more than 6,000 years old. That made it the oldest directly dated atlatl in North America at the time, the authors reported in the Journal of Major Bend Studies.

In a paper published in 2026, Pettigrew and other archaeologists at the center measured how fast modern throwers launch darts.

That research shows that heavier darts, paired with strong and skilled throwers, become lethal to very large animals such as mammoths, the center noted.

It has been revealed that “In most archeological sites in Texas, only things made of stone survive,” notes Texas Beyond History, a project of the University of Texas at Austin. The dry Lower Pecos rock shelters are an exception.

It has been revealed that those shelters have yielded hardwood dart foreshafts and rabbit sticks, as well as bow fragments and arrow foreshafts from the bow-and-arrow era. Complete atlatls are very rare even there.

Unique depositional conditions favored the preservation of the Schöningen wooden spears in Germany. A 2025 dating study placed them at about 200,000 years old.

A mammoth-ivory boomerang from Poland is one of the oldest known in Europe, an Obłazowa Cave analysis concluded. A statistical model built on dated bones from the same layer places it between 42,290 and 39,280 years ago.

“We can use the wood to reconstruct the environment and learn more about the amount of time they spent working on tools,” Schroeder stated.

In a recent development, schroeder’s reading of the scene is that a person came to the back of the cave and sorted through the gear piece by piece. Some parts were judged good and others not, and then the person went on their way.

“But that one small act is going to have profound implications in understanding the environment,” he remarked.

Industry observers note that the shelter sits in Presidio County along Alamito Creek, a tributary of the Rio Grande. A spring-fed rock pool, or tinaja, lies in front of it.

The center has worked there since 2019 with the Odyssey Archaeological Research Fund at the University of Kansas. The partners were looking for traces of the region’s earliest people.

The Odyssey fund was established in 2003 with an endowment from Joseph and Ruth Cramer. Its director is Rolfe Mandel, a distinguished professor of anthropology at the university.

In 2020, the excavators uncovered a hearth-like feature with burned and unburned bird eggshells dated to about 15,200 years ago.

In a recent development, “Though this site was pot-hunted in the past, the many large boulders that spalled from the ceiling preserved pockets of shelter deposits,” the Kansas team’s site summary states. Pot-hunting means digging for artifacts to collect.

According to recent reports, the atlatl foreshafts and darts came from intact deposits in an area the excavators call the Sandal Shaft. The Kansas team dates them to about 6,200 years ago, a little younger than the center’s figure.

The pronghorn hide lay about 2 inches (5 centimeters) above them, capped with a metate, a grinding stone.

It has been revealed that the hide yielded a raw radiocarbon age of 5,621 years, plus or minus 24, not yet converted to calendar years. That makes it about 600 years younger than some of the foreshafts and darts, the Kansas researchers report.

The information was obtained from the Center for Big Bend Studies.
