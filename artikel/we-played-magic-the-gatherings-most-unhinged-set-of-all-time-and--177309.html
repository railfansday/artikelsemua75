In a recent development, at MagicCon: Atlanta 2026, Polygon had the opportunity to open some of the weirdest Magic: The Gathering packs in the game’s 33-year history. Mystery Booster Commander Edition brings the chaos of Mystery Booster to the multiplayer Commander format, and it's exclusively available online through limited Festival in a Box releases or at events like MagicCon. Each pack mixes reprints from across Magic history with experimental playtest cards and 60 latest "Rulebreaker" cards that literally break Commander’s typical deck-building restrictions in strange ways by transforming how other cards work. In some cases, they let you add additional colors to your deck. There's also a sorcery that can be your Commander.

It has been revealed that “My goal was that each card would be charming,” principal designer Gavin Verhey told Polygon at MagicCon. “You should see it and you’d be like, ‘That’s crazy. I’ve never seen that before.’ Or, ‘That’s so funny, made me smile.’”. (Related: Should I Quit My Job? A Decision Framework...)

Sources indicate that the charm is absolutely there, and it makes it extremely difficult to pass on some of the set’s weirder cards when they don’t fit your deck’s strategy. Polygon participated in a Mystery Booster Commander Edition draft event. Despite pulling from the same pool of cards, Corey Plante and Jake Kleinman walked away with totally different decks: Corey built a rabbit army around Cadira, Caller of the Small. Jake went for flying creatures and graveyard tricks. Here’s how it went for both of us. (Related: Alaska Mileage Strategy → Cathay Pacific / Qantas to Au...)

Each player is assigned to a pod of eight and given three packs of Mystery Booster Commander Edition, each containing 20 cards. Although each pod has eight players, it's a "Pick 2" draft, meaning you grab two cards out of each pack you see, which speeds up the process and makes it easier to develop your strategy early on.

Industry observers note that jake: I participated in an Unknown event at MagicCon last year, so I was somewhat prepared for this, but it's always overwhelming opening a pack full of chaotic cards from across Magic history and beyond. My first pack had a new legendary creature that was all about Phyrexian synergy, which seemed fun in theory but useless in a draft. Plus I'm not really a Phyrexia guy, so I passed it. I did grab a playtest card called Magus of the Chains as part of an early blue-black graveyard strategy I started developing with lots of discard and mill cards. I also took every flyer in those colors I saw because evasion is extra-powerful in a chaos draft like this one.

Sources indicate that corey: When it came to opening each of the three packs, my primary goal was to claim​​​​​​​ the coolest, rarest cards I could find. I made a few mistakes in prioritizing​​​​​​​ the various playtest cards I came across for their novelty​​​​​​​. Abbot of the Sacred Meeple caught my eye as an unhinged hodgepodge of board game mechanics. But it’s worth almost nothing and is basically unplayable. Massive mistake. Too many of my pulls were defined by grabbing what looked cool.

Jake: I was similarly tempted by a weird little white playtest card called Tax Taker that's not even worth the cardboard it's printed on. At one point, midway through the draft, I also picked up a few powerful white cards and considered pivoting​​​​​​​ to black-white lifegain. My real hope was that I'd find a blue-white-black commander that let me combine all three, but that never happened and I wasn't able to grab much white interaction. In the end I felt like I had a pretty powerful pile of blue and black cards with plenty of card draw and removal to overwhelm my opponents. My two favorite draft picks were Visara the Dreadful and a totally bonkers playtest card called Lich's Duel Mastery (neither of which I ever actually got to play).

Corey: I managed to grab some great, weird cards too! Starting Town NPC is a green creature that gives every creature in your hand a two-cost adventure called Fetch Herbs that gains you two life. And, for some reason, every creature you cast from exile enters with an extra +1/+1 counter on it. My two favorite picks out of the whole draft were Norwood Priestess and Ashaya’s Enduring Bond. For only four mana, Norwood Priestess can tap to put a green creature from your hand onto the battlefield. It feels like the kind of card that can slot into any green deck.

Recent findings suggest that I pulled Ashaya’s Enduring Bond in my third pack. It’s the somewhat infamous new Gruul (red-green) sorcery that can be your Commander. It discovers X where X is the amount of mana spent to cast it, so it actually gets some use out of Commander tax. I pocketed this one so I could build it ASAP.

Jake: I also grabbed one fun card later on purely so I could build a deck around it in the future. Feroz, Ulgrotha's Warden is a Planeswalker that lets you cheat out your own creatures (one of my favorite Commander mechanics) while imposing an extra two-mana tax on everyone else's creature spells. I can't wait to make the rest of my pod suffer.

With the drafting finished, each player had 45 minutes to build a 60 card Commander deck. You could pick one multicolored Commander and grab any two monocolor Commanders​​​​​​​ as if they had Partner. Or, if you somehow only had a Commander in one color, you could just pick a second color for your deck.

Jake: I need to make a confession: I totally misunderstood the rules. In my defense, the event was complicated and could have been better explained by the staff. Then again, nobody else seemed to have any concerns following the rules, so maybe I'm just dumb.

Industry observers note that the Companion app that Wizards uses to organize these ticketed events stated to build a 40-card deck, so that's what I did. I had also assumed, based on the Unknown event I did last year, that we'd be playing one-on-one matches. Instead, this was four-player Commander with 60-card decks. So my deck was totally out of place. Weirdly, this worked to my benefit in game 1 and doomed me in game 2, but more on that later.

Corey: Yeah, I'm not sure where you got that idea, but I'm glad it worked out.

I pulled Norwood Priestess and Emiel the Blessed early, which pushed me toward Selesnya (green-white is a safe space for me). I wound up taking almost all of the green and white cards that came across my playmat.​​​​​​​ Eventually, I breathed a sigh of relief when I picked up Cadira, Caller of the Small. She’s a 3/3 orc that costs three mana (one colorless, one green, and one white)​​​​​​​ with trample that, whenever she deals combat damage to a player, generates a 1/1 white rabbit creature token for each token you control. They quite literally… multiply like rabbits.

In a recent development, I stumbled upon a strong-ish interaction between Emiel the Blessed and Valorous Steed. Emiel can blink your creatures (not good for tokens, actually), but he also lets you pay one green or white mana whenever one of your creatures enters to give them a +1/+1 counter, or two such counters if that creature is a unicorn. Valorous Steed is indeed a unicorn (yay!) that creates a 2/2 white knight when it enters, so in one game I got a strong loop going where Emiel kept blinking Valorous Steed to generate more tokens.

Industry observers note that I also had the flavorful Champions of the Hareish, which depicts an angry-looking rabbit with a carrot sword. He establishes a “Buddy list” where you put a +1/+1 counter on Champion whenever a creature on his Buddy list enters. Creatures entering that don’t qualify allow you to add one of their creature types to the list. The final key piece for me was Intangible Vines.

Jake: My own confusion aside, the deck I built was actually pretty cool. I didn't wind up using any of the weird playtest or Rulebreaker cards as my Commander, though a few made it into the deck. Instead, I paired up Grazilaxx, Illithid Scholar and Rankle, Master of Pranks.

Grazilaxx could get me lots of card draw (especially with all my flyers), while Rankle would let me control the tempo of the game and fill up my graveyard so I could reanimate my scariest creatures later on and swing in for the win. I figured Magus of the Chains would also help with the graveyard strategy, although that card ended up being more trouble than it was worth.

In a recent development, the only thing left to do is shuffle up and play. The Companion app gave everyone a new seating assignment to create a series of four-person groups. (Some people were missing, but the staff worked promptly to make sure everyone had a full pod.) After game 1 the same thing happened again for game 2.

Corey: My first match ended in a stalemate. Everybody mostly built up their board states, but nobody had a powerful win condition.​​​​​​​ This went on until the judges forced us to move on. I was pleased to see that my deck was working as intended here, especially since I was targeted by the table several times.

I was able to play a ramp piece in the early rounds and cast Norwood Priestess on turn three​​​​​​​. The guy to my left literally noted aloud, “Oh that’s gotta go.” Then he destroyed it with some kind of removal. I also got my Commander in ASAP and started building up a modest token army, so she doubled my tokens every time she dealt combat damage to a player. I basically just attacked whoever I could connect with and apologized for it, saying I was just trying to build up some tokens. Those tokens also proved really useful for chump blocking.

Recent findings suggest that what my deck really needed, however, was more token generation and ways to buff and protect Cadira so she could reliably connect in the late-game. My deck kind of stalled over time, but I had enough tokens by then that nobody could touch me. Eventually, the clock just ran out.

Recent findings suggest that jake: My first game went surprisingly well. The archenemy at the table very quickly became a guy who'd built a mono-green deck​​​​​​​ with a pair of squirrels as his partnered Commanders. Meatsqueak, Hoard Lord generated food tokens whenever another creature died, which then buffed all his squirrels, while Toski​​​​​​​, Bearer of Secrets was an indestructible card-draw engine. Toski got even scarier when he played Questing Cosplayer, a playtest card that gives another creature all the abilities of Questing Beast (vigilance, deathtouch, haste, and some other scary stuff). Another guy at my table made a land-focused deck built around Grizzlegom​​​​​​​, Hurloon Hero, which gets lots of cool benefits based on how many lands you have out. It was a cool idea, but he put way too many lands in his deck and got mana flooded.

The squirrel army overwhelmed the rest of us pretty rapidly, and the two other players were quickly knocked out. I thought I was done for​​​​​​​ too, but thanks to my army of flyers and a bunch of removal spells, I was able to clear a path and sneak in for the win. My MVP for that first match was Sigil of Sleep, a one-mana Aura from 1999 that let me bounce his creatures, providing a way to deal with that indestructible Toski​​​​​​​.

Sources indicate that corey: In my second match, I fared even better, coming in at a close second thanks entirely to Intangible Vines hitting the battlefield early. It’s a silly little two-mana white enchantment​​​​​​​ playtest card that’s wickedly powerful for Cadira: All creatures are tokens. That wound up pretty absurd since I also had plenty of smaller nontoken creatures that suddenly counted as tokens. This allowed me to protect myself long enough for the significant threat at the table to take out the other two players before she set her sights on me. By this point, Champions of the Hareish (getting rabbit on his buddy list and churning out rabbit tokens is OP here) had gotten big enough that I was becoming the threat. If I’m being perfectly honest, I didn’t stand a chance since she had several big creatures with interesting abilities and some weird Planeswalker that unleashed its ultimate.

Jake: My second game was a ton of fun. This time the problem promptly became another player who built a deck around Nephilim Epochal, a Rulebreaker card designed to finally give the fan-favorite creature type Nephilim​​​​​​​ its own Commander. Nephilim Epochal lets you search for another Nephilim when it enters, and then each one buffs the rest​​​​​​​ while making it so they can't be blocked by monocolor creatures (which is most other creatures in Mystery Booster Commander). The deck didn't actually have any other Nephilim in it, but it did have a few changeling creatures, which accomplished the same thing. The guy piloting the deck was also a competitive-level player, and he quickly outmaneuvered the rest of us.

It has been revealed that I actually might have had a shot at winning again, but my illegal 40-card deck wound up being my demise. The game went long and at one point, I pulled off a fun combat trick by flashing out Illusory Ambusher, a blue creature that lets you draw cards based on how much damage it takes. I drew 12 cards, inadvertently leaving just five left in my library. After that, the Nephilim focused on taking out the other two players, while I slowly decked myself to death.

According to recent reports, corey: For Magic fans who love the sheer randomness of the draft experience, you’d be hard-pressed to find something more exciting than Mystery Booster Commander Edition. So many of the cards just feel weird and delightful, the playtest cards in particular.

According to recent reports, jake: I totally agree, one of my favorite parts of the whole experience was just reading all the bizarre and hilarious cards that got passed my way during the draft. One upcoming Commander card I haven't even mentioned yet is a blue creature card featuring a pair of bug-men called Sash and Wasitcast, Unmen​​​​​​​ that lets everyone at the table manifest the top card of their library into a 2/2 face-down creature and then rewards them for attacking each other. I also just loved seeing which cool old cards were in these packs, some of which made me feel nostalgic for my time playing Magic in the early 2000s.

Industry observers note that the event itself was also a blast. Everyone I met was super-cool and down to just have fun — even when they found out my deck was technically illegal. I do wish the entire thing had been organized slightly better, but considering this was the first official Mystery Booster Commander, I assume Wizards will work out the kinks of the live event soon.

Corey: My one substantial regret is that I didn’t choose one of the newly introduced Mystery Booster Commander Edition cards as my Commander rather than Cadira, who’s originally from 2022’s Battle for Baldur’s Gate. I also pulled a Feroz, Ulgrotha’s Warden! But he came too late to matter to me since I had absolutely zero blue cards.​​​​​​​ I like how his vibe is about oppressing the rest of the table. He was enticing enough to grab, and I might wind up building a deck around him.​​​​​​​.

Jake: I also pulled a Feroz and thought about building it, but it was too late in the draft. Also, that card is mean!

Recent findings suggest that corey: I’m genuinely excited about pulling Ashaya’s Enduring Bond and have already started building a deck with it as my Commander. The first-ever sorcery Commander? Talk about a fun challenge. I’m thinking of emphasizing abilities with inherent randomness to them, more Discover cards, and effects that copy sorceries.

Jake: I've already put a few cards I picked up from the event into my various Commander decks, and I may still build Feroz to play against my friends at home when I'm feeling extra spicy.
