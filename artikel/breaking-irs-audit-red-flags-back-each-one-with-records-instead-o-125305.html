According to recent reports, few returns get an IRS audit, but some features raise the odds, such as 1099 income missing from the return, deductions large for your income, and business costs that look personal or estimated. Keep receipts, logs, and statements behind those lines as you go, and claim every deduction you can back up.

In a recent development, the IRS audited about 3 of every 1,000 individual returns filed for 2021, the latest year with near-final figures in its Data Book. Filers with $25,000 to $500,000 of income before losses faced about 2 in 1,000, those with $1 million to $5 million about 1 in 100, and those with $10 million or more about 1 in 15.

What’s on the return matters too. The IRS gets a copy of every W-2 and 1099 filed under your name, plus the W-2G for gambling winnings, and matches them against your return, so income left off shows up. Its computers also compare each return with similar ones.

None of these lines is a reason to skip a deduction you’re owed. Each is a reason to keep records behind it. A freelance photographer who deducts all her car and phone costs without logs has only a guess to offer when an auditor asks how much of that use was personal. With a mileage log kept as she drives and a business-use percentage for each, she can prove the business share she deducts.

The IRS’s usual window to audit a return is three years, so keep tax records at least that long, and some longer.

Log each expense and business drive when it happens, not at tax time, since IRS Publication 463 gives a record made at the time more weight than one written up later.
