Presage SmartSpectra SDK Challenge Submission.

This is a submission for the Presage SmartSpectra SDK Challenge.

Most breathing apps assume six breaths a minute suits everyone. Paceprint measures what suits you, from a webcam, and refuses to show numbers it can't trust.

It's for someone like me: a final-year pharmacy student in exam week who has tried a breathing app and wondered whether the pace it prescribes is actually right for them. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

Recent findings suggest that you breathe along with a calm guide at five slow paces (7.0 to 5.0 breaths a minute). Paceprint reads your breathing and your pulse waveform through the SmartSpectra SDK, measures at which pace your heart rhythm and breathing move together most (a dose-response curve: pace is the dose, coupling the response), asks how easy each pace felt, and shows both: measured vs felt. Then it guides a five-minute session at your pace.

The rule that shaped everything: never show a number the data can't support. Move, step out of frame, sit under a flickering light, or lose the pulse waveform, and Paceprint pauses, tells you why, and throws that stretch away. Talking is the one thing it couldn't measure well enough to throw away: the SDK's talking flag fired during silence in our tests, and every fix we tried failed its test. So it warns you live, asks you up front to stay silent, and the result reveals how much of each pace was flagged. (Related: Tech Report: Jimmy John’s reveals Christmas Vacation me...)

It's a wellness tool for self-awareness, not a medical device.

It has been revealed that the result: about 6 breaths a minute, weak peak. The weak label is the app doing its job, because the two measures disagree. My heart rate swung most at 6.0/min (8.4 bpm, against 3.5 to 4.1 at the other paces), but at that pace the swing didn't keep steady timing with my breathing (coupling 0.07). At 5.5/min it did (0.77). A "clear" peak needs four things: it stands out from the paces either side, the three 30-second parts of the measurement agree, at least four paces were read, and coupling at the peak is at least 0.5. This session met the first three and failed the last, so Paceprint called it weak and remarked why. It still names 6.0/min because the ranking weights swing more than coupling.

One possible reason, which I haven't tested: heart rate also rises and falls on its own about six times a minute (a blood-pressure rhythm often called the Mayer wave), so a big swing at exactly 6/min need not come from breathing. This data can't tell the two apart.

In a recent development, felt ease didn't separate the paces either: 6.0, 5.5 and 5.0 all felt "OK" (3 of 5). The card's "Felt easiest 6.0/min" is the first of that three-way tie. Seven breaths a minute and my baseline were not read: the trusted data came only in short fragments, so Paceprint left them out instead of guessing.

Best Thoughtful Real-World Use. A real question (is the "standard" pace right for me?), answered with the person's own data, with consent and honesty as design features: local only, no video stored, no AI services, telemetry off, measured network audit, one-click delete, and a visible refusal instead of a confident guess.

It has been revealed that most Creative or Delightful Use. A webcam becomes a breathing-pace finder: you breathe along with a calm pacer, and the app draws a curve of how your own heart rhythm answered each pace, puts how easy each pace felt beside it, and ends with a share card of your pace.

Industry observers note that resonance-frequency breathing is established in HRV-biofeedback research (Lehrer, Vaschillo & Vaschillo, 2000). Presage's own Coherence by Presage Tech app offers interactive breathwork with pulse and breathing tracking (we could not verify whether it runs a pace sweep), and phone apps such as Outliyr's Coherence, BreathTuner HRV and HRV4Biofeedback find a personal pace from a fingertip on the camera. Paceprint doesn't claim to be first. What it adds is the combination: webcam-only, validity-gated, honest about uncertainty, measured vs felt, consent-first, local, and no AI service.
