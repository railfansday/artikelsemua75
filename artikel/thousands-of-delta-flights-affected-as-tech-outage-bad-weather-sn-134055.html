The Thanksgiving travel rush is off to a messy start.

Industry observers note that the culprit: bad weather and the latest tech outage to hit a major U.S. company. (Related: Industry News: “Determined” woman completes 7 marathons...)

Delta Air Lines delayed or canceled thousands of flights on Sunday after a problem with a key system that automates weight and balance calculations. (Related: University launches $5m smart system that checks who is...)

The disruption led to mass delays at its Atlanta mega-hub — the world’s busiest airport — and cascaded to other airports around the country.

Recent findings suggest that as of 9 p.m. EST Sunday, more than half of all flights had been delayed at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL), according to FlightAware.

Delta remarked it had restored its automated system by late Sunday afternoon. But the disruptions continued to climb as the carrier worked to get operations fully back on track.

And some preemptive cancellations for Monday will affect travelers to start the week, too.

Sources indicate that delta's residual flight delays climbed through the day on Sunday. And cancellations surged late Sunday evening.

As of late Sunday evening, Delta had preemptively canceled around 34 flights scheduled for Monday.

Industry observers note that around 70% of the flight disruptions were due to the tech concerns, the airline had mentioned. The rest were tied to Sunday's weather problems, and air traffic control delays that resulted.

“We thank our customers for their patience and our people for their professionalism," Delta said in a statement to TPG.

Travelers had other problems to contend with Sunday, too.

Mother Nature also snarled air traffic at some major hubs.

Low cloud ceilings and poor visibility fueled flight delays and led to dozens of planes getting diverted.

New York’s LaGuardia Airport (LGA) was most heavily affected, with nearly 60% of all departing and arriving flights delayed Sunday, per FlightAware.

42 flights headed to LGA on Sunday were diverted to other airports, according to aviation analytics firm Cirium.

Many of those flights landed instead in Philadelphia; Albany, N.Y.; Harrisburg, PA; and Syracuse, N.Y.

These disruptions come a week after one of the most chaotic weekends at airports in recent memory.

More than 18,000 flights were canceled last weekend after an outage on Nov. 13 to critical Federal Aviation Administration technology.

Last week, a brief technical outage at United Airlines also fueled a host of flight delays.

In a recent development, what to do if you’re affected: This week, the TPG team put our heads together to offer some of our top tips for navigating a travel meltdown. Here’s what to know.

Recent findings suggest that if your flight is canceled or significantly delayed, you’re entitled to a refund under U.S. Department of Transportation policy — but only if you choose not to fly.

If you get stranded in Sunday’s travel mess, it pays to know what the reason was for the disruption.

In a recent development, if you get stuck because of Delta’s tech outage, the carrier may be willing to pay for any hotel, meal and ground transportation costs you incur — if it’s a major delay. See details at FlightRights.gov.

The carrier on Sunday acknowledged it was providing those accommodations to passengers "when necessary.".

According to recent reports, if you get stuck because of bad weather, it's less likely your airline will offer any sort of compensation. But keep your receipts: If you paid for your trip with a credit card that has travel insurance, you might be able to file a claim and seek reimbursement.

Sunday’s travel woes also coincide, of course, with the start of the Thanksgiving travel rush — with airports expected to be very busy with holiday travelers over the next few days.

Sources indicate that all eyes are on next Sunday — Nov. 29 — which could be the busiest day of the year to fly.

Industry observers note that last year, the Sunday after Thanksgiving set the record as the most crowded day ever at U.S. airports.
