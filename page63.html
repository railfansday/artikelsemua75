Rohingya genocide: What was remarked in court and what happens now?

Myanmar has been found guilty of committing genocide against its Rohingya minority by the International Court of Justice (ICJ).

The verdict marks the first time a state has ever been found to have committed genocide by an international court.

Yuji Iwasawa, president of the UN's top court, said Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya population and engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" to hide its crimes.

A military crackdown in 2017 killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee Buddhist-majority Myanmar, formerly known as Burma. Myanmar denied the claims against it, saying its forces had responded to attacks by armed groups.

Rohingyas at the Cox's Bazar camp in Bangladesh, where around a million refugees now reside, watched the verdict live.

The court found on Thursday that "genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference that can be drawn from the evidence" it had seen.

According to recent reports, the verdict was reached by a majority of nine votes to six among a panel of judges from around the world. The ICJ also found in favour by majority that Myanmar had violated several other parts of the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The case brought by The Gambia centred around so-called "clearance operations" initiated in 2017 in Rakhine State, where many of the Rohingya Muslim minority resided.

After fighters from the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (Arsa) launched deadly attacks on more than 30 police posts there, Myanmar troops, backed by local Buddhist mobs, launched a wave of violence, prompting a mass exodus of Rohingya.

Refugees recounted entire villages being burned down and the mass killing of civilians, while numerous survivors and eyewitnesses provided testimony that the Myanmar military had also raped and abused Rohingya women and girls.

The ICJ determined that around 10,000 Rohingya were "violently killed" during the clearance operation, while more than 700,000 are thought to have fled to Bangladesh by foot or by sea. The court found that approximately 815,000 had been targeted in some way.

Iwasawa remarked Myanmar's military engaged in "indiscriminate" killings, the execution of men, "widespread" sexual violence, "extreme violence" against children and the deliberate burning of Rohingya homes.

He said the military had also attacked Rohingya as they were fleeing to Bangladesh.

Myanmar, he added, had engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" by burning or disposing of bodies, burned and bulldozed sites that were cleared and failed to prosecute those who had perpetrated the acts of violence.

While the ICJ recognised the role Arsa played, it found there was "overlap" between an "anti-Rohingya hate speech campaign" - which portrayed the Rohingya as "sub-human" and "a violent threat" - and the clearance operations that made violence against the population more likely.

Myanmar consistently denied genocide against the Rohingya, saying its operations had targeted militant or insurgent threats in response to the Arsa attacks.

In January, government representative Ko Ko Hlaing told the ICJ that the country "was not obliged to remain idle and allow terrorists to have free reign". He said he had "no comment" following Thursday's verdict.

The ICJ also looked at the wider treatment of Rohingya leading up to the clearance operation and found that policies like limiting married couples to two children were discriminatory and aimed to erode their rights.

It said Myanmar had an obligation to pay proportionate reparations, but said an exact figure could be determined at a later date.

The court's decision was welcomed by Rohingya communities and human rights groups.

"This is like formal recognition of our suffering," indicates Mohammed Nowkim, a Rohingya who was driven out of Rakhine in 2017 and now lives in Canada. "The Rohingya have been waiting for justice for decades.".

Human Rights Watch said the ruling was "long-awaited recognition of the suffering the Rohingya have endured".

To implicate the Myanmar government in genocide, the court had to determine not just that terrible acts had been committed, but that there was an intent on the part of officials or arms of the state to partially or completely destroy the group.

Thursday's ruling is without precedent. Myanmar now carries the stain of being the first state to have been found to have violated the 1948 Genocide Convention.

The ICJ has only twice before ruled on claims of state-backed genocide in the years since the Genocide Convention was signed in the aftermath of the Holocaust, in both cases finding there was insufficient evidence to attribute the actions to the state.

Dozens of individuals have been found guilty of genocide, but finding their actions were at the direct behest of the state requires a higher evidential bar to prove genocide, the gravest of crimes under international law.

Rohingya refugees were seen reacting as the ICJ verdict was broadcast on a big screen in Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh.

Philippe Sands KC, a top human rights lawyer who helped bring the case, described it as an "historic judgment".

He told BBC Radio 4's World at One programme that it was "right up there with the great moments of international justice: Nuremberg, Pinochet, and others", referring to the trials of Nazi leaders following World War Two and that of the Chilean dictator, respectively.

However, the ICJ is not a criminal court and its powers of enforcement are limited. It cannot send military officials to prison, while it relies on the UN Security Council and political pressure to impose its rulings.

The ruling will, though, have implications for other genocide cases pending with the ICJ, including one brought by South Africa against Israel over its war in Gaza.

Sands remarked that pending case had been the "elephant in the room" during the Myanmar hearings.

He added that, while ruling against Myanmar did not mean the court would find similarly with Gaza, if it had not found Myanmar responsible, the case against Israel "would, in effect, be over". Israel indicates the case against it is "wholly unfounded".

ICJ president Yuji Iwasawa noted Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya population.

Antonia Mulvey, executive director of Legal Action Worldwide, told the BBC that the court had not accepted Myanmar's counter-terrorism defence and had found for the first time that rape and sexual violence was an act of genocide, positions it would bear in mind for future cases.

A spokesperson for the US State Department mentioned "the proper interpretation" of the Genocide Convention is of "paramount importance", and so is "preventing politicisation" of the International Criminal Court. "We are studying the decision to fully assess its implications," they said.

They mentioned they share the "grave concerns over the continued abuses of human rights against Rohingya Muslims and members of other minority groups", and added the US is the "leading donor for the Rohingya refugee humanitarian response".

The European Commission has been accused of sugarcoating figures to enable it to release funds to Egypt under a landmark pact with the authoritarian government of Abdel Fatah al-Sisi.

The charge relates to a €7.4bn deal promised by Ursula von der Leyen, the European Commission president, to al-Sisi on a high-profile visit to Cairo in 2024. The three-year “strategic and comprehensive partnership” was intended to bolster Egypt’s shaky economy, deter migrants from coming to Europe and shore up a region reeling from the 7 October attacks and Israel’s war on Gaza.

The disclosure raises broader questions about the EU-Egypt relationship, as Egyptian authorities face scrutiny over shrinking press freedom following the jailing and alleged torture of six journalists, the entire newsroom of Matsadaash, a factchecking and investigative platform, and the closure last month of one of the country’s last independent news outlets and conviction of its high-profile editor, Lina Attalah.

Attalah is appealing against that conviction and will be in court this week as she seeks to overturn other charges including “publishing false news”, for which she was sentenced to a year in prison in absentia without her knowledge.

Recent findings suggest that the spotlight is on €5bn in EU “soft” loans to Egypt – lending with easy repayment terms, known as macro-financial assistance, that is meant to be available only to countries that respect human rights and democracy. While the EU conditions on democracy and rights are vague, the loans impose much more specific requirements on Egypt to carry out economic reforms.

Separate data sources – from leaked EU documents and deleted from an Egyptian government website – raise questions about the European Commission’s claims that Egypt met targets linked to social welfare programmes, which enabled the EU to release €2.5bn in assistance.

In confidential documents, the commission told EU lawmakers that Egypt had “broadly fulfilled” and then “fulfilled” two separate targets to increase the numbers of poor people on social welfare programmes between 2024 and 2026. But data points to a net reduction of 85,734 households as the Egyptian government removed people from its welfare programmes rather than expanded coverage.

If Egypt had expanded the programmes in line with the EU’s apparent conditions – and not removed any households – about 245,000 households, equivalent to roughly 909,000 people, would be getting these benefits. (The Guardian used World Bank data to make this rough calculation).

According to the leaked documents, the commission wanted Egypt to “further expand cash transfer programmes”, but despite a net reduction in beneficiaries it gave the green light anyway to two separate “soft” loans totalling €2.5bn.

In response to the Guardian’s findings, one of the European parliament’s leading spokespeople on EU-Egypt relations, the Dutch MEP Tineke Strik, noted she was introducing a question to the European Commission demanding clarification.

Industry observers note that “The finding by this investigation that the commission is seemingly sugarcoating Egypt’s performance is worrying,” Strik said. “From the beginning, the commission has used macro-financial assistance primarily as a tool to lure Egypt into migration cooperation, while considering formal conditions and its reporting obligations towards parliament as an inconvenient formality.”.

Under the strategy, Egypt has so far received €3.5bn in soft loans in three instalments, with a final payment of €1.5bn expected before the end of 2027.

The charge of sugarcoating figures relates to two social welfare programmes: the Takaful and Karama cash transfer programmes for poor and vulnerable households.

The Takaful (Solidarity) programme gives poor families cash payments as long as recipients have regular health visits and good attendance at school. The Karama (Dignity) programme gives unconditional cash payments to people living with a disability, older people, orphans and widows.

One of the EU conditions for Egypt to gain the first €1bn loan was to increase the number of Takaful and Karama beneficiary households by 100,000 to 4.7 million.

In a leaked report to lawmakers in the EU council of ministers and the European parliament, the commission described that requirement as “broadly fulfilled”.

It has been revealed that the data in that report, however, reveals a net fall of 62,281 households during this period, owing to what the EU officials describe as “a multi-annual recertification process”. Some beneficiaries were added to the list but a larger number were removed because they were deemed ineligible.

In order to achieve a later instalment of €1.5bn, Egypt was required to “further expand” the programmes, adding 60,000 households to the Takaful and Karama beneficiaries list. A second leaked report to EU lawmakers demonstrates that the commission concluded the target was “fulfilled” because 442,118 families were added to the list.

On this occasion, the commission did not report on numbers removed. Figures from Egypt’s ministry of social solidarity, however, show a net reduction of 23,453 beneficiaries to the programmes in the 11 months until June 2026, the period leading up to the release of that payment. The EU unveiled the release of the €1.5bn payment in July 2026.

The data has since been deleted from the Egyptian ministry website but the Guardian has seen screenshots and archived webpages.

During a period from 2024 to 26 when the EU required Egypt to add 160,000 households to the rolls of the Karama and Takaful programmes, there was a net decline of 85,734 households.

For critics, the questions about the EU’s willingness to hold Egypt accountable on very precise socioeconomic targets do not bode well for the broader but less defined goals for democracy and human rights.

Strik said: “The fact that Egypt and the commission even seem to ignore the crystal-clear socioeconomic conditions doesn’t increase my expectation that they adhere to the vague precondition on democracy and the rule of law.”.

Claudio Francavilla, an associate EU director at Human Rights Watch, said: “Without benchmarks, the EU has turned a human rights conditionality into a blank cheque for Egypt’s authoritarianism.”.

It has been revealed that in a statement to the Guardian, the European Commission mentioned the policy target on Takaful and Karama referred to new beneficiary families “and not to its net increase”. The spokesperson said there was “no contradiction” between this statement and that in a confidential document that said Egypt was required to “further expand cash transfer programmes”.

Asked about the release of the remaining €1.5bn in promised loans, the commission remarked it “will be made once all conditions have been met”, referring to 24 economic policy conditions, keeping an IMF programme on track and “taking concrete and credible steps towards respecting effective democratic mechanisms, including a multiparty parliamentary system, and the rule of law, and guaranteeing respect for human rights”.

It added: “Compliance with the conditionality is examined thoroughly” by EU officials and diplomats based on “information provided by the Egyptian authorities and other relevant sources”.

Egypt’s mission to the EU in Brussels did not respond to a request for comment.
