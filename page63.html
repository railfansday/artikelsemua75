📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Deutsch Design restores Snapple's 90s racetrack logo in the Snapple rebrand 2026, rebuilt with custom letterforms and flavor-first packaging.

The change starts with the mark itself. The navy serif Snapple script sits tilted inside a white capsule, ringed in Racetrack Red on a field of Snapple Blue, and the whole thing reads at a glance, on a bottle or on a phone screen. The lowercase blue sans serif from the 2021 rebrand is gone, and with it the flat, generic feel that had crept in over a series of rebrands. What replaces it is warmer and louder: bolder color, higher contrast, and a shape that people who grew up with the brand recognize before they read a word.

According to recent reports, the letterforms were drawn from zero, more geometric and less condensed than the 90s original, with the massive curling S leading the wordmark and the strokes built to hold their weight at any scale. A construction grid reveals the mark measured out at 1X and 4X, and a second spread draws the letters as outlines with their bezier points exposed, annotated with notes on how the curves cue taste appeal. The supporting type is Cygnet CF by Connary Fagan, shown in 18 styles across 9 weights, set huge in magenta over raspberry photography.

According to recent reports, on the bottle, each flavor gets its own world. Snapple Apple bursts across green with sliced apples and leaves, Raspberry Tea goes full magenta with whole raspberries around the bottle, and the ten-bottle lineup shows the range working as one shelf, from Peach Tea to Orangeade. The fruit is shot substantial and close, so the taste feels immediate before a single word is read.

Sources indicate that the palette is specced out in full: Snapple Blue at #10079E, Snapsolutely Blue at #004EE0, Racetrack Red at #E10800, and Zero Sugar White, each with hex, RGB, CMYK and PMS values. Real Tea and Real Juice badges sit on the labels, and the headline says it plain: Sometimes you just want a drink that tastes good.

The rebrand was led by Deutsch Design in Los Angeles, with Adhemas Batista as chief design officer and Erica Park as group design director. See more from Deutsch Design on Behance.

One month after launch, MaPC has shipped 32 versions, added three stores, and run The Witcher 3 and Hogwarts Legacy on Mac.

MaPC introduced in mid-October as a free native Mac app that runs Windows games from Steam, Epic Games and GOG on Apple Silicon, with no Wine setup and no tinkering. Connect a store account, install a game, press Play. DirectX 9, 10 and 11 run through Wine, DXVK and MoltenVK, all set up automatically, with per-game graphics settings, Retina rendering and a performance HUD. The interface takes its colors from each game's cover art, and the library switches between a Magazine view and a Panels view.

The pace since launch has been fast. MaPC is now on version 1.1.32, with 32 releases in the 1.1 line alone. Four of them landed on a single day this week, mostly fixes for Red Dead Redemption 2 and the Rockstar Launcher. Three more stores joined during the month: Amazon Games, the EA app and Ubisoft Connect. Epic cloud saves arrived too, along with controller fixes and a help page for games that may run.

It has been revealed that the response has been the best part. The launch post passed 1,100 likes and 100 replies on Threads, and the game posts kept going: 007 First Light passed 1,200 likes, Hogwarts Legacy passed 700, The Witcher 3 passed 400. The games people played say it well: Guardians of the Galaxy, Red Dead Redemption 2, Batman Arkham Knight, Jedi Fallen Order, Kena, Winds of Viridia, Pragmata, and free demos of Onimusha and Resident Evil Requiem.

Two of the runs are on video: 44 seconds of Batman: Arkham Knight, and Red Dead Redemption 2 at 60 fps.

We will keep improving it, because we at Abduzeedo love to play games and we love our Mac. MaPC is free in public beta for Apple Silicon Macs on macOS 15 and up. Find it at mapc.games, with the full changelog there.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Almost four in five people with chronic migraine have low levels of this vitamin.

Almost four in five people with chronic migraine have low levels of this vitamin.

Frequent headaches are linked to lower vitamin D levels.

People with chronic migraine have lower levels than those with occasional migraines or no headache disorder.

Lower vitamin D is also associated with greater muscle tenderness.

The vitamin helps muscles and nerves function, making it relevant to understanding pain.

One possibility is that low levels amplify sensitivity to pain, although this remains an explanation to investigate.

The research compared around 150 adults, divided equally between chronic migraine, episodic migraine and a group without headache disorders.

Blood tests revealed low vitamin D in 78 percent of the chronic migraine group, compared with 60 percent of those with episodic migraine and 46 percent of the comparison group.

Average levels were lowest in chronic migraine and highest among those without headaches.

Chronic migraine means headaches on at least 15 days a month for more than three months, with migraine features on at least eight days.

These headaches can change in character, even within the same day, and can include pain resembling tension headaches.

Lower vitamin D levels were associated with more tenderness when muscles were pressed.

The researchers suggest that muscular discomfort and changes in pain processing could interact, potentially helping sustain headaches.

However, the association could also run in the opposite direction: people with migraine may spend less time outdoors because of light sensitivity or fatigue.

The findings do not establish that supplements prevent migraines.

Vitamin D’s established functions extend beyond maintaining strong bones.

It helps the body absorb calcium, supports muscle movement and allows nerves to carry messages between the brain and the body.

Deficiency can cause a condition called osteomalacia in adults, involving bone pain and muscle weakness.

The body produces vitamin D when sunlight reaches the skin, while food provides another source.

Oily fish, including salmon and mackerel, are among the best natural sources, while egg yolks contain smaller amounts.

Some breakfast cereals and other foods are fortified with the vitamin.

People who rarely expose their skin to sunlight can have more difficulty obtaining enough.

A blood test measures vitamin D status, helping distinguish a nutritional shortage from symptoms with other possible explanations.

The study was published in the journal Cephalalgia Reports (Prakash et al., 2026).

Here’s a look at what the day is about, plus a few activities that can help kids feel understood, at home or in the classroom.

World Children's Day is a global day of action for children, run by UNICEF. In 2026, it falls on Friday, November 20. This year’s theme is “My day. My rights. My future.” It invites children to tell world leaders what kind of future they want.

The day celebrates children and the rights they’re born with. In many places, children “take over” high-profile roles in media, sports, and government for the day. Landmarks light up blue to show support for children's rights.

November 20 marks two milestones for children’s rights. On this date in 1959, the UN General Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of the Child, a statement of shared ideals. Thirty years later to the day, it adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1989, a treaty that nations can commit to follow.

The Convention sets out the rights every child has, including the right to be safe, to play, and to have their opinions taken seriously. Nearly every country has formally agreed to follow it, and it’s the most widely ratified human rights treaty in history.

Recent findings suggest that some countries do pick dates of their own. Turkey celebrates on April 23, and India on November 14. The dates differ, but the idea is the same: it’s a day dedicated to the youngest members of a community.
