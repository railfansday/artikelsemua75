Sources indicate that MIT researchers have developed a machine-learning framework that guarantees bounded trajectories for AI models of chaotic systems, addressing simulations that can drift into physically impossible values during long runs. MIT revealed the work on November 20, 2026.

The research by graduate student Sunbochen Tang and professors Navid Azizan and Themistoklis Sapsis concerns models of weather, climate, turbulence and other nonlinear processes. Their paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences was published online on October 12, 2026, and appeared in the journal’s October 20, 2026, issue.

Chaotic systems are highly sensitive to initial conditions, so tiny differences can produce dramatically different outcomes. A neural network may match data over a short period and still fail when left to run: small errors can accumulate until its simulated values grow without limit, even though the real system remains bounded. That behavior can invalidate estimates of long-term statistics, a central goal of modeling dissipative chaotic systems.

The guarantee therefore concerns long-term bounded behavior rather than exact point-by-point forecasting far into the future. It seeks to preserve valid long-run statistics without suppressing the complicated variation that makes the system chaotic. As Azizan put it, “Chaos does not mean anything can happen. These systems often remain bounded, and their models should, too.”.

The framework simultaneously learns a dynamics emulator, which predicts how the system evolves, and an energy-like representation. That representation is used to construct an algebraic dissipative constraint inside a projection layer, which adjusts the model’s predicted evolution. Outside a learned boundary, the energy-like quantity must decrease, drawing trajectories toward the bounded region while allowing chaotic behavior within it.

The paper indicates this construction enforces dissipation by design and provides formal trajectory-boundedness guarantees over arbitrarily long forecast windows. The learned energy boundary also supplies an outer estimate of the system’s strange attractor, whose complex geometry makes it difficult to characterize directly.

Tang mentioned soft penalties or incentives applied during training need not hold when a deployed model faces newly introduced inputs, so unbounded predictions remain possible. Arbitrary caps can keep outputs within a chosen limit, but they can also restrict the model’s ability to represent complex features that should be learned from data. The projection layer is modular and model-agnostic, allowing it to work with different neural-network architectures.

In a recent development, MIT reports that the approach closely tracked true outcomes in tests ranging from textbook systems to complex multidimensional cases, while conventional methods moved into impossible predictions. The published demonstrations include Lorenz 96 and a reduced-order model of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation, where the method produced bounded long-horizon forecasts that preserved invariant statistics.

Industry observers note that A related Energy-Constrained Operator paper by Andrea Goertzen, Tang and Azizan was published on June 7, 2026. It introduced a closed-form quadratic projection layer and reported stable long-horizon forecasts that captured invariant statistics for systems governed by the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky and Navier–Stokes equations. Both papers use control-theoretic energy conditions to tackle predictions whose unbounded growth prevents meaningful evaluation of long-run statistics.

Industry observers note that the algorithms and computer code have been deposited on GitHub, and the paper and open-source code are accessible online. The researchers hope other teams will build on the modular layer and test it on larger, more complex problems.

Recent findings suggest that the team plans to test progressively larger systems and data-driven models of realistic weather. Realistic-weather performance therefore remains an open research question: the immediate contribution is a formal limit on runaway simulations, while the next tests must establish how that guarantee affects the usefulness of models’ long-term statistics.

That's because at the heart of it, all LLMs are just fancy next token predictors. An LLM first processes your entire prompt to produce one new token. Then it keeps adding tokens one by one, each time reading everything that came before, until it decides generation is over.

This generation process is computationally expensive: it requires passing the input through billions of parameters for each token generated. To make these models practical for real-world applications, particularly when serving many users simultaneously, researchers and engineers have developed a range of efficient inference techniques.One of the most impactful optimizations is continuous batching, which attempts to maximize performance by processing multiple conversations in parallel and swapping them out when they are done.

To understand how continuous batching works and why it's so effective in high-load serving scenarios, we'll build up from the fundamentals of how LLMs process tokens.

According to recent reports, the attention mechanism is the central piece of how LLMs work. A language model processes text by breaking it down into pieces that we call tokens. We can conceptually think of "tokens" as "words", but sometimes a word might be composed of several tokens. For each token sequence, the network computes a prediction of what the next token should be.

Many operations in the network are token-wise: each token is processed independently, and the output for a given token depends only on that token's content, not on any other tokens in the sequence. Operations like this include layer normalization or matrix multiplication. However, to create connections between words in a sentence, we need operations where tokens can influence each other.

This is where attention comes in. Attention layers are the only place where different tokens interact with each other. Understanding how a network connects tokens together means understanding attention.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, transformers' version v4.0.0rc-1, the initial release candidate for version 4, was released on November 19th, 2020. Five years later, we now release v5.0.0rc-0.

According to recent reports, today, as we launch v5, Transformers is installed more than 3 million times each day via pip - up from 20,000/day in v4 🤯. Altogether, it has now surpassed 1.2 billion installs!

The ecosystem has expanded from 40 model architectures in v4 to over 400 today, and the community has contributed more than 750,000 model checkpoints on the Hub compatible with Transformers, up from roughly 1,000 at the time of v4.

Recent findings suggest that this growth is powered by the evolution of the field and the now mainstream access to AI. As a leading model-definition library in the ecosystem, we need to continuously evolve and adapt the library to continue being relevant. Reinvention is key for longevity in AI.

We’re fortunate to collaborate with many libraries and apps built on transformers, in no specific order: llama.cpp, MLX, onnxruntime, Jan, LMStudio, vLLM, SGLang, Unsloth, LlamaFactory, dLLM, MaxText, TensorRT, Argmax, among many other friends.

For v5, we wanted to work on several notable aspects: simplicity, training, inference, and production. We detail the work that went into them in this post.

The first focus of the team was on simplicity. Working on transformers, we see the code as the product. We want our model integrations to be clean, so that the ecosystem may depend on our model definitions and understand what’s really happening under the hood, how models differ from each other, and the key features of each fresh model. Simplicity results in wider standardization, generality, and wider support.

It has been revealed that transformers is the backbone of hundreds of thousands of projects, Unsloth included. We build on Transformers to help people fine-tune and train models efficiently, whether that’s BERT, text-to-speech (TTS), or others; to run fast inference for reinforcement learning (RL) even when models aren’t yet supported in other libraries. We're excited for Transformers v5 and are super happy to be working with the Hugging Face team!

Transformers, at the core, remains a model architecture toolkit. We aim to have all recent architectures and to be the “source of truth” for model definitions. We’ve been adding between 1 - 3 upcoming models every week for 5 years, shown in the timeline below:.

We’ve worked on improving that model-addition process.

According to recent reports, over the past year, we’ve heavily pushed our modular design as a significant step forward. This allows for easier maintenance, faster integration, and better collaboration across the community.

We give a deeper overview in our Maintain the Unmaintainable blog post. For brevity, we aim to achieve a much easier model contribution process, as well as a lower maintenance burden. One metric we can highlight is that the number of lines of code to contribute (and review), drop significantly when modular is used:.

While we respect the “One model, one file” philosophy, we continue introducing some abstractions making the management of common helpers simpler. The prime example of this is the introduction of the AttentionInterface, which offers a centralized abstraction for attention methods. The eager method will remain in the modeling file; others, such as FA1/2/3, FlexAttention, or SDPA, are moved to the interface.

Over the past couple of years, the increasing amount of 0-day support for new model architectures and standardization of attention handling has helped to simplify our support for post-training modern LLMs.

It has been revealed that we’re building tooling to help us identify which existing model architecture a upcoming model resembles. This feature uses machine learning to find code similarities between independent modeling files. Going further, we aim to automate the conversion process by opening a draft PR for the model to be integrated into our transformers format. This process reduces manual effort and ensures consistency.

According to recent reports, we’ve significantly refactored the modeling and tokenization files. Modeling files have been greatly improved thanks to the modular approach mentioned above, on top of standardization across models. Standardization contributes to abstracting most of the tools that don’t make up a model, so that the modeling code only contains the relevant parts for a model’s forward/backward passes.

Alongside this work, we’re simplifying the tokenization and processing files: going forward, we’ll only focus on the tokenizers backend, removing the concept of “Fast” and “Slow” tokenizers.

We'll use tokenizers as our main tokenization backend, just as we do for PyTorch-based models. We’ll offer alternatives for Sentencepiece or MistralCommon backed tokenizers, which will be non-default but will be supported. Image processors will now only exist with their fast variant, which depends on the torchvision backend.

Finally, we’re sunsetting our Flax/TensorFlow support in favor of focusing on PyTorch as the sole backend; however, we're also working with partners in the Jax ecosystem to ensure we have compatibility between our models and this ecosystem.

With its v5 release, transformers is going all in on PyTorch. Transformers acts as a source of truth and foundation for modeling across the field; we've been working with the team to ensure good performance across the stack.

We're excited to continue pushing for this in the future across training, inference, and deployment.

In a recent development, -- Matt White, Executive Director, PyTorch Foundation. GM of AI, Linux Foundation.

Training remains a massive focus of the team as we head into v5: whereas previously we would focus heavily on fine-tuning rather than pre-training/full-training at scale, we’ve recently done significant work to improve our support for the latter as well.

Supporting pre-training meant reworking the initialization of our models, ensuring that they worked at scale with different parallelism paradigms, and shipping support for optimized kernels for both the forward and backward passes.

Going forward, we’re excited to have extended compatibility with torchtitan, megatron, nanotron, as well as any other pre-training tool that is interested in collaborating with us.

We continue collaborating closely with all fine-tuning tools in the Python ecosystem. We aim to continue providing model implementations compatible with Unsloth, Axolotl, LlamaFactory, TRL and others in the PyTorch ecosystem; but we are also working with tools such as MaxText, in the JAX ecosystem, to have good interoperability between their frameworks and transformers.

All fine-tuning and post-training tools can now rely on transformers for model definitions; further enabling Agentic use-cases through OpenEnv or the Prime Environment Hub.

We’re putting a significant focus on inference for v5, with several paradigm changes: the introduction of specialized kernels, cleaner defaults, fresh APIs, support for optimized inference engines.

Similarly to training, we’ve been putting some effort in packaging kernels so that they’re automatically used in case your hardware and software permits it. If you haven’t heard of kernels before, we recommend taking a look at this doc.

Alongside this effort, we ship two newly introduced APIs dedicated to inference:.

According to recent reports, we see this as a major step forward for use-cases such as evaluation, where a great number of inference requests are done simultaneously. We don’t aim to do specialized optimizations like the dedicated inference engines (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT LLM). Instead, we aim to be perfectly inter-compatible with these, as detailed in the next section.

The Transformers backend in vLLM has been very enabling to get more architectures, like BERT and other encoders, available to more users. We've been working with the Transformers team to ensure many models are available across modalities with the best performance possible. This is just the start of our collaboration: we're happy to see the Transformers team will have this as a focus going into version 5.

-- Simon Mo, Harry Mellor at vLLM.

Standardization is key to accelerating AI innovation. Transformers v5 empowers the SGLang team to spend less time on model reimplementation and more time on kernel optimization. We look forward to building a more efficient and unified AI ecosystem together!

Recent findings suggest that recently, we've been working hand in hand with the most popular inference engines for them to use transformers as a backend. The value added is significant: as soon as a model is added to transformers, it becomes available in these inference engines, while taking advantage of the strengths each engine provides: inference optimizations, specialized kernels, dynamic batching, etc.

We've also been working very closely with ONNXRuntime, llama.cpp and MLX so that the implementations between transformers and these modeling libraries have great interoperability. For example, thanks to a significant community effort, it's now very easy to load GGUF files in transformers for further fine-tuning. Conversely, transformers models can be easily converted to GGUF files for use with llama.cpp.

The Transformers framework is the go-to place for reference AI model implementations. The framework plays a crucial role in enabling modern AI across the entire stack. The team and the community behind the project truly understand and embrace the spirit of the open-source development and collaboration.

The same is true for MLX, where the transformers' safetensors files are directly compatible with MLX's models.

It has been revealed that it’s hard to overstate the importance of Transformers (and datasets, tokenizers, etc) to the open-source and overall AI ecosystem. I can’t count the number of times I’ve personally used Transformers as a source-of-truth.

It has been revealed that finally, we’re pushing the boundaries of local inference and are working hand-in-hand with the executorch team to get the transformers models to be available on-device. We’re expanding the coverage to multimodal models (vision, audio) through optimum.

Quantization is quickly emerging as the standard for state-of-the-art model development. Many SOTA models are now released in low-precision formats such as 8-bit and 4-bit (e.g., gpt-oss, Kimi-K2, Deepseek-r1), hardware is increasingly optimized for low-precision workloads, and the community is actively sharing high-quality quantized checkpoints. In v5, we're making quantization a central focus of Transformers support, ensuring full compatibility with all major features, and delivering a reliable framework for training and inference.

We introduce a significant change to the way we load weights in our models; and with this, we move to quantization being a first-class citizen.

Our collaboration with the Transformers team was highly productive, marked by their proactive code reviews, feedback, and technical expertise. Their support was crucial in integrating TorchAO, expanding quantization features, and improving documentation for broader adoption in the V5.

In a recent development, we're excited that v5 has made quantization a first-class citizen. It provides the foundation for bitsandbytes to better support key features like TP and MoEs, and also makes it easier to integrate upcoming quantization methods.

-- Matthew Douglas & Titus von Koeller, bitsandbytes.

The overarching theme of this version 5 release is “interoperability”. All refactors, performance improvements, and standardization are aligned with this theme. v5 plays nicely and end-to-end with the growing ecosystem: train a model with Unsloth/Axolotl/LlamaFactory/MaxText deploy it with vLLM/SGLang, and export it to llama.cpp/executorch/MLX to run locally!

Version 5 is undeniably an accomplishment of the past five years by a very large number of people in our community. We also see it as a promise, and as a beacon of the direction we want to go.

We took it as an opportunity to clean up the toolkit and isolate what mattered; we now have a clean slate on top of which to build. Thanks to the many changes from the community and team, improvements in performance, usability, and readability, will be simpler to ship.

Now that v5.0.0's first RC is out there, we'll be eagerly awaiting your feedback. Please check our release notes for all the technical details, and we'll be awaiting your feedback in our GitHub issues!

My position is that AI becomes harmful when the institution uses it to remove friction from a process whose friction was doing useful work.

Em 20 de novembro, a recomendação era instalar a 19.4.10: as versões 19.2.10 e 19.3.9 corrigem esta CVE, mas não a CVE-2026-132825. Sources indicate that as linhas 19.5 e 19.6 estão fora da faixa afetada por esta vulnerabilidade; os builds indicados para elas eram 19.5.8 e 19.6.1. Já quem usa a linha 19.1 ou anterior precisa considerar a atualização para a 19.4.10, seguindo as etapas de upgrade exigidas, pois versões antigas continuam expostas a outras falhas, incluindo as CVEs-2026-105437 e 2026-132825.

A escolha do build também importa porque os repositórios da distribuição Linux não incluem essas correções do GitLab. O código corrigido está nos repositórios de pacotes do próprio GitLab, na imagem Docker oficial ou no gráfico Helm oficial; instalar ou atualizar somente o Ruby fornecido pelo Ubuntu, Rocky Linux ou outra distribuição não corrige o Ruby embutido.

Verifique a versão do GitLab, e não apenas a versão do Ruby do sistema. Em uma instalação Omnibus, a tarefa de ambiente, o arquivo VERSION e o gerenciador de pacotes mostram o build em uso. O exemplo abaixo consulta esses três locais em um servidor Ubuntu:.

O arquivo VERSION pode não terminar com uma quebra de linha, então a saída pode se juntar à do comando seguinte sem que isso indique uma versão diferente.

Recent findings suggest that O Ruby empacotado pelo GitLab pode continuar aparecendo como 3.3.12 mesmo depois de uma atualização corrigida, porque o GitLab aplicou a correção à cópia embutida sem alterar a versão exibida. A equipe Ruby relacionou a falha ao Onigmo e indicou correções em Ruby 4.0.8, 3.4.12 e 3.3.13; instalações a partir do código-fonte e imagens personalizadas também precisam atualizar e reconstruir o Ruby.

Faça a busca antes da atualização, que reinicia Puma e Workhorse, e antes que a rotação apague registros antigos. As detecções publicadas pelo GitLab incluem respostas 502 ou badgateway em CI lint e GraphQL, rejeições e tempos limite do RegexpMatcher, caminhos não ASCII muito longos, falhas do Puma com quadros Onigmo e encerramentos de workers Sidekiq após o envio de um .gitlab-ci.yml.

Industry observers note that O Workhorse pode oferecer o sinal mais forte: uma falha de worker Puma pode aparecer como resposta 502 nos endpoints usados pelo ataque. Estes comandos contam três indicadores nos logs Omnibus: erros de CI lint ou GraphQL, mensagens do RegexpMatcher e despejos de falha do Puma.

sudo grep -E '"uri":"[^"]*(ci/lint|/api/graphql)' /var/log/gitlab/gitlab-workhorse/current | grep -cE '"status":502|badgateway' sudo grep 'RegexpMatcher' /var/log/gitlab/gitlab-rails/application_json.log | grep -cE 'rejected at compile time|match timed out|time budget exceeded' sudo grep -c '[BUG]' /var/log/gitlab/puma/puma_stderr.log.

According to recent reports, uma contagem positiva deve ser tratada como possível incidente. Se o cadastro estava aberto e havia projetos públicos, examine também logs arquivados e contas criadas no período. Em Docker, procure os mesmos arquivos no diretório do host montado em /var/log/gitlab.

The CUGA architecture begins with the user's message flowing into a chat layer that interprets intent and constructs the user's goal, based on context. A task planning and control component then decomposes this goal into structured subtasks, tracked programmatically through a dynamic task ledger. 📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A meeting in Oulu rarely stays in one language.

Sources indicate that two people start in Finnish, someone joins from Berlin and switches to English, and one sentence goes to Turkish because that is the word that fits. Everyone in the room understands. The recording is fine. The note is where it falls apart: if the note keeps the language it was spoken in, the person reading it at 21:00 reads three languages in one document.

Gistsmith separates two things that usually travel together: what was spoken, and what you read.

Sources indicate that the note language is yours to pick. It does not follow the first speaker, the loudest person, or the system language. You set it once, and the note comes out in that language, whatever the room did.

Industry observers note that the numbers as they stand today: you can speak in 35 languages, notes and live translations come in 37, and the app itself speaks 12 — English, Turkish, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese (Brazil), Dutch, Japanese, Korean, Chinese (Simplified) and Finnish. The interface follows your Mac's language. The note language is a separate choice.

The obvious alternative is a per-meeting default: look at the first five minutes, take the dominant language, use it for the note.

According to recent reports, it demos well and fails in real use, because the dominant language of a meeting is a property of the room. The person who needs the note at the end of the day is usually not the one who spoke most in the call.

Industry observers note that A per-meeting default also asks a question at the worst possible moment: right after the call, when nobody wants to answer anything. One setting asks nothing.

There is a real trade-off: a phrase in the original language often is the point. "Se on ihan sama" carries a tone that "it doesn't matter" flattens into nothing.

Recent findings suggest that I have not found a rule that keeps every phrase and still gives you a readable document in one language. Choosing a note language buys readability and pays for it in tone. That is a trade I would make again: a note you skim before the next call has to be readable first.

According to recent reports, the mismatch I did not expect was between the two languages a person uses. Almost nobody changes the language they read in by accident. Almost everybody changes the language they speak in, depending on who is in the room.

Fix the reading side, make it boring, and the writing side can stay as messy as the meeting was.

If you are building anything that turns speech into a document, the question is not only "which language is this?". It is also "which language does this need to be read in?". Those are two settings, and only one of them belongs to the user.
