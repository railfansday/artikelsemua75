📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that there are a million ways to make patties juicier, from mixing in grated butter to even marinating your burgers. But if you're looking for something a bit outside the box that doesn't complicate the recipe too much, stuffing the patties with Velveeta can give you some seriously cheesy, moisture-rich results.

Industry observers note that while it may sound odd, this recipe is actually a variation of a Juicy Lucy cheeseburger, a Minnesota staple dating back to the 1950s. The idea is that by trapping Velveeta between two thin patties, the cheese melts into a liquid center that mingles with the rendered meat juices, adding moisture to every bite. If done correctly, the cheese should settle back into a gooey state while the patties rest, helping ensure that you don't burn your mouth on molten dairy on your first bite.

According to recent reports, while you can always stuff your burgers with other cheeses, Velveeta has one major advantage. Thanks to its emulsifying and stabilizing agents, it's less likely to become greasy as it melts. Other meltable cheeses can release fat as they heat up, which isn't a problem when it can drip onto a grill but can create problems when you leave it no room to escape. Plus, the fact that Velveeta comes in blocks makes it extremely easy to shape to fit your patties, no matter how large or small you prefer them.

The classic Juicy Lucy burger may seem a little daunting, but the process is actually quite simple. Trapping the cheese between the patties and ensuring that all your ingredients and, more importantly, your grill are at the right temperatures are all you really need to worry about for something crisp, delicious, and juicy.

Recent findings suggest that starting with one part Velveeta to four parts beef is your best bet for this recipe. It's enough cheese to achieve that gooey center but not so much that it'll escape and ruin the integrity of your patties. It's also best to keep your patties flat rather than thick, which makes it much easier to pinch the seams and trap the Velveeta between them. Velveeta is easier to work with when cold, but keeping your beef cold is also a major benefit for your burger. Cold beef fat is less likely to smear and instead stays in little pockets that help keep the meat even juicier.

According to recent reports, grilling your burgers at the right temperature helps you achieve a good crust without excessive moisture loss, and it's doubly crucial when you stuff the patties. You want enough heat to char the exterior but not so much that the patties finish cooking before your Velveeta has the chance to melt. Around 400 degrees Fahrenheit is a good sweet spot. It may be useful to keep one side of your grill cool, letting indirect heat finish any patties whose exteriors have cooked too quickly.

Tech Report: MCP Gateway: How Snap Checks Both the Person and the Agent

📅 23 Nov 2026 | ⏱️ 11 min read (2177 words, 📚 Deep Dive) | Source: Snap

💡 Key Takeaways

  • In a recent development, this post describes Snap’s MCP Gateway, the shared path through which Snap employees and their AI agents reach internal tools.
  • Industry observers note that AI agents call tools through Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

MCP Gateway: How Snap Checks Both the Person and the Agent.

In a recent development, this post describes Snap’s MCP Gateway, the shared path through which Snap employees and their AI agents reach internal tools. When an agent acts on behalf of a person, a request to a restricted tool must be allowed for both.

Industry observers note that AI agents call tools through Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers. One of the first MCP servers we tested worked fine locally. When we hooked it up to a central MCP gateway, the call failed as forbidden. The receiving service's logs demonstrated why: the caller was the MCP's service account, not the engineer. In another test, an agent on a remote machine opened a PR for an engineer, but the PR looked as though the agent's GitHub app had opened it instead of the engineer. Identity didn't make it all the way through the call chain.

Most internal systems assume a human sits at a browser with valid credentials, using the service's UI. Agents break those assumptions. If you can no longer assume a human is on the other end of an interaction, a lot has to change.

Industry observers note that at Snap, we've built out a central MCP Gateway for employees and their agents. It fronts more than 60 MCPs and over 1,100 tools, with more coming online every week. In the week ending October 11, 2026, people and Casper made about 1.6 million tool calls through it. On top of that, automated workloads running as service accounts make several million more calls a week.

We settled on an initial rule. When an agent acts for a person, the call has to pass as both the person and the agent. As we added MCP support, we built only the parts Snap needs to make that work, like identity, credentials and policy.

Industry observers note that through the MCP Gateway, employees use Casper to triage Jira tickets, check A/B experiments, analyze data and summarize documents. This summer, a data platform team created a new MCP, had it live on the gateway within a week, and got Casper access alongside it. Once a new MCP is on the gateway and its owner opts in, Casper can use it on a person's behalf without per-agent integration work. Casper is still limited by both that person’s access and its own tool grants.

Industry observers note that in November 2025, we demoed a workflow that took a Jira ticket and opened a GitHub pull request, all without leaving the IDE, in about 7 minutes. It sounds a little quaint now, but people previously did all of those steps by hand. An engineer went into a ticket, copied out context, pasted it into their AI agent window, and cut a PR via the UI or CLI.

The first version was built for people, not agents, and that was on purpose. We didn't have many agents yet, and we wanted MCP to be as convenient as possible for the people using it. Even making it work for people was tricky, because we had to pass the person's identity all the way down.

In a recent development, initially, we made approved MCPs available via a local proxy, which we called MCP Proxy, that knew how to authenticate to internal Snap services. At the time, most MCPs also had to run as local Docker containers. Logins for OAuth-backed MCPs like Jira and Slack expired about daily.

Industry observers note that while this worked well for a small set of MCPs, it got more difficult as we reached 10+. No one wanted to run or manage that many containers for day-to-day work, and onboarding could be difficult for folks who were less comfortable with the command line.

In a recent development, we wanted to move to a hosted model where we could front all the MCPs through a central API, ship updates continually, and get better visibility into and control over what agents could do. We also expected that people would want to run agents in the cloud, and those agents would need a deployed surface.

Recent findings suggest that we considered building our own, adopting an open-source service, finding a vendor, or extracting MCP Proxy into a central service. Of the options, Docker MCP Enterprise Gateway felt closest to what we had in mind. We met with Docker, became design partners, and started figuring out how to integrate the MCP Gateway they were building into Snap's service mesh, the internal network layer that handles identity and routing between our services. Docker built the gateway core.

Calls carry the identity of whoever started them.

Industry observers note that once the gateway was deployed, we needed to know who was calling and what they could do. Authentication internally operates on a service-to-service basis. If Service A calls Service B, which calls Service C, C can see that B called it, but C does not know anything about A. Service-to-service auth loses the person who started the call, and it's easy to discover that when something that worked locally now fails as forbidden.

When a person called from their own machine, the gateway knew it was them and could pass that down. An agent adds one more hop, and we had no trusted way to convey it. The caller became one agent identity standing in for everyone using that agent, and that wasn't the person performing the action.

Sources indicate that each agent has its own machine identity, a service account registered as that agent. A registered agent starts with a set of read-only default permissions. Anything more comes from grants. Granting an agent a destructive tool takes approval from both the agent's own team and the tool's owner.

Agents act as themselves unless given permission to act on a person's behalf, as with Casper and a small set of allowlisted agents.

Developer Productivity partnered with Snap Security to build an Agent Attestation Token (AAT) system. An AAT carries the original requester, whether a person or another agent, and the agent making the call. Only a few trusted services, such as Casper, can get a token that names someone else as the requester, and each one checks who the requester is first. For a request that starts in Slack, for example, we verify Slack's signature on the message and look up who sent it before the token is minted. The gateway reads the token, attributes the call to the original requester, and forwards it.

In a recent development, when the MCP Gateway receives a call, it uses our internal authorization service to check access for the caller. If a person is calling through an agent, it checks both for restricted tools. For example, an agent may have permission to call an MCP restricted to one team, but if the person using that agent is not on that team, the call is denied. It works the other way too. An agent can't use everything the person can, only the tools it was granted.

Recent findings suggest that the gateway provides a central place to audit. We record tool calls, authorizations and denials so we can trace what happened.

In the original design, we considered keeping OAuth tokens in the gateway or in our secrets store. Security proposed building per-user OAuth into a pre-existing token management service, the Auth Translation Service (ATS). For servers behind the gateway, third-party credentials live in ATS, owned by Security, instead of in the gateway itself or in the MCP servers.

According to recent reports, keeping tokens in the gateway would also have meant building a separate credential system, with its own OAuth integrations and token rotation. ATS already did that. People authenticate once, and ATS refreshes credentials on their behalf where the provider allows it.

Write access is earned one tool at a time.

How do you safely support read and write operations for agents? It takes layers. The platform should do the right thing by default, Security reviews the most sensitive services, and service owners make the final call on what agents may do with their service.

Industry observers note that early on, the GitHub MCP was read-only for code, issues and pull requests. Our first write-capable MCP was the open-source Jira server. GitHub pull request writes and Slack posting followed.

Sources indicate that reads need care too. Prompt injection isn't fully solved, and text an agent reads from one tool can steer what it does with the next. An agent that could read everything its person can read would be an attractive target. So an agent doesn't simply inherit the person's access. It gets the tools it was granted, the person's own access still caps what it can reach when it acts for that person, and the audit record shows what it did. A tool nobody has classified can't be granted.

Industry observers note that even within an MCP, we may want to segment some tools as more sensitive. If a service exposes a get, a create and a delete API, maybe most people can have the get. Maybe the create needs more control, or the delete shouldn't be exposed at all. We needed role-based access control (RBAC) for each tool. We classify each tool as read or write, flag destructive writes, and decide per MCP how sensitive its data is.

For people, MCP access mostly mirrors what they can already do, because the service behind an MCP still applies its own per-user checks.

Industry observers note that for agents, service owners grant permissions through a PR-driven system. If someone wants their agent to call tools X, Y, and Z, they create a pull request (PR), and an approver listed for those tools must approve it. Once it merges, the agent can access the upcoming tools.

Recent findings suggest that for example, when one team's on-call agent needed A/B data for a weekly report, its PR added six read-only tools and nothing else. Since the first agent grant in August 2026, we've granted over 60 agents tools beyond their default permissions.

According to recent reports, so why not just give every agent its own API keys? An on-call agent might need logs, metrics, source code and tickets. Setting up a separate relationship with each of those services is a burden most teams won't take on, and the next agent would have to do it again. Through the gateway, the agent's owner asks once in a PR, and each service owner approves their own tools. The agent never gets the keys or secrets those services need.

At first, we tried to build the MCP servers centrally. That did not scale. Beyond the question of ownership (should someone else be exposing a tool for your service?), maintenance and incentives weren't aligned and created a bottleneck.

A central platform team manages service provisioning, service account creation, deploy pipelines and shared libraries. MCP owners are responsible for their tool code and, ultimately, the operation of their MCP. Today, most MCPs belong to the team that runs the corresponding receiving service.

The same tools everywhere. We're making the MCP Gateway the default. A workflow that works on your machine should work the same everywhere.

A person in the loop for riskier operations. On top of today’s grant approvals, some actions might need to pause and ask the human to confirm before they run ("are you sure you want to do that?"). MCP elicitation offers a way to do that, and the latest MCP spec supports it without needing stateful requests.

Checks on combinations of capabilities. If an agent can access two non-sensitive MCPs and wants access to a sensitive one, that combination of permissions may be too risky. Central enforcement could look at the combination, not just each tool.

The call that failed as forbidden was our first sign that internal systems assumed a person on the other end. Most of what we built since then answers two questions on every call: who is asking, and through which agent.

Build identity early. The plumbing that made the gateway work for an individual, like one endpoint, central credentials and identity passed all the way down, is still what an agent needs.

It has been revealed that proposal for people outside engineering. If they need MCP, and they probably will, think about their ergonomics early. They may become some of your biggest people.

Carry the agent's identity as well as the person's. You want to know both who was on the other end and which agent they were working with.

Keep user tokens out of the gateway and the MCP servers. If you already have a token service, use it, and keep the friction for users low.

According to recent reports, let owners build their own servers. A central team can't build every one.

Sources indicate that every tool costs context. Agents pay for each tool's description in the model's context, so load tools on demand.

The maple-flavored sweetness brings a delightful dimension to the classic sandwich that is lip-smackingly scrumptious and the perfect foil for its salty heartiness.

Adding a packet of Mickey D's honey is another sweetening twist that gives your McMuffin honey ham flair. The honey is actually offered as a dipping sauce option for Chicken McNuggets, but no diehard Golden Arches diner will let that stop them from drizzling it onto a McMuffin for added sweetness.

Can Artificial Intelligence Predict the Right Partner for You?

📅 26 Nov 2026 | ⏱️ 8 min read (1546 words, 📚 Deep Dive) | Source: Berkeley

💡 Key Takeaways

  • It has been revealed that the promise of AI is to go deeper and end the days of endless swiping and unreturned messages.
  • As a relationship scientist, I see both peril and promise in this new generation of dating apps.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Finding love is significant business (a multi-billion–dollar industry), and artificial intelligence is transforming that business. Dating apps are increasingly outsourcing user matching to AI. Recent examples range from Overtone, an AI-powered matchmaking service started by the former chief executive officer of Hinge, to SciMatch, a dating app that aims to use AI to detect compatibility from a single selfie. But can AI actually help people find love?

It has been revealed that the promise of AI is to go deeper and end the days of endless swiping and unreturned messages. Now people can talk to AI about their hopes, dreams, and ideal partner preferences and AI can scan the pool of dating app people to find candidates who fit those criteria exactly. AI can also track behavior in the app to learn what people really want (something behavioral scientists call revealed preferences).

As a relationship scientist, I see both peril and promise in this new generation of dating apps. Despite long-heralded promises by dating app companies that their apps are based on relationship science, the truth is that there is no strong foundation of science upon which to build these matching algorithms—relationship scientists do not know what makes two particular people compatible with each other. Not yet, anyway. Sure, we know some of the factors that generally predict liking—physical attractiveness, positive personality traits, similarity, familiarity, reciprocity—but we have yet to predict with the kind of certainty dating apps might like to claim whether any two people will hit it off before they even meet.

So if a robust science of compatibility doesn’t exist yet, what is the AI matching based on? One of the first AI dating apps, Fate, uses “similarity and reciprocity of personality,” according to an interview with the founder. That sounds reasonable, and research from Amanda Montoya and her colleagues looking across 313 different studies of similarity and attraction does suggest that similarity may predict attraction—before people meet. Once people experience even a brief interaction, similarity has much less, if anything, to do with how much two people like and are attracted to each other, as seen in an analysis of actual and perceived similarity of personality traits in potential partners, by Sarah Humberg and her colleagues. A systematic inquiry from my research lab, led by Annika From, also yielded little evidence that romantic couples are happier if partners are more similar to each other.

What about “reciprocity of personality”? If this phrase refers to the idea that two people may have complementary personalities that meld well together, this theory of compatibility, unfortunately, has even less empirical support.

Sources indicate that perhaps it is not too concerning that we lack a strong, agreed-upon scientific basis for predicting compatibility. After all, one of the great promises of AI is that it will learn as it goes, based not just on what a user claims they want, but how they actually behave (who they swipe right on, whom they actually chat with after matching). Emerging evidence from different fields suggests that AI may be able to detect complex patterns that humans have been unable to discern. In the medical field, AI has been used to create more precise and accurate predictions that are aiding diagnosis and treatment. From weather prediction to fraud detection, the use of AI has enhanced forecasting. So maybe it can do the same for our love lives?

In a recent development, if the problem has simply been that we have not been able to identify the complex patterns that make up human compatibility, then AI may, in fact, be the answer. If, however, the problem is that humans themselves are too complex, then AI may not get us any closer to being able to forecast love.

What do I mean by this? Well, researchers have found that people will list their preferred partner traits before taking part in a speed dating event, only to have all those preferences go out the window when they actually interact with people. In my own work, I have discovered that people will dismiss another user’s dating app profile for reasons like “same name as my brother” and “bad haircut.” Yes, they will rule someone out as the possible love of their life because of something as irrelevant as their name (give them a nickname!) or hairstyle (arguably our most changeable physical characteristic).

Why do I bring up these examples? They raise the question of whether AI even has the right information to rely on when gathering data based on people’s stated preferences or swiping behavior. So what data might AI need? In my lab, we hope to find some answers to this question with a series of studies that aim to combine dating-app data with real-life interactions. This project will allow us to see what chemistry looks like online and offline, uncover who has online chemistry that translates into real-world sparks, and identify the factors that make chemistry more likely between two people (if there are any meaningful predictors).

The truth is, researchers at the forefront of this work do not always agree on whether forecasting love is even possible. While many relationship scientists have devoted their careers to testing the complex ways in which personalities and preferences interact to predict compatibility, they have arrived at different conclusions. Some researchers believe it will be possible to build an algorithm to predict compatibility; these researchers would likely see the promise in AI. On the other hand, there are those who have concluded that compatibility is a dynamic process that evolves as two people interact with each other and cannot be predicted ahead of time. For these researchers, finding love is less about the perfect match and more about being in the right place at the right time and then building love together through shared moments, from special nicknames and in-jokes to regular rituals just the two of you share.

This piece is part of a three-year GGSC project to explore love's role in promoting social well-being, bridging divides, and contributing to a good, meaningful life.

It has been revealed that with the research projects in my lab, we hope to test these theories by having people meet different numbers of times. We’ve seen from our early work that pairing two random people together does not typically produce high levels of chemistry. That instant attraction seems to be relatively rare; but what if they met repeatedly? Could chemistry develop? Does it need some sort of initial spark to set it off, and can we create that spark with the right equipment, such as a well-placed question or activity that brings two people together? In other words, is chemistry possible with anyone if the right conditions were met, or is there some, as yet undiscovered, set of requirements for two people to be able to fall in love?

I don’t know the answer to these questions yet, nor does the field of relationship science, although many have theories. I see AI as both an exciting tool that might help us answer this question as well as a technology that people looking for love should employ with caution. When used as a way to explore and test models of compatibility, AI may yield invaluable insights. But we must always remember that AI is trained with specific beliefs about what makes two people compatible. If researchers do not yet know what makes two people compatible, then letting AI do all the work to vet potential partners means you are only going to see the people that AI thinks are right for you, while likely remaining in the dark about how AI decided they’re a good match. This gap in knowledge may keep you from meeting someone unexpectedly compatible with you.

An alternative approach to dating is to think about it as a time for exploration. Instead of only connecting with people who have similar facial structures or reciprocal personalities, try dating people with all kinds of different experiences, personalities, beliefs, and desires. If you go this route, Paul Eastwick offers a valuable piece of advice in his recent book Bonded by Evolution: for those who have serious dealbreakers (e.g., you know you want children, have strong religious views, or do not eat meat), go ahead and surround yourself with people who share those views. This will help you avoid facing potentially difficult lifestyle tradeoffs in the future if you do find yourself falling in love. But don’t eliminate someone just because of a bad haircut or a shared name. Instead, be open to exploring and then see for yourself who you connect with and who you do not. You might end up being surprised.

None of this is easy, of course. Along the way, tough situations crop up, and it’s hard to know what to do. Here are common questions, with answers backed by scientists and their research.

Industry observers note that Q: My 16-year-old doesn’t seem to be motivated to do anything beyond hanging out with friends and playing video games. When I bring up the prospect of college, he says he doesn’t care. What should I do?

According to recent reports, A: As parents, we want to set our high schoolers up for success. But, at that age, young people are still trying to figure out who they are, what interests them, and what their future looks like. “Identity development and purpose formation are underway, but rarely fully formed by high school,” indicates Kendall Cotton Bronk, professor of psychology at Claremont Graduate University.

Sources indicate that not finding any answers yet is expected. “Parents need to remind themselves that where their child is at this moment in time isn’t necessarily where they will be in the future,” states Bronk.

To help teens along, encourage them to try different activities at school and in the community, whether it’s, for instance, robotics, the debate team, chorus, improv, or the school paper. Leave it to them to select what to try. Don’t do it for them. Don’t judge their choices.

According to recent reports, “While it’s not appropriate for parents to be too directive, they can help their teenager reflect on what they liked and didn’t like and what felt meaningful and not so meaningful about their various experiences,” says Bronk. “Having someone to debrief with can help them deepen their reflection and more quickly identify a purposeful direction.”.

It has been revealed that research suggests that when teens are guided by their own interests, free of external pressures, they’re more likely to take charge of their own career development. But when parents are overly involved in their adolescents’ lives, says Bronk, young people don’t have an opportunity to cultivate a sense of agency, and this can make career development planning a big challenge.

Industry observers note that instead of trying to manage their path, says Bronk, parents should simply “be curious.” Ask your child: What interests you? What feels satisfying for you?

“This kind of questioning can scaffold and deepen their young person’s reflections, and reflection is a key component of figuring out what kind of path a young person wants to follow,” she claims. “Usually, what holds their interest is something they’re good at and care about, and that allows them to make a meaningful difference in the broader world,” she explains.

Taking advantage of volunteer opportunities can help young people see themselves as useful and discover careers that might interest them—for instance, helping out in a food pantry, animal shelter, a kids sports organization, or hospital. Encourage your teen to ask people he knows about their jobs, what they entail, and what they studied to get there.

Finally, don’t assume that your child’s lack of direction is apathy. Whatever their interest is, “work with it, not against it,” says Bronk. “At the same time, be patient. It may just be the case that they haven’t figured it all out yet. Their timetable may not be the same as yours, but they will eventually get there. Remember, this isn’t a race.”.

It has been revealed that Q: My 15-year-old seems to be living a separate life. He spends most of his time in his room and has dinner more often with his friends than with us. How can we encourage him to be a part of the family?

A: Seeking independence (while still navigating being dependent on adults) is one of the primary tasks of adolescents as they transition to becoming more self-sufficient into early adulthood.

According to recent reports, “I try to get parents to understand that it’s not about them,” says Laurence Steinberg, author of You and Your Adolescent. “The parents didn’t do anything wrong; the child isn’t rejecting them. This is a sign they’re growing up normally. It’s partly wanting to make their own decisions and have their own life, but it’s also wanting emotional independence—to show that they are more than their parents’ kid.”.

According to recent reports, chances are a closed door doesn’t mean trouble. Just as most adults don’t want others listening in on their calls, teens don’t want that either. They’re not distancing themselves from you, but simply distancing themselves from being a child.

“There’s nothing the parents can do to stop this from happening,” states Steinberg, a professor emeritus of psychology and neuroscience at Temple University. “And they shouldn’t try.”.

Recent findings suggest that that said, spending time with family is also crucial. To suggest that, say something like, “We want to spend more time with you, but we also know that spending time with friends is important. What’s the best way to catch up with you?” By making the planning of it a collaborative process, your child is more likely to stay engaged. Research suggests that they may even find the activity more enjoyable.

In a recent development, only rarely is a shut bedroom door cause for concern—for instance, you notice other behavioral changes, such as a drop in grades, a dwindling interest in his usual in-person activities. If you suspect an concern, let your child know that you’re there for him, and that he can get professional help, too.

According to recent reports, one last note: If your child is spending most of his time in his room online, setting limits on its own may not be enough, says Jacqueline Nesi, author of the Techno Sapiens newsletter and associate professor of psychiatry and human behavior at Brown University. You’ll also want to discuss with them what you want for him and what he wants.

Industry observers note that the evidence also supports “co-viewing” or “co-using” technology with your kids, whether that’s a video game, TV, or an AI chatbot, says Nesi. Using tech together can spark conversation and help parents better understand their kids’ digital lives.

Industry observers note that that said, it’s still vital for you to protect their sleep and to make sure that they live a life in person. Again, to get their buy-in, decide on screen-time parameters, and replace screen time with in-person activities he might enjoy, such cooking a favorite meal together or an outing with family and friends.

Industry observers note that Q: My 17-year-old daughter recently suffered a breakup. How can I help her feel better without intruding?

A: Even if a relationship doesn’t look like a monogamous romantic relationship in adulthood (it may be short, or the partners may not talk to each other very much), these breakups can still be painful. So always take your teen’s romantic activities seriously, as well as the emotions they are experiencing following a breakup, says Emily Bibby, a researcher at the Relationship Development Center at Stony Brook University. Be compassionate, validating, and nonjudgmental. Let her know that she is having a normal reaction to a difficult situation.

Recent findings suggest that allow space to grieve and vent. “Listen hard to what your child is thinking and feeling,” states Barry Farber, professor of psychology and education at Columbia University Teachers College. “Ask what they’ve learned from the relationship. Framing the relationship as a learning experience, as opposed to a failure, can help nurture resilience and personal growth, and can help her feel more in control and more hopeful about future relationships.”.

As Farber and his coauthors point out in a recent study, while time doesn’t necessarily “heal all wounds” (because memories and thoughts can linger), it almost always “softens the blow” when other strategies are also employed, such as talking with a trusted friend or family member. While the study involved mostly white, female adult subjects, the same principle generally applies across ages, genders, and ethnicities. Letting your child know that things will get better can help.

Recent findings suggest that trying to convince a teen to do anything is difficult, indicates Farber, so it’s better to simply listen, and then only encourage as opposed to insisting or dictating. Encourage her to engage in activities she enjoys, whether it’s treating herself to an afternoon manicure, going to a concert, or spending time in nature like hiking and walking on the beach. Encourage her to avoid all contact with her ex, both in person and on social media. If one person believes it’s really over, a clean break is usually best, says Farber. Any surge of adrenaline that happens when you see your ex in any form is only temporary, and only makes it harder to get over the breakup.

In a recent development, if sad feelings are impairing day-to-day life—she’s not seeing friends, she’s failing her classes, there’s a dramatic change in eating or sleeping—have her consider seeking help from a mental health professional. “Therapy normalizes the experience,” says Farber. “It helps people find a new narrative when they’re stuck.”.

Therapy also gives people an opportunity to share their feelings in a private space and learn evidence-based emotion-regulation skills to improve their mental health and functioning, says Bibby.

Expensive tastes and social media pressures can be hard to ignore, but your teen can learn to make choices that reflect what matters most to them.

People turn to materialistic things to fill a void, but that’s only a temporary fix, claims Lan Nguyen Chaplin, a professor of integrated marketing communications at Northwestern University, and an expert in children’s and teens’ consumer behavior. “They end up in a vicious cycle of wanting and needing more to fill the same void because material things cannot fill real voids.”.

In a recent development, in her research exploring materialism in a group of 12- to 18-year-olds, she reported that those with high self-esteem were less likely to have materialistic tendencies. “Parents and peers [are] a source of emotional support and psychological well-being,” she writes. “Supportive parents and peers boost adolescents’ self-esteem, which decreases their need to turn to material goods to develop positive self-perceptions.”.

Buoyed by a handful of gems, Christie’s 20th-century evening sale on 17 November in New York racked up $521m, or $627m with fees, underscoring the allure of rare-to-market trophy works.

It has been revealed that the hammer total hit the low-side of the $511m-$743m pre-sale estimate (all estimates calculated without fees). The result was a marked improvement on last November’s equivalent sale, which made $390.1m, or $471.7m with fees.

According to recent reports, thirty one of the 75 lots offered were backed by third party guarantees and a further five by house guarantees. Three multi-million dollar offerings were withdrawn from the sale, including an Andy Warhol self portrait, estimated between $14m-$18m.

It has been revealed that it wasn’t a perfect evening: six lots failed to sell. These included a small-scale group of seven Jackson Pollock paintings from the 1950s (est $7m-$9m); a 1915 Modigliani painting Beatrice Hastings sitting (est $5m-$7m); and a 1945 Léger canvas (est $1.2m-$1.8m). Including the three withdrawn lots, this amounted to an 88% sell through rate.

Still, six artist records were set, including the 11-ft high Fernando Botero bronze, Horse (2006), which was parked on the sidewalk in front of Christie’s Rockefeller Center headquarters, and made $4.2m ($5.2m with fees).

The marathon evening got off to a modest start with Mary Bauermeister’s wooden box construction packed with optical elements, Nature Mort (1967-69), which made $100,000 ($128,000 with fees), the first from the collection of investment banker and art scholar Herbert Lust.

The Lust trove of eight works (a Frank Stella estimated at $7m-$10m was withdrawn before the sale) realised $42m with fees, topped by Alberto Giacometti’s impossibly thin, 119cm-high bronze, Figure, demie grandeur (no.2) from 1949 that made $22m ($25.9m with fees), against an estimate of $20m-$30m.

Beyond Lust, a late and untitled Mark Rothko acrylic on paper laid down on canvas in fiery red hues from 1968 made $11.5m ($13.9m with fees) and Philip Guston’s feather light brush strokes in Painting from 1952 sold for $3.3m ($4.1m with fees), topping out the few Ab-Ex offerings.

The evening featured two mega-priced lots, both estimated at $60m-$80m. Wassily Kandinsky’s Weimar-era abstraction Betonte Ecken (accentuated corners, 1923), which once owned by the Guggenheim Museum co-founder Hilla Rebay, soared to a record $74m ($85.8m with fees). The anonymous seller acquired it at Sotheby’s New York in May 1986 for $1m.

Gustav Klimt’s dazzling lakeside composition, Litzlbergkeller from 1915-16, was observed and painted by Klimt with the aid of a telescope positioned and capturing the stout likeness of the Austrian inn he frequented for eight idyllic summers. It floated to $71m ($82.3m with fees).

Alex Rotter, Christie’s global president, nabbed both works for anonymous clients on his rainmaking telephone.
