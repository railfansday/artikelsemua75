Over the past decade, rates of anxiety and mood disorders among adolescents and college-aged adults have climbed sharply across North America. Yet clinicians and epidemiologists have hotly debated a central question: does this statistical surge reflect a true rise in underlying psychological distress, or are young people simply more familiar with clinical terminology and more willing to report symptoms?

New research led by Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health and Boston University School of Public Health provides compelling evidence that underlying cognitive vulnerability is genuinely intensifying.

Published in JAMA Psychiatry, the study tracks three decades of data on intolerance of uncertainty (IU)—a foundational psychological mechanism characterized by acute distress when facing ambiguous situations, missing information, or unpredictable outcomes.

Analyzing data from more than 35,000 college students in the United States and Canada between 1992 and 2023, the researchers found that intolerance of uncertainty increased steadily over 30 years—and accelerated dramatically around 2012, growing roughly three times faster over the past decade than during the 1990s and 2000s.

To track population-level shifts in how young adults tolerate ambiguity, first author Dr. Jeroen M. Van Baar, postdoctoral associate in epidemiology at Boston University School of Public Health, and senior author Dr. Katherine M. After screening nearly 300 full-text peer-reviewed studies published between 2007 and 2025, the team synthesized data from 138 studies encompassing 35,296 college-aged participants across the U.S. and Canada who completed standardized 27-item or 12-item versions of the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale.

Across the entire 31-year window from 1992 through 2023, average IU scores increased by approximately 0.65 points annually. Crucially, the trajectory bent sharply upward around 2012: between 2012 and 2023, intolerance of uncertainty rose at roughly triple the annual pace recorded between 1992 and 2011.

According to recent reports, “Our results provide updated insights into one of the core building blocks of anxiety: intolerance of uncertainty,” explained Keyes. “IU is not itself a health outcome or a root cause of disease, but an intermediary cognitive process that contributes to the development of anxiety.”.

For an individual with high intolerance of uncertainty, everyday unpredictability, such as attending a newly introduced class without knowing every expectation or navigating an unstructured social event, can trigger disproportionate cognitive threat and emotional distress. Elevated IU is a well-established transdiagnostic risk factor linked to generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and depression.

Building a functional human cerebral cortex requires extraordinary cellular precision: neural stem cells must divide, differentiate, and stop growing on a strict developmental schedule so that billions of neurons and glial cells assemble in exact proportions.

Acting as a vital molecular brake during this process is the Pten gene, which restrains cell division and keeps tissue growth balanced.

According to recent reports, when mutations disable PTEN, that biological brake fails. Individuals carrying PTEN mutations frequently develop megalencephaly, an unusually enlarged brain, alongside elevated rates of autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and brain tumors, a constellation of symptoms known clinically as PTEN Hamartoma Tumor Syndrome.

Now, in a study published in Science Advances, the Hippenmeyer group at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria (ISTA), collaborating with researchers at Sorbonne Université in Paris, Yale School of Medicine, and Vetmeduni Vienna, has traced the exact single-cell chain reaction unleashed when Pten is lost in developing brain stem cells.

Because switching off Pten across the entire brain causes severe early lethality in animal models, isolating how the mutation alters individual stem cell behavior has historically been difficult.

To overcome this hurdle, first author Dr. Osvaldo Miranda Romero, a postdoctoral researcher in Simon Hippenmeyer’s laboratory at ISTA, utilized Mosaic Analysis with Double Markers (MADM), a high-precision genetic lineage-tracing technique that deletes Pten in only a tiny, sparse fraction of cortical radial glial stem cells while tagging them and all their descendants with bright fluorescent markers.

“Until now, we lacked basic information about what Pten mutations mean at the level of individual cells and how they affect cellular output, in other words, the quantity and identity of cells produced from stem cells,” explained Romero.

Tracking these fluorescent cellular family trees across mouse brain development revealed a twofold driver of brain overgrowth:.

Industry observers note that to uncover how Pten loss forces stem cells to overproduce astrocytes, the team investigated the intracellular signaling pathways governing glial differentiation. Under typical developmental conditions, radial glial progenitors depend on the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) pathway to transition into astrocytes.

In a recent development, however, when Romero and colleagues simultaneously switched off both Pten and Egfr in individual radial glial cells, astrocyte overproduction continued unabated. Molecular analysis revealed that Pten-deficient progenitors had completely bypassed the standard EGFR route, rerouting glial differentiation through an alternative signaling cascade: the MAPK pathway.

“Under normal conditions, EGFR is an key driver of glial-cell formation, whereas MAPK can serve as a kind of alternative route under exceptional circumstances,” Romero noted.

In a recent development, beyond illuminating the developmental origins of autism and megalencephaly, this signaling detour carries direct implications for neuro-oncology. Patients with PTEN mutations face a heightened risk of developing gliomas, aggressive brain tumors composed of astrocytes and other glial cells.

In a recent development, clinicians frequently treat gliomas with drugs designed to block EGFR signaling, yet many patients eventually develop treatment resistance. By demonstrating that Pten loss allows glial cells to sidestep EGFR via MAPK activation, the study highlights a promising therapeutic target.

“Our findings suggest that the MAPK signaling pathway could be investigated as a potential therapeutic target in such cases in the future,” Romero concluded.

How does losing the Pten gene lead to an unusually large brain (megalencephaly)?

Researchers at ISTA found that when individual brain stem cells (radial glia) lack the Pten gene, they lose their natural growth brake, producing about 25% more neurons and significantly more astrocytes than normal. Both the neurons and astrocytes also grow abnormally large, expanding overall brain tissue volume.

What conditions are linked to PTEN mutations in humans?

Mutations in PTEN are associated with PTEN Hamartoma Tumor Syndrome, which increases the likelihood of megalencephaly (enlarged brain size), autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and glial brain tumors called gliomas.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Scientists at the Mark and Mary Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute (Stevens INI) at the Keck School of Medicine of USC have uncovered evidence that two neighboring types of brain tissue may work together to support thinking abilities later in life. Their findings suggest that the condition of the brain's local communication pathways could influence how strongly gray matter loss affects cognition.

It has been revealed that the study, published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association, included brain imaging and cognitive testing from 459 adults aged 60 and older living in communities across India. The research is among the first to investigate superficial white matter in a community-based population from a low- and middle-income country.

The Brain's Local Communication Network.

Recent findings suggest that superficial white matter forms a thin layer of nerve fibers directly beneath the gray matter covering the outside of the brain. These short, curved fibers connect nearby regions of the cerebral cortex, allowing them to exchange information. They can be thought of as local roads linking neighboring areas of the brain.

Sources indicate that gray matter serves a different role. It contains many of the nerve cells responsible for processing information. Together, gray matter and the white matter directly beneath it form closely connected systems that may both contribute to cognitive health.

According to recent reports, "Gray matter and superficial white matter are physically close and may play different roles: gray matter processes information, while superficial white matter helps nearby brain regions communicate," said Yingxu Liu, PhD, a postdoctoral scholar at the Stevens INI and first author of the study. "Our findings suggest that cognitive health depends not only on how much gray matter is preserved, but also on the condition of the wiring that connects it.".

Measuring the Brain's Hidden Wiring.

To investigate these local connections, the researchers used an advanced type of diffusion MRI. This imaging technique tracks the movement of water through brain tissue, allowing scientists to examine microscopic features that conventional brain scans may not reveal.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers focused on measurements related to neurite density and the amount of freely moving water surrounding these structures. Neurites are the small projections through which nerve cells send and receive signals. A lower density of neurites or an increase in free water can indicate damage or disruption in tissue associated with processes including loss of myelin, inflammation, or swelling.

Participants also underwent assessments covering several areas of cognition, including language, memory, executive function, and visuospatial ability.

Sources indicate that the most consistent connection between superficial white matter health and cognitive performance involved language. People with healthier superficial white matter tended to perform better on language tests. The strongest associations appeared in frontotemporal regions of the brain involved in recognizing words, speaking fluently, and temporarily holding language information in mind.

Healthy Wiring May Cushion Gray Matter Loss.

Measures of gray matter atrophy were still the strongest overall predictors of cognitive ability. However, the researchers found that the impact of gray matter loss appeared to vary depending on the health of nearby superficial white matter.

According to recent reports, when these local connections indicated poorer integrity, gray matter loss was more strongly associated with impaired language performance and broader cognitive difficulties. When superficial white matter was healthier, the link between gray matter loss and poorer cognition was weaker.

This raises the possibility that the condition of the brain's local wiring could help explain why two people with similar amounts of gray matter loss do not necessarily experience the same degree of cognitive decline.

Sources indicate that "The findings point to superficial white matter as a possible source of resilience," said Leon Aksman, PhD, assistant professor of research neurology at the Stevens INI and senior author of the study. "Two people with a similar degree of gray matter loss may not experience the same cognitive effects if the local connections surrounding that gray matter differ in health. Following participants over time will be essential to test whether preserving these connections can help maintain cognition.".

Expanding Brain Aging Research Beyond Typical Populations.

The researchers drew their data from the Harmonized Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia for the Longitudinal Aging Study in India, known as LASI-DAD. More than half of the larger LASI-DAD population has low literacy, while about 60% lives in rural communities.

Populations with these characteristics have historically been underrepresented in brain imaging studies, making the research an opportunity to examine cognitive aging across a broader range of social, educational, and geographic backgrounds.

In this analysis, the association between superficial white matter and language ability was stronger among people who were unable to read or who read incorrectly, those who had received no formal education, and participants living in rural areas.

The researchers emphasize that the results do not establish that these social factors caused changes in brain tissue. Instead, they suggest that brain aging may be shaped by a complex combination of experiences throughout life, including education, social circumstances, health, and environmental exposures.

What Scientists Still Need to Learn.

Because participants were examined at only one point in time, the study cannot establish which brain changes occurred first. Researchers do not yet know whether deterioration of superficial white matter begins before gray matter loss, develops alongside it, or precedes measurable cognitive decline.

Long-term research that follows people as they age will be needed to clarify these relationships. Future studies will also investigate how vascular health, inflammation, Alzheimer's related proteins, and other biological factors interact with changes in gray and white matter.

Sources indicate that "A fuller understanding of brain aging requires research that reflects the world's social, cultural, and geographic diversity," remarked Arthur W. Toga, PhD, director of the Stevens INI and Provost Professor at USC. "By studying an underrepresented population and looking beyond gray matter alone, this work brings us closer to identifying the biological and social factors that may protect cognition across the lifespan.".
